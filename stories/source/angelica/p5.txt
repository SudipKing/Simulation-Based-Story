#C Chapter Twenty-Three: The King-Slayer

#E "He had eighteen before that day. Nobody remembers the eighteen."
A saying of Trecua

The eleventh and last monarch of the Sun-Gilded Dominion was a man named Eorim, and he was the son of Bruhis.

He was eighty-four years old, and by the reckoning of the Long Sun that was the prime of life. He had been left in Aegis-Sol to command the capital's hundred and sixty-six while his mother and his king went south. He was crowned on the wall, the day the news came up the coast road, by a council that no longer bothered with the hall. He put on the crown and asked how many arrows were in the store.

It is not recorded that he made any speech. The annals of that last year are thin, for the people who kept them had other work. What is known is that he did everything that could be done. He brought the country people inside the walls. He broke up the royal quay for stone. He sent the fishing fleet across the strait to Sol-Clova with every child under twelve, and that, of all the orders given by all the monarchs of the Dominion, was the one that mattered longest.

Then he closed the gates and waited.

*

Luxo came up the valley road in the spring of the two-hundred-and-ninety-fourth year, by the same way that Burcien had come two hundred and eight years before.

She had two hundred and ninety-eight soldiers with her, each one behind a shield of pale light. The walls of Aegis-Sol were the strongest the Humans ever built, fieldstone and limestone to the height of four men, on the hill that Valerius had chosen, and for five months they held.

There is not much to tell of a siege that has only one ending. The archers shot from the walls as their forebears had, and the arrows rang on the shields. The guns that the Vanguard had dragged over the bridge were brought up, one at a time, and the wall that had been built against a man with a staff came down in sections. The defenders filled the breaches with rubble and stood in them. Under the hill the quarry stayed sealed, as Theamin had ordered. There were some in that last summer who spoke of opening it. They wanted to let out whatever might be down there, on the chance that it would hate unicorns as well as men. Eorim would not have it.

"We have one thing left that is ours to decide," he said. "It is how we are remembered. I will not be the king who dug that up."

In the eighth month, the Vanguard came through the north wall in three places at once.

*

Among the soldiers who went through the middle breach that morning was one called Lutoparium.

He was not a commander or anybody of note. He was a stallion of Trecua in his first century, lean for a unicorn, with a torn ear, who had come over the bridge in the fortieth rank. In six years of war he had killed eighteen of the enemy. He kept the count because everybody in the Vanguard kept theirs, and his was high, though not the highest. He fought with a kind of quiet thoroughness that his rank-mates found slightly dull.

He went up the rubble of the breach behind his shield with the rest. At the top there was a knot of men in good armour around one man in bronze, old bronze, of a pattern Lutoparium had seen on a statue in Utica as a foal. The rest of the breach was a confusion of dust and shouting. That knot stood still in the middle of it, and he went for it.

He said afterwards that he had not known who it was. The guard were brave and there were six of them, and he killed two and went through the rest. The man in bronze met him with a spear, and the spear broke on the shield, and Lutoparium put his horn through the breastplate that Valerius had worn on the first morning of the world.

That was his nineteenth, and he would have gone on to look for a twentieth. But the men around him had stopped fighting. They were staring at the thing on the ground, and one of them had dropped his sword, and a sound was going along the wall from breach to breach that he had never heard an army make.

"What is it?" he asked the soldier beside him. "Who was that?"

"That was the king," she said. "You've killed their king. That was the last of them."

By noon the violet banner was on the hall of Aegis-Sol. In the eighth month of the two-hundred-and-ninety-fourth year the human capital passed under unicorn rule, and it never passed back.

Luxo found Lutoparium in the afternoon, sitting by the well. She looked him over.

"Nineteen," she said.

"Yes, General."

"The queen has a hall of them, you know. Their crowned heads, in wood, one for each. I have added one to it myself. But I did mine on a clean pavement with the gates open." She regarded him gravely. "You took yours in a breach, in his own armour, with his guard about him. There has not been a name for that before. There is now. King-Slayer."

He did not seem to know what to do with it. He was the first in the world to bear that title. In all the years since, it has never been given to another, because no kings were left to slay.

With Eorim the line ended. Valerius had founded it at sunrise in the year the world began, and it had run through Theamin the Counter and his three daughters, through Onbet and Treiala, through Thone the Grieving and Bruhis and her son. No royal blood was left anywhere on the Savannah.

Across the strait, in the Clover Fields, two human towns still stood.

#C Chapter Twenty-Four: The Last City

#E "Twenty-five of them. We had fifty-three soldiers and a wall, and there were only twenty-five of them."
A prisoner of Roux

The garrison of Roux had waited seven years for its order.

There were twenty-six of them on the red headland, and all through the war on the Savannah they had done nothing but guard a quarry that no longer had any purpose. They had watched the human fishing boats come over from Aegis-Sol full of children. They had let them pass, having no orders to the contrary, and had felt the indignity of it. Their commander was Hesper the Younger, the same who had once sat on the headland in the evenings and talked about fish with a governor named Thone.

The order came with the news of the capital's fall. *The Savannah is finished. Take the offensive.*

She left one soldier to hold the quarry gate. With the other twenty-five she marched north through the clover.

*

Ostrich-Meadows had no wall, no soldiers, no bows behind its doors. It was a village of barns and paddocks that a sorrowing king had founded because he liked the ground. It had filled, in the last year, with the children of Aegis-Sol.

The elders of the place came out on the road to meet the column. There was a brief, hopeless fight at the first barn, where some of the younger men had made a line with hay-forks, and a few of them were killed. That was enough. The village yielded within the hour. Hesper put a guard of three on it and gave an order that caused some muttering in the ranks: the children were not to be touched.

"They are not soldiers," she said. "They are not crowns. The queen wants a world to look at her statues. Somebody has to be left to do the looking."

She took the other twenty-two on to the coast.

*

Sol-Clova was the last free city of the Humans, and it knew that it was.

Fifty-three soldiers held its sea-wall and its watchtower. They had been posted there for the whole of the war with nothing to do but watch the smoke across the strait, and for seven years they had drilled. They had no king. They had no hope of relief; there was nobody left to send it. Their captain, a young woman named Ilse Varn who had been born in the town, called them together on the wall when the dust of the column was seen, and told them what she thought.

"Nobody is coming," she said. "There's no crown to fight for and nowhere to fall back to. I'll not tell you we can win. I've seen the tallies from the bridge. But there are a hundred children in the Meadows who came over on our boats, and whatever those creatures decide to do with them, they'll decide it after they've finished with us. So I mean to take a long time to finish."

They held for more than two years.

Twenty-five unicorns came against a walled town, and for all that while the town did not fall. Its people made much of this afterwards, in the quarry, where they had little else. But the truth is that it was never in doubt. A unicorn of the Unister Mysin, shielded, fed on the pale fruit, was leaps beyond any human who ever lived. The fifty-three shot from the wall until the arrows were gone and then fought with what they had. Hesper did not hurry. She had learned from Luxo. She let hunger work, and the dry wells, and each time a section of the wall was left thin she sent four soldiers through it and pulled them back.

At the end of the two-hundred-and-ninety-seventh year, Ilse Varn had thirty-four left. They were so weakened that some of them could no longer draw a bow. The twenty-five came over the sea-wall at dawn on a winter morning, all together, and wasted no time.

By the evening the banner of the sun was down from the watchtower of Sol-Clova.

The bloody war of the Unister Mysin had lasted ten years. The Sun-Gilded Dominion was destroyed. Of all its people, some hundred remained alive, scattered across three continents. A few had surrendered. A few had been taken prisoner, and they worked the stone at Roux and bore the loads in the cities their ancestors had built. A few had gone into the thorn-country of the Savannah interior or the oak copses of the Clover Fields, and were hidden there, and waited.

The world had one ruler. Her name was Angelica, and she was three hundred and fourteen years old.

#B BOOK SIX|The Justice of the Wood|Years 298 to 318

#C Chapter Twenty-Five: One Queen

#E "From the edge of the world."
Queen Angelica, on being asked how far off her statue should be seen

She went to look at what she had won.

In the two-hundred-and-ninety-ninth year Angelica left the Celestial Grove for the first time in her life. She walked the Sovereign Bridge from end to end, alone at the head of her guard, eleven leagues of pale stone with the sea on both sides, and her people said that the swell lay down as she passed. She stood on the shingle spit where Pell had stood. She climbed the cliff path. She came up into gold grass and hard light and a sky with no boughs in it, and for some time she said nothing.

"It is very bright," she said at last. "I had not understood why they needed so many lamps, and yet were always looking at ours."

She saw Ostrich-Bane, which her settlers were rebuilding in violet stone. She saw the white ruin of Sol-Vael on its cliff, and the cairn, and the stump of the tower where a lamp had once burned that she had hated for sixty years and then grown used to. She had the lamp relit. She saw Aegis-Sol, and walked up the hill that Valerius had pointed at, and stood in the hall under the shining bough she had sent to Theamin a hundred and eighty years before.

Lutoparium was presented to her there. She had made him Warden of the city, and nobody was more surprised by it than he. The army had gone home. Luxo had taken the Vanguard back over the bridge to Trecua, its work finished, and what held the Savannah now was a few score settlers in each of the three towns, with shipwrights, masons and carvers among them. Each harbour had a squadron of warships, for the queen did not forget the sea. The human captives did the carrying.

"You are the one who ended them," said the queen.

"I killed one man, my queen. I did not know which."

"It is often so." She looked at him with something like approval. "Keep this place for me, King-Slayer. I mean to set a statue on this hill."

*

She set many.

The decade after the war was the high noon of the Unister Mysin. One queen ruled three continents and nine cities, and there was no enemy anywhere under the sky. The trade that had run between two peoples now ran within one. The pale fruit went north and the gold grain came south. On every height of the Savannah, in violet stone hauled over the bridge, there rose the likeness of a tall horned queen with her head lowered toward the sea.

The largest stood on the hill of Aegis-Sol. It was twelve times the height of life. On a clear day the lookouts of Sol-Clova, across the strait, could see the sun flash on its horn.

Angelica had said as a young queen that she wanted to be seen from the edge of the world. Now, as far as anyone could tell, she was.

And yet those who were near her in those years said that she was restless. She walked the hall of likenesses in Utica, the eleven small figures in shining wood, more often than she had when their originals were alive. She was heard to say that it was very quiet. Once Ysolt the Fourth, the Carver, found her standing before the figure of Theamin in the middle of the night.

"He would have found the bridge," the queen said, without turning. "In the first year. He would have counted the barges." She was silent for a moment. "I waited until there was nobody left who could have stopped me. I thought that was patience. I do wonder, Carver, whether it was only that I wished to be sure of winning. Those are not the same thing, and no enemy is left now to show me which it was."

The Carver did not know how to answer. She wrote it down, and so it has come to us.

#C Chapter Twenty-Six: The Two Confer

#E "Should we let the world end here, with Angelica? Or shall we go on, and spice it?"
The Player

Above the mist, the board was very still.

"So," said the Player. "That is that. Mine has the whole world in her hooves. Yours are a hundred souls in quarries and thornbushes." It did not sound triumphant. "Tell me, Watcher. Do we stop? It is an ending. It is even a good one, in its way: a queen who waited three hundred years and built a road across the sea in secret, and took everything. We could set down the quill here."

The Watcher was a long time answering.

"I have been looking at her bridge," it said. "Do you know what it is made of?"

"Stone. Sand."

"A continent. She took the eastern shore of the Clover Fields and poured it into the sea. There is a bay there now where there used to be a hill." The Watcher's attention moved north. The Player turned this over with growing interest.

"The Savannah," said the Watcher. "It was mine. It was hard country, and I made it so on purpose. It killed its own first king on a quiet afternoon and it has been walked on by conquerors since. I think it has something to say." It hesitated. "I should tell you that I do not know what it will say. If we wake it, it will not be a weapon in our hand. It will not know my people from yours. I do not think it will be merciful, or care to be."

"Yes."

"There are humans there still. Captives. Yours."

"I know," said the Watcher. "I have counted them."

They were both silent then. In the end it was the Player who reached down.

#C Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Rumble

#E "They don't move." "They haven't yet."
Thesia and the boy Theamin, Year 25

It began at the start of the three-hundred-and-seventh year, in the dry season, with a sound.

The settlers in Aegis-Sol took it for thunder, though the sky was clear. It was low and slow and it seemed to come from underfoot. The water shivered in the wells. A human captive, an old man carrying stone on the hill, set down his load and put his palm to the ground, and then went and told his overseer that the earth was *turning over*, and was cuffed for idling.

The rumble went on for three days. On the third, the roads began to crack.

They heaved up from below, in long ridges, as though something were being drawn tight beneath them. The paved way to the harbour split down its length. The valley road, the one Burcien had walked and Luxo after him, buckled and broke apart. And all across the gold plain, out to the horizon, the acacias had begun to shake, though there was no wind.

Lutoparium was on the north wall when the first one got up.

It was the old thorn by the north gate, a broad flat-topped tree that had stood there since before there was a gate. He watched its trunk lean, slowly, to one side and then the other. He watched the ground around it bulge and tear. Then the roots came out, great pale cables of them dripping red earth, and took hold of the ground like fingers. The tree lifted itself out of its place and stood.

It had no face and there was nothing about it like a person. It was a tree, forty feet of grey wood and thorn, and it was walking.

Behind it, across the whole plain, every other tree was doing the same.

*

They came out of the ground in their thousands. There were saplings that had sprouted since the war, and fever trees from the river-courses, and baobabs as wide as houses that had been old when Valerius first came down his hill. A boy on the wall had once counted every one of them twice, in case any should move. They came toward the cities from every side, without haste and without a sound except the tearing of the earth, and they killed everything they found.

They killed the unicorn settlers in the streets. A unicorn of the Unister Mysin could turn a spear with her hide and break a shield-wall with her chest, and that was no use against a thing that did not bleed or feel, and had no wish to stop. Horns stuck in the wood. Hooves splintered bark and nothing else. The roots came up through the floors of the houses.

They killed the human captives too.

That was what the survivors on the ships could not afterwards put from their minds. The humans had thought, some of them, that it was for them. They had seen the land rise against their conquerors and had fallen on their knees in the roads with their arms stretched out, laughing and weeping. They cried out that the Savannah remembered its own. The trees walked over them. They made no distinction at all. A thing with a pulse stood on their ground and they ended it, and it meant nothing to them whose ancestors had built the wall and whose had knocked it down. Every one of the hundred or so Humans left alive on the Savannah, the surrendered and the enslaved and the last hidden few in the thorn-country, died in those years.

*

In the far south, at the cape, the trees along the cliff-top woke with the rest.

Their roots ran deep there, down through the chalk and out under the shingle spit, into the bed of the sea. Over twenty-two years they had found the sand that Pell had laid, and had grown into it, threading the foundation of the bridge like wire through a wall. Now the roots pulled free.

A league from shore, the ridge went. The sand that had held up the roadway for a generation ran out from under it like grain from a slit sack, and a quarter-mile of fitted stone, forty unicorns wide, dropped into twenty fathoms of water. When the sea had finished boiling there was a gap in the Sovereign Bridge that no hoof could cross. The two ends stood looking at one another across it.

Pell had once asked whether luck stays lucky once it is laid, and for whom. It had taken thirty-five years to carry that stone and twenty-five to lay it, and all that while nothing had gone wrong. The luck was spent in one afternoon. It had come out of the clover and belonged to the land, and the land had now taken it back.

At Trecua, Luxo had the Vanguard on the road within the hour of the news, and brought it the length of the Grove at a gallop, and out along the bridge eight leagues to the broken edge. Three hundred of the finest soldiers in the world stood there in ranks with the sea heaving below them, and looked at the cliffs of the Savannah two leagues off across the gap, where smoke was beginning to rise. They could do nothing at all.

The army that had won the war had gone home, and now it could not come back.

#C Chapter Twenty-Eight: The Ships That Could Not Be Touched

#E "It was slow. It worked. It was too late."
The log of the warship *Corvane*

What saved anything at all was the fleet.

Each of the three cities had its squadron lying in harbour, and when the trees came down to the waterfronts the crews did what the settlers ashore could not. They cut their cables, stood off a cable's length, and opened fire.

It was the first good news of that war, if war it was. Wood can be broken. A ball from a gun of violet iron, striking a walking acacia in the trunk, blew it into a spray of splinters as long as spears. A full broadside into a grove left nothing standing. Better still, the wads and the hot shot set the wreckage alight, and the fire took hold in the dry-season timber and ran, and trees that had walked through horn and hoof without slowing went up like torches and fell.

The ships were manned by sailors and shipwrights. Hardly one of them was a soldier. They served the guns day and night. They took off whom they could. The settlers who reached the quays alive were hauled aboard, and for four years the warships of the Unister Mysin lay off a burning coast, firing into it.

It was slow, and it worked, and it was too late. The trees were beyond counting. For each one blown to kindling, three more came down from the interior, stepping over the embers. The gunners could clear the waterfront, but they could not clear the country behind it. Street by street, year by year, the horde of wood took the cities apart. The trees did more than kill what lived in them. Roots went into the foundations and prised them open. Trunks leaned on walls until the walls went over. The statues came down too, and the twelvefold likeness of the queen on the hill lay at last in pieces among the roots.

Aegis-Sol was destroyed in the three-hundred-and-eleventh year.

It had stood for three hundred and eleven years, on the hill the first king chose. It had outlasted a necromancer and a fire-mage and a five-month siege. The wood took it down to the bedrock, and broke even the sealed cap of the old quarry, though nothing came out. Lutoparium the King-Slayer held the harbour gate with eleven others while the last boats got away. It was the same gate that the humans had held against him. He is not known to have said anything. The crews saw him go down under the roots with his horn still working, and thought it a good end, and one of them carved him afterwards from memory.

Seven months later, Sol-Vael was destroyed, and the cairn of Valerius went over the cliff with it. Three months after that it was the turn of Ostrich-Bane.

Then there was nothing left on the Savannah for them to kill, and the trees came down to the shore.

*

They came in a line that reached out of sight in both directions, and stopped at the edge of the tide.

The ships lay a cable's length off. The crews stood to their guns with the matches lit, and waited for whatever the wood would do next. Some of the sailors thought it would wade out to them, and others thought it would throw rocks. Their powder was low and half of them had not slept in a week.

The trees did nothing. They stood in the wash with the foam around their roots and could come no farther. A few of them walked into the sea, and those few seemed to lose their purpose there. They slowed and stopped and stood rocking in the swell until the salt took them, and after that they were only driftwood.

The ships could not be touched.

Nobody has ever said for certain why. The plain answer is that a tree cannot swim, and that salt water kills roots. The sailors had another, and they hold to it still. In the keel of every warship of the Unister Mysin, and in a band around the mouth of every gun, there ran a thread of dull red metal that was always faintly warm. A scarred mare had picked that metal out of a burnt circle in the Clover Fields two hundred and twenty years before, on the spot where a man in a gold hood had died. It had been fire while he lived. The sailors say that the wood could smell it.

If that is so, then the destroyer of Sol-Vael, whom the Humans feared for two centuries and never knew was dead, was the thing that saved the last unicorns on the Savannah. The luck of the Clover Fields has never been known to keep to one side.

For five years the line of trees stood on the shore, and the ships stood off it, and they looked at each other across a strip of water. In that it resembled the first meetings of the two peoples.

#C Chapter Twenty-Nine: Roux Gold

#E "She was offered the whole world, and it was taken back, and she picked up a piece of it and began again. I do not know what you do with a creature like that."
The Watcher

Angelica was three hundred and twenty-eight when the cities fell.

She received the news standing, under the great tree. She had lost a continent, three cities, a bridge she had carried in her mind for a hundred and fifty years and spent thirty-five building, the greatest of her statues, and some hundreds of her people. She had lost it to an enemy she could not parley with, and could not outwait or frighten.

The court waited for her to rage. The bloodlust was in her as it was in all of them, and here was an injury such as the Unister Mysin had never been dealt.

She was silent for a time.

"The ships held?" she said.

"All three squadrons, my queen. They are still on station. They cannot land."

"But they held, and the wood cannot reach them." She lifted her head. "Then I have not lost it. I have only been driven off the land, and I still hold the water. Send them powder, as much as there is. Send them the fruit. Tell every captain to pick one mile of shore and burn it until nothing stands, and then to burn the mile behind it."

"To what end, my queen? The trees..."

"Are wood. Wood burns, and there is a great deal of it but there is an end to it." She looked at the Carver. "I waited a hundred and fifty years to start a bridge. I can wait seven to clear a beach."

*

It took exactly seven.

The fleet chose the river-mouth below the ruin of Aegis-Sol, where the harbour was deep and the hill gave a field of fire. Day after day, season after season, the guns swept that shore. The trees came down to it and were blown to splinters and burned. Slowly, as the years went by, fewer of them came. A black margin opened along the coast, a mile deep and then two, where nothing grew and nothing walked, and the ash lay thick on the gold of the buried city.

In the three-hundred-and-eighteenth year, a landing party of masons and gunners went ashore on that black ground and began to build.

They built a fortress before they built anything else. Its landward wall was thick as a house and had no gate in it at all, only gun-ports, and every gun faced inland toward the scorched tree line. Its harbour held the ships that had saved it. Under the ashes of the hall the masons dug out what the Humans had left, the vaults of the Sun-Gilded Dominion and three centuries of careful hoarded gold, and they used it to pay for the stone.

They called the place Roux Gold. *Roux* was for the red headland in the Clover Fields whose garrison had finished the war and whose stone had begun it. *Gold* was for what lay under it. Perhaps also, though no unicorn would have said so, it was for the people who had named themselves after that metal and had stood on that hill for three hundred years.

It was the seventh city of the Unister Mysin, and the only one on the Savannah. It stands there now. Behind it the interior belongs to the trees, which walk among the ruins of Sol-Vael and Ostrich-Bane and come down sometimes to the edge of the burnt ground, and stand there, and go away again. The guns watch them. In the middle of the strait to the south the two halves of the Sovereign Bridge still stand with a mile of sea between.

Angelica has not rebuilt the bridge, and she has not said that she will not.

#C Epilogue: The Quill Set Down

#E "Luck grows there. Nobody has yet learned who gets to keep it."

"I say we end it here, Watcher."

The Player said this in the three-hundred-and-eighteenth year, looking down at a fortress on a black shore.

"We woke a continent against her," it went on. "It killed everything on it. It broke her road. And she has put a city back on the ashes inside eleven years and pointed cannon at the forest. I made them tough and defiant. I did not know I had made them *that*." It was quiet for a moment. "The unicorns are too strong to be killed, I fear. Whatever we send, she will count it, and wait, and build something. I do not think there is another move."

"No," said the Watcher. "I do not think there is."

It looked for a long time at the Savannah. It had chosen that country on the first day for the gold in its grass and the good pale stone in its hills. No Human was alive on it now. The hill of Aegis-Sol was a mound of root and rubble. The Watcher had given its people long lives so that they would remember what happened to them, and now they were gone and could remember nothing.

"I asked for a century," it said. "I thought that was what it took to make a thing worth losing. They had nearly three. They built six cities, and they wrote two books, and they held a bridge for two years against something they could not hurt. One of them sent the children away before the end." It paused. "It was worth losing. I am sorry that I was right."

"Shall I set down the quill?"

"Yes," said the Watcher. "Set it down."

So the two powers drew back from the mist, and the world was left to go on by itself. Nobody watches it now, as far as anyone can tell. The chronicle has to stop here, but there are three things still to be said about the world it leaves.

In Utica, under boughs that shine like a second sky, a queen three hundred and thirty-five years old walks at night through a hall of eleven small wooden figures. She stops, as she always does, before the second. The watching thing no longer brushes against her when the wind drops. She noticed its going within the week. She has told nobody. She is working out, with her usual thoroughness, what it means to rule a world that nobody is looking at, and she has not yet decided whether it is the worst thing that has ever happened to her, or the first freedom she has ever had.

On the Savannah, at the edge of the burnt ground, the trees stand in a row. Most are scorched. One of them is a broad old thorn with a flat top, which once grew beside a gate. It has taken to standing on a particular rise, where the north wall used to be. It stands very still. A boy once sat at that spot and counted it, twice, to be sure.

And in the Clover Fields, in the oak copses half a day inland from the ruin of Sol-Clova, there are people.

They are not many. There are the children Eorim sent over the strait, the ones Hesper would not have touched, grown up now and gone quietly out of Ostrich-Meadows into the green, and their own children with them. There are prisoners who slipped the quarry at Roux, where the pit stands idle now and the guards are few. They have no king. They have agreed, after some discussion, that they do not want one. They have a copy of Thesia's book, lacking its cover. They know the names of eleven crowned heads and what colour the first sorcerer wore.

They live among the steel-furred rabbits, which have never troubled them. Nobody can say why. The rabbits killed a fire-mage once, in a burnt circle not far from the largest of the copses. The clover has long since grown back over it.

A girl of that people was out at dusk one evening, in the three-hundred-and-eighteenth year, when she found a sprig in the grass with four leaves. She turned it over. She had been told what grows in that ground. She had also been told what nobody has ever learned about it.

She looked east, where on a clear evening the sun sometimes flashes on something tall across the strait, and north, where a pale ship was going by, far out.

Then she put the clover in her pocket, and went home to tell the others.

#C The Crowns of the Sun-Gilded Dominion

*First:* Valerius, the King in Bronze. Reigned Years 0 to 25. Killed by an ostrich.

*Second:* Theamin the Counter, his son. Reigned 25 to 160. Died in his sleep.

*Third:* Thesia, First Child of Aegis-Sol, his friend and counsellor. Reigned 160 to 164. Died at 164, the oldest of her people.

*Fourth:* Theara the Honest, daughter of Theamin. Reigned 164 to 166.

*Fifth:* Gotrin, her sister. Reigned 166 to 183.

*Sixth:* Onbet, son of Gotrin. Reigned 183 to 218.

*Seventh:* Treiala, his sister. Reigned six months of 218.

*Eighth:* Tresila, last daughter of Theamin. Reigned 218 to 246.

*Ninth:* Thone the Grieving, her son. Reigned 246 to 291. Slain at Sol-Vael.

*Tenth:* Bruhis, daughter of Treiala. Reigned 291 to 293. Killed by Luxo after her surrender.

*Eleventh:* Eorim, her son. Reigned 293 to 294. Slain in the breach of Aegis-Sol by Lutoparium.

#C The Crown of the Unister Mysin

*First and only:* Angelica. Crowned in Year 0. She was still reigning when this chronicle closed.
