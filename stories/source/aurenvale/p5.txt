#B BOOK FIVE|The Gnome War and the Golden Age|Years 148 to 189

#C Chapter Eighteen: Nimnook

#E "A war you cannot win is only a long way of walking home."
(Marching saying of the Eddale garrison, Year 150s)

In the hundred and forty-eighth year the Watcher made its fifteenth declaration of war upon the Dominion of Zathixal, and in the same year it understood, with the slow horror of a careful mind, that it had been making the same mistake every three years since the hundred and third.

The report that taught it was plain. Zathixal had grown. Where the Watcher had once counted three saurian souls behind a ruined temple, it now counted twenty-three, spread across five hamlets (Zathixal, Zathzun, Iskthal, Thexaizzar and Raszzizzar), each in a high fold of the pines where no wagon could climb and no cannon be dragged. Aurenvale's generals had marched into those folds with the best soldiers of three ages. They had burned one hamlet. By the time the smoke rose the war was over, as every one of those wars was over, because a people who can be burned out of one valley simply live in the next, and the Spine does not care whose banner is on the pass.

Not one hamlet kept. Not one border moved.

The Watcher sat above the world in the place that is not a place, and it did what it had not done since the Orphan Years. It laid the whole continent out before it and counted.

There were more realms now than at the beginning. Peoples had a way of splitting; a city grew proud, a priest found a different god, and one realm became two. The Watcher had watched it happen to Luriel and Bolthul and Nobmuck and Dobwhistle, and had thought of it, mostly, as other people's trouble. Now it saw what the splitting meant. Every realm that cracked was a realm that would have to be ended separately. The Maker's terms had not changed. *Make them prosperous and the only realm left standing.* But the number of realms to stand over was not shrinking. It was growing.

In the corner of every sky the red star hung low. A child in Eddale could have covered it with a fingernail in the first year, and could not have covered it with a thumb now. The humans called it the Falling Star, and the older soldiers, who were fond of omens, called it Aurelan's Eye and touched their collars when they said it. The elves called it the Wanderer. In Bolthul it was Krukka's Drumstone, and the shamans said that one day the god would strike it and the whole sky would ring. The Watcher noticed that no people called it anything kind.

It did not look at the star for long. It looked at one town on a long northern peninsula, under a lone mountain knot, in a country of temperate forest.

The Burrow of Nimnook had risen against Dobwhistle in the hundred and thirty-fifth year, worshipping the Deep Ancestors, and had come back to Gorim Gemheart in the hundred and forty-third. It had one town and fifty-four souls. The road to it ran over ground a column could cross in a season.

*One town*, thought the Watcher. *Not five hamlets scattered across a mountain range. A people with a single heart, and a heart can be reached.*

It was the kind of thought the Watcher had been proud of for a hundred and forty-eight years, and it would be wrong.

*

The herald that decade was named Annric, though the soldiers called him Annric the Younger, because the first Annric had been a child in the days of the First Host and this one was his namesake and a good deal taller. He stood in the great hall at Eddale on the first morning of the hundred and fifty-first year and read the decree in the clipped, even voice that heralds learn so the words will not belong to them.

"The Watcher decrees war upon the Burrow of Nimnook. The army will muster at the Birchgate by the thaw."

A generation ago there would have been a murmur. But the hall had listened to fourteen wars on the saurians, and the room that heard this one was quiet in the way of a room that has stopped being surprised.

It was a bad year to be at war in two places. That same season the Warren of Nobstink, a goblin realm that had risen against Nobmuck twelve years before and served Zhulka, Mother of Ash, sent a holy war up the roads against Aurenvale. The goblins burned outlying farms, drove off cattle, and screamed their mother's name at the walls of Brookham until the garrison rolled out and the expedition scattered. At the end of the year, far to the north, the Hold of Torzul fell into ruin, and its hero Dwagrim, after seven victories, fell with it. Aurenvale had once besieged those dwarves for nothing. Nobody in Eddale marked the passing; the news arrived in the week of the cheese tax.

*

What the soldiers found in the country of the gnomes was a land arranged so that they would find nothing in it.

The forest was beautiful and wet. The road was a bed of packed leaf-mould that took a wheel and gave it back. There were farms, small and tidy, with fences built to the height of a gnome's elbow and doors you had to kneel to enter. They were empty. The kettles were still warm in three of them. A column that sets fire to a farm and finds the cat watching from a stump feels something it cannot quite name, and the army burned eleven before it understood that every farm had been left with a lamp lit in the window, as an invitation or a joke.

The Burrow itself lay beneath a long round hill at the foot of the mountain knot. There was a town above ground, built of stone as grey as the hill, and a much larger town below it, and the soldiers who tried to reach the second through the first learned things about gnomish carpentry that they did not enjoy.

The Burrow kept a Book of Grudges, which was not a metaphor. It was a great vellum volume bound in copper, kept in a niche beside Gorim's altar, and every injury that any gnome or any gnome's grandmother had suffered at the hand of any other people was written in it, in small neat hands, with the date and the name. It had been carried out of Dobwhistle in a wheelbarrow, under a blanket, by a woman named Pibble who had personally been wronged by a tax collector. Aurenvale was in it three times already. The keeper of the Book, a calm grey-bearded gnome who was the nearest thing the Burrow had to a general, added a fourth entry on the day the first farm burned.

Fizfen, who was young and quick and the best-known fighter in Nimnook, stood beside him as the page was inked.

"They will have a long list of what we did to them," said Fizfen.

"They have nothing," said the keeper. "That is why it is a war. Wars between people with lists end quickly."

"Tok lo," said Fizfen, which was the Burrow's motto and meant, more or less, *we endure*, and the keeper blotted the page.

The army spent one season in the country of Nimnook. It took one outlying hamlet that held a mill and eleven stubborn gnomes, who retreated into the wheel-pit and could not be dislodged. It lost forty horses to a bog marked on no map and a dozen soldiers to small arrows from places that turned out, afterward, to be hollow trees. In the autumn the Watcher saw that the town below the hill was not going to open, that no winter siege could be afforded, and that nothing in the campaign would ever resolve into a battle in which Aurenvale's guns could be brought to bear.

It decreed peace.

The war cost the Burrow three souls; Nimnook, which had numbered fifty-four, now numbered fifty-one, and a people who write their dead into the margins of a grudge-book are not inclined to forgive. It cost Aurenvale more, in ways the ledgers did not measure.

*

In the hundred and fifty-third year the old general laid down his cloak.

The record gives no battle for Wilold's ending. It gives only that in the autumn, in the hall of Eddale, he unpinned the grey cloak and held it out to the herald and said in a voice meant for the back row, "Somebody young, please. My knees have declared peace with the Watcher and I do not intend to break it."

The hall found somebody young. Her name was Joda, a lieutenant of the Brookham garrison, twenty-six years old, who that season won six fine victories against raiders and beasts and, in one extraordinary night action, a column of Nobstink goblins who found themselves to their alarm on the wrong side of a river. The hall loved her at once. In her first month she was seen at the Tally Wall, the long plaster wall on which the generals of Aurenvale had their names cut in a column, running her thumb over the grooves.

"There's a lot of room under Wilold," she said.

"There is," said Annric. "There always is. They do not tend to need it."

She fell the same year. There was no great beast. A skirmish at a ford, a bolt out of the reeds, and the grey cloak lay in the mud with a very ordinary stain. The old stone-cutter, Tomold, whose great-grandfather had cut the first names, came to the Tally Wall with his mallet and added JODA beneath WILOLD, and beside it the figure 6, because by then the hall had taken to carving the number.

The Watcher watched him work. It had always known that generals died young. It had not understood until that morning that it had built a machine that required them to.

Elda took the cloak in the hundred and fifty-fourth year, and they called her Swift, because she had been the first soldier in Aurenvale to get from Eddale to the Birchgate in a day and a half. She was practical, quiet, a little deaf from the cannon, and fond of a black bread she baked herself. On her first morning in the war-room she asked what the objective was, and nobody could tell her. She cut a piece of her black bread and offered half to the staff officer, and said she had not thought so.

The second war on the gnomes was almost exactly the first. The farms were empty, the lamps lit, the bog where it had been. The army carried marsh-planks this time and burned fifteen farms instead of eleven, and Nimnook sank to forty-eight, and Fizfen's name began to be spoken in the kitchens of Eddale with the dread and affection people give an enemy they have never seen.

In the hundred and fifty-sixth year a lean man of the high meadows named Corwin, whom the garrison called Tough, struck the last blow on the Pale Eye, the basilisk that had stalked the middle lands since the days of the first generals. He came home with stone-dust in his collar and a limp that never healed, and the herald announced it so cheerfully that the whole hall forgot for an afternoon what the army was for.

The year after, the Watcher decreed an alliance with Luriel. It was a small, strange kindness. The elves had been Aurenvale's enemies in the twelfth year and the twenty-eighth, and friends in the forty-ninth and the hundred and ninth, and their envoys came to Eddale tall and quiet in the pale blue their Circle preferred, and were shown the Tally Wall as a courtesy, the way one might show guests the family portraits.

The envoy was a woman named Lirael. She walked the length of the wall in silence, reading the names and the numbers beside them, and looked for a long moment through the open door at the Wanderer, red and patient over the hills of Eddale. "Among us," she said gently, "a general is a woman who lives long enough to be unremarkable."

#C Chapter Nineteen: The Golden Age

#E "The harvest came in so thick that year the mice sat down and wept."
(Folk rhyme of the Eddish country, Year 160s)

The orcs were the first to show what a giant looks like when it comes apart.

The Horde of Bolthul had been the largest realm of the south, a country of clover-meadows and war-drums, and Durgoth was its greatest city, eighty-seven souls strong and never quite at ease beneath the Horde. In the hundred and fifty-eighth year it stopped pretending. A shaman climbed the steps of the Skull-Hall and told the captains that Krukka had been replaced in the night by Ghorak the Skullfather, god of war and conquest, and that anyone who wished to remain loyal to Bolthul could do so from the other side of the river.

Most of the city went with him. Bolthul, which had numbered a hundred and eleven, was left with seven.

The Watcher counted it twice. It had never seen a realm go from a hundred and eleven to seven in a single year without an army marching on it, and it understood, belatedly, that this was the most important thing it had ever seen. That was how the world ended its giants. Not on a battlefield. A giant fell apart.

The same year the Horde of Kragdak sent a war-band against Aurenvale's southern farms. It was beaten at the lemon groves, and the garrison went home smelling of crushed fruit. But the Watcher was not looking at the lemon groves. It was looking at Aurenvale's own streets, where something was wrong.

*

The taxes had risen, and the soldiers who marched north did not come back in the numbers that went. The harvest had been middling, then poor, then middling again. In the inns of Brookham and along the Birchgate road people had begun to say that the Watcher's wars were an expensive way of getting nowhere. In the spring of the hundred and fifty-ninth year someone painted a crowned figure on the side of a granary in Eddale, with a small red star above it. Nobody in Aurenvale had worn a crown in all its history. Everyone knew what the painting meant. It meant Stanfield, and Isbert, and the Rising Crown, a village that had been Aurenvale's own and had declared itself a kingdom and burned.

The painting was scrubbed off in an hour. But the hall had seen it.

"The people are not rebellious," Annric told the Watcher, in the way a servant brings a small coin to a large table. "Not yet. They are tired. The inns are full of men saying that nothing good can come of it."

"Of what?"

"Of the wars, my lord. Of everything."

The Watcher considered it. It could not strike a rival. It could not curse, or convert, or declare a war for another's sake. Wars were won by soldiers, not gods. But its own people were not another realm, and for its own the Covenant permitted, once in a decade, a Sign. It had skipped the Sign of the hundred and fiftieth year, thinking it had no right to a miracle on behalf of a people whose wars it was losing. Now it saw that the people did not need a victory. They needed a harvest.

So in the hundred and sixtieth year it declared again its war upon Nimnook, because it did not yet know what else to do, and in the same breath it gave the Sign. The people called it the Golden Age.

It began in a single night. The farmers of Aurenvale woke in the first week of the planting to a smell none of them could place until a girl in Brookham put it into words: it was the smell of wheat already ripe. The fields stood tall and gold, three weeks early, and when the reapers came out the stalks bent under the sickles as if glad. The granaries filled, and filled again. By the end of that summer there was no one in Eddale who had not eaten white bread, and the bakers of the market square, who had sold black loaves at a loss for ten years, put out trestle tables and gave bread away until their flour ran dry.

The streets went quiet. That was the strangest part. A full belly does not make a saint of anyone, but it removes a great many reasons to shout. The brawls in the market stopped. The informers had nothing to inform on. The man who had painted the crowned figure was found, in the end, to be a decorator's apprentice, and was given a commission to paint a whole wall in the temple of Aurelan instead, and did it so well that the priests wept.

*

Not everyone was singing.

In the Warren of Nobstink the goblin painters finished a very large work called Victory over Aurenvale. It showed, in red and black, a goblin captain on the broken gate of a human city, holding up an enormous skull. Nobstink had gone to war on Aurenvale nine years before and been driven from the walls of Brookham, and had decided, as people do, that if the war had no victory in it the painting would have to supply one. They hung it in the Skull-Hall and held a feast beneath it.

In the orc country, the other side of the same year was being written in red. A warrior of Durgoth named Snagnak, tall and silent, with ancestor-skulls braided into his hair, had won ten great battles for the new Horde, each against a larger force than the last. The shamans said Ghorak walked beside him. In the hundred and sixtieth year, in the season that Aurenvale's wheat stood ripe, Snagnak fell in the tenth victory, and the whole of Durgoth went silent for three days and then began to drum. It was merely the way of the age. Heroes came out of nowhere, fought like fire, and were gone, and the world was filling with the light of short comets.

*

The Golden Age gave the art back to Aurenvale. For a long time the hall had patronised only the arts of war, the Siege of this and the Banner of that. In the hundred and sixty-third year the Watcher decreed something else, and the painters of Eddale, who had become numerous because there was bread enough to raise a class of them, worked three seasons in the square before the temple. What they made was The Radiant Harvest of Aurenvale, and it was nothing like the old murals. No throne, no banner, no army. A field at dusk, full of people bent to the cutting, with a child asleep in a basket at the edge of the frame. The wheat was painted so gold that people walked in from three villages to see it.

In the hundred and sixty-second year a young soldier named Maeric, twenty years old, had struck the last blow on Shrikka, the great spider of the high birch, who had come for Eddale again and again and never yet been killed for good. The hall gave him a whole day of festival. In the hundred and sixty-fifth year a rebel named Ugdush led some of Durgoth's orcs out of their own city and founded the Horde of Bolgor, which would be among the smallest realms in the world. In the hundred and sixty-seventh year a young man named Maery, named for one of the old orphans, struck down Skyrend the thunderbird, and in the same year Corwin, who had ended the Pale Eye, fell in the field after six victories, and the stone-cutter carved his figure by the generals, though he had never worn the cloak.

It was a good decade. Nobody who lived it said otherwise. It was only that on certain evenings, when the harvest suppers were over and the singing had died, some of the old men sitting on their doorsteps looked up.

*

In the hundred and sixty-ninth year the Age changed.

Aurenvale had been Modern since the hundred and twentieth year, a time of wires and engines, and thought it knew what came next. It did not. The Future Age came in the spring, in a month. The glass came first. The masons of Eddale, who had laid stone for a century and a half, found that a certain sand from the river bank, heated a certain way and drawn into sheets, would stand without a frame. In six weeks the first spire was up, a slender thing of translucent green seven storeys tall, with light running through it at dusk like water through a leaf. By autumn there were nine.

The soldiers got their armour. It was not metal. It was layered grey plating over a lattice that clicked when the soldier moved, and it turned a woman of five feet four into a thing that could carry a cannon on one arm. The guns became lights. In the barracks a corporal fired one at a target for the first time and then sat quite still for a quarter of an hour with the weapon across his knees, saying nothing.

Eddale had been six souls once. Then three. Now it had spires. There were children in the market who would not believe the realm had ever been small. It was exactly what the Watcher had been aiming at for a hundred and sixty-nine years. The Maker had said *make them prosperous*, and the Watcher had.

That same spring Elda fell. She had been general fifteen years, longer than any since Wilold, and in all that time she had kept every promise she made to the garrison. She fell on a wet hillside in Nimnook's forest, in the ninth month of the war, struck by something small and unseen, in a moment so ordinary that the soldiers beside her did not at first understand. She had five victories. They found half a loaf of black bread in her satchel and, by garrison custom, shared it among the nearest twelve.

The cloak went to Maery.

*

On the last night of that year the Watcher set out its arithmetic as plainly as if writing for a clerk. The realm was prosperous. The realm was powerful. And it had waged five wars on the gnomes of Nimnook and had not taken a hamlet.

The Maker's terms were not a request for greatness. They were a request for an ending, and the Watcher had spent a hundred and sixty-nine years refusing, with perfect integrity, to do the things endings required. It had refused to curse. It had refused to strike. It had told itself this was a virtue, and in the nights, when the star shone through the high cloud, it could no longer tell whether it was a virtue or a very elegant way of failing.

"You are quiet," said the Maker.

The Watcher did not turn. The Maker's presence had never been a matter of turning; it simply was, as a lamp is when lit. It held something that might have been a cup.

"I was thinking of the year Maery slew Skyrend."

"You are always thinking about heroes."

"They are what I have." The Watcher looked at the star, which was the size now of a plum held at arm's length. "I have been counting, Maker. I do not think I will be the last."

"You do not know that."

"I know the arithmetic. Every year there are more of them, not fewer. Every war I wage ends in the burning of a barn. And the star comes down."

The Maker was silent a while. It was not unkind. It had the look of someone who had seen a good many reach that conclusion and was curious what they did with it.

"What will you do?"

"Keep the Covenant."

"Why?"

The Watcher thought about it honestly, a thing it had learned over the centuries. "Because if I break it now, everything I did to keep it was a game. I would like to have been a Watcher who meant it."

"That," said the Maker, with the faint dry smile of a very large power in a very small room, "is an answer."

It did not say whether it was a good one.

#C Chapter Twenty: The Short Generals

#E "Cut the number small. The stone is cheap and the name is long."
(Instruction of the stone-cutter Tomold the Younger)

Maery lasted a year.

He was twenty-six when he took the cloak, stocky and cheerful, with big hands and a laugh that carried across the barracks yard. He had slain Skyrend on an open hillside above the Birchgate with the sky going white around him and every hair on his body standing up. A thunderbird does not fight like a basilisk or a spider. It fights like weather. He had been struck twice and had not fallen, and the soldiers who found him afterward, grinning and smoking slightly at the collar, said he asked them whether the bird had been a *good* one or a *bad* one, and nobody could tell him.

The first Maery had been a child at the hall's table when there were nine souls in all Eddale. The second wore power armour and commanded a hundred. His first act as general was to open the great hall to the public on feast days. His second was to suggest, in a tone that left no doubt he thought it reasonable, that the army stop marching to Nimnook.

In the hundred and seventieth year the Watcher gave the Sixth Host, and the world watched it come.

The First Host had stood in the empty hall at Eddale, six grown soldiers from nowhere, the year the Watcher swore its Covenant. The Sixth arrived in the Future Age. On the first morning of the year a company of grown soldiers in plated grey armour stood in a ring in the plaza before the green spires, where the paving was still cold. They were grave and young and did not speak. They had faces nobody knew and memories nobody could have given them. One, a woman with a scar through her eyebrow, looked at the sky, found the red star, and kept looking, as though she had been told about it.

"Look at the stance," said the quartermaster, in the careful voice of a man trying to be professional. "They have the whole drill."

Nobody was listening. The Host had come into a realm that had lost its general four days before. Maery had stepped forward at the edge of Nimnook's forest to look at a ford, and a bolt had come out of the reeds. There was no beast, no last stand, only a soldier who wanted to see for himself. For weeks afterward a sergeant would stop in the yard and look round, as if someone had called his name. They cut MAERY into the Tally Wall with a number, and beside it the stone-cutter, at somebody's request, carved a very small bird of lightning.

*

The cloak went to Maeric.

He was twenty-nine, the soldier who had struck down Shrikka, a sober, watchful man who read military history in the evenings and suffered from a stomach that did not like Future Age rations. He took the cloak in the hundred and seventy-first year with the look of someone handed an extremely large bill.

"I have read the list," he said to the herald, nodding at the Tally Wall. "Do you know how long the average general of Aurenvale has lasted since the hundred and fiftieth year?"

"I have not counted."

"I have. Fourteen months. One of them lasted fifteen years, but she is a statistical error."

"She was Elda," said the herald gently.

"I know who she was," said Maeric. "I loved her bread."

That year the dwarf city of Thorgar rose against Dwaldun, taking the Deep Ancestors for its gods and a fierce young hammerman named Fungrim for its captain, and Maeric, studying the map, said only that a world likes to divide.

In the same season a soldier named Isora, called Tough, struck the last blow on Old Bramble, a troll of the hawthorn country as broad as a house and as slow as a hill, which had broken fences and flattened barns for as long as there had been fences. She was thirty-one, square-built and plain-spoken, a woman who regarded her own wounds with mild interest. The garrison watched her go in, in the grey armour, with a long glass-edged spear, and it was over in one bellowing minute, and the troll came down with a crash that cracked a granary wall three miles away.

"That," said Isora afterward, to nobody, "is a very large amount of troll."

She was made general in the hundred and seventy-second year, because Maeric fell that spring.

The record treats his death strangely. It gives no battle, only: *Year 172. The general Maeric has fallen.* The war on Nimnook was in its third season, and he had gone forward to a ridge to see for himself, as he had told the herald he would not do. He had left the field kitchen that morning without breakfast, in a soldier who read history a kind of flaw. He was thirty. Isora found him herself, and wrote in her report in the clipped hand she used for everything that the general had been struck from the front and had not suffered. It was what a good officer writes. It was probably not true. She added a line the herald decided not to read aloud, though he copied it into the record: *I would like it noted that the Burrow has very good shots.*

On her first morning in the cloak she stood a long time before the Tally Wall. The names ran down the plaster in a long ragged column: Annwin, Gereth, Edmund, Marric, Annwin, Edwin, Corry, Henwin, Annbert, Wilold, Joda, Elda, Maery, Maeric.

She went on four years, which for a general of those days was an eternity. She ended the war of the hundred and seventy-second year with a peace so plain it could have been written on a slate. In the hundred and seventy-fifth the army burned seven farms and one mill, and the gnomes lost three souls and the army forty, and in the autumn a great war-chief of the diminished Horde of Bolthul named Rukmash, who had won twelve victories against every neighbour, fell at last, and the news came to Eddale with a bitter flavour.

*

The first time anyone in the barracks at Eddale heard the name of Annmund as a general, in the hundred and seventy-sixth year, there was a flicker of recognition and then, oddly, no particular reaction. The name was an old one in the garrison.

The first Annmund had struck the last blow on Shrikka in the hundred and twenty-second year, a very young soldier with big hands and an untidy salute, and had lived long enough to hold a grandson and give the boy his name. This was the grandson, thirty-six years old, born in the hundred and fortieth year under the Fifth Host and raised on his grandfather's one story. He had been a lieutenant, then a captain, then the man the barracks consulted when a gun jammed. He was tall and spare and already going grey, with a face the weather had been at and a quiet that made people lower their voices. His wife had died in the hundred and seventy-first year. He did not speak of it.

Isora fell in the autumn of the hundred and seventy-sixth year. The record gives it one line, the same line it gave the others. She had held the cloak four years and had one great victory, the troll. She had stood before the Tally Wall and read the names and had not once let it show.

The cloak went to the grey captain. The herald, who had presided at many such ceremonies, found his voice catching on the last sentence and had to begin again. Annmund took the grey cloak and turned it once in his hands, and looked not at the hall but through the open door at the red star.

"It is larger than when I was a boy," he said.

"Yes, my lord."

"I do not think," said Annmund, "that it is going to be kind."

The herald did not know what to answer. The hall, which had been listening, drew in its breath together and let it out slowly. It was the first time anyone had said the thing aloud.

#C Chapter Twenty-One: Fifteen Victories

#E "He did not win the war. He won the next day, and the next, and he did not lie about which one he was in."
(Epitaph proposed for Annmund, never cut)

There are generals who are loved and generals who are obeyed. Annmund was neither at first. He was trusted, which is rarer and slower.

He took the garrison in hand as a craftsman takes up a tool he has used since boyhood. He did not shout. He walked the barracks at dawn with his hands behind his back, and when he saw a thing wrong he said so once, in a voice you had to lean to hear, and the next morning it was right. The soldiers began to straighten when he came into a room. The cooks began to save him the heel of the loaf. In all the flat bright bustle of the Future Age he was the one person in Eddale who always seemed to be standing where he had stood the year before. He wore the power armour as other men wear a coat. In his first month he took it apart on the barracks floor, studied the joints, put it together again, and pronounced it "adequate."

In the hundred and seventy-seventh year Old Bramble came back.

It was a different troll, or the same troll, or only the name; the old soldiers argued, and the hall had long ago concluded that a monster's name was a crown passed from head to head. It came down out of the hawthorn country in the cold of the year with a roar that made dogs howl three villages away, and the farmers of the Birchgate road came into Eddale in the dark with their children on their backs. Annmund met it on the Birchgate at first light with forty soldiers behind him and nothing at all in his face. The record does not describe the fight. It records that the general walked three paces ahead of his line, and that when the thing fell it fell at his feet.

The hall gave him a festival. He attended the first hour, accepted a loaf of white bread from a child, and went back to the barracks to see about a jammed gun.

*

The Watcher, in those years, was no longer sure what it was doing.

It held the decree each third year as it always had. In the hundred and seventy-eighth it decreed war on Nimnook once more, and the army went north under Annmund and burned its farms and left the gnomes' lamps burning. In the autumn of that year the Hold of Kazgar rose from Dwaldun, taking Gorim for its god. Where there had been one dwarf realm of the west there were now three.

In the hundred and seventy-ninth year Annmund ended Mossjaw, a lichened, one-eyed troll with a jaw like a quarry wall, which had been seen in the south for three generations and was said to eat whole barns. He went into the southern woods because it was reported asleep, and found it not asleep. What followed took the best part of a day. When he came back to the edge of the wood in the evening, bleeding from the scalp, moving with the deliberateness of a man who has decided that pain is for later, the troll was no longer a threat to any barn in Aurenvale.

Nobody was counting aloud, but it was his eleventh victory.

In the same autumn, in the country of the gnomes, a thing happened that nobody in Eddale noted. Fizfen of Nimnook, who had stood beside the keeper on the day the first farm burned, fell. He was no longer young. The record says only that he fell in the season of the war, and whether by the hand of Annmund's soldiers or a rockfall in the dark it does not say. The keeper entered it in the Book of Grudges either way. Aurenvale now had five entries.

"He was a good one," the keeper said to a young gnome who had come to ask what to write. "Write it small. We will want the room."

In the hundred and eightieth year the Watcher did not give a Sign.

It had skipped the Signs of the hundred and tenth year and the hundred and fiftieth, and it was tempted now. The star was a coin at arm's length, so bright at dusk that farmers on the Birchgate road had begun to drive their cattle in early. The gnomes called it the Gem Above and said it was Gorim's own jewel and that there would be a reckoning. The dwarves called it the Unmined Stone and said with great simplicity that they did not like it. In the saurian hamlets the elders named it the Judge, and did not elaborate.

The Watcher could have given its people a seventh gift in a year when they would have welcomed it. It looked instead at what the last Sign had done, at the green spires and the grey armour and the people who had forgotten the Orphan Years, and found to its surprise that it did not trust the gift. A Sign was what you gave when you could not do the work yourself. A decade ago it would have said that this was proof of love.

"Not this year," it told the herald, who had asked in his dry way.

"The people will wonder."

"Let them wonder. They have a general."

That was true. A second Roric, namesake of the one who had slain Shrikka seventy years before, struck down Webmother, the great spider, in the hundred and eightieth year with Annmund standing by, and a soldier named Rory struck her again the year after, when the creature rose, as such creatures did, in the same place under the same name. Annmund, forty that year and carrying fourteen victories by his name, said that she would not be back in his time, which was not long.

*

In the hundred and eighty-second year the dwarf Bofok of Kazgar struck the last blow on the Weaver, the first spider that had ever broken an army at Eddale, risen again in the dwarf country, and fell the same year; Annmund heard it in the barracks and said only that he was glad it was a younger man who had ended it. In the hundred and eighty-fourth year he climbed the tallest of the green spires at dusk, with the herald and the scarred captain of the Sixth Host, and stood on the open platform with the wind in his hair and the lights of Eddale spread below. The Birchgate road shone faintly. To the north, where the forests began, a thin line of mist lay on the hills.

And over it all, the star.

It was a thumbnail now at arm's length, so red it seemed to bleed. It sat low in the north-east where it had always sat, and did not twinkle. It looked down with the steady mild attention of something that has been told exactly when to arrive.

"When I was a boy," said Annmund, "we used to try to cover it with a coin."

"And?" said the captain.

"It always fit." He smiled a small creased smile. "I wonder whether it still would."

That same year Aurenvale made its alliance with Luriel for the third time in the century, and the delegation included Lirael, who looked at the weathered general with the clear impartial attention elves have for the dying, and told him gently that carefulness would not be enough. "I know," he said. "Nothing is."

That year a farmer-soldier named Marly went into the stony country in the south and killed Stonegaze the basilisk. It was a victory like the others, sudden, bloody, over in an afternoon, followed by a hall full of cheering.

*

In the hundred and eighty-seventh year the Watcher decreed one more war on the Burrow of Nimnook, and did so with a perfect understanding of what it was doing.

It would end as the others had, with a season of burning and a peace in the following year, and nothing gained. The keeper of the Book was very old now, and the Burrow had grown from forty-eight souls to sixty-six, a larger number than at the beginning. The gnomes had not been broken. They had been shaped, as water shapes stone, by being struck over and over in the same place. Aurenvale had become for them a kind of weather.

The Watcher knew it. It knew that every decree it had given was a small bad thing done in good faith, and that ten of them did not add up to a good thing. But the Covenant forbade it the blow that would end a world, and the star did not wait, and all that was left was an old routine: the herald, the decree, the grey ranks marching over the same hills. It was by now a ritual, a way of saying *I am still here*.

The general went north at the head of the army for the last time. He had stopped asking the herald for news.

*

He fell in the hundred and eighty-ninth year, in the last season of the war.

There is no monster to describe. His fifteenth victory was won on a cold morning in the forest north of the Birchgate, in an action the report does not name: a hill-farm, a short sharp fight, a gnomish flag taken. It was a victory in the way all of them had been, in that nobody lost the war and nobody gained it.

What the record gives is this. In the evening, while the army made camp in the trees, the general walked a little way up the slope to look at the sky, as he had every evening for thirteen years. He stood looking north-east at the star, which that night for the first time cast a faint reddish shadow on the snow. He spoke aloud, quietly, to no one, or to the Watcher, or perhaps to a girl named Anneth who had been a child in the hall in the thirtieth year and had been dust for a century. And something small and unseen came out of the dark of the trees, and the general sat down in the snow as if to rest, and did not get up.

There were fifteen victories beside his name, the greatest number any general of Aurenvale had held, and the stone-cutter's apprentice, of the family of Tomold, chiselled the figure with great care and stood back from the wall.

"There is room," he said, out of habit.

"There is always room," said the herald, who was himself old now. "That is the trouble."

They buried him on the hill above Eddale in the plain grey of the general's cloak, and the whole realm turned out. The garrison stood in grey armour, row on row. The scarred captain held the folded cloak. The elves sent a wreath of pale blue flowers. Annric the Younger read the names of the victories into the cold air, one by one, in the voice that does not belong to anyone, and when he reached the last, which was only a hill-farm, he found that the voice did belong to him after all, and could not go on.

That night nobody sang. In the bakeries the white loaves went unsold. A man stood in the street with a child on his shoulders, and the child pointed at the star and asked what it was.

"A star," said the man.

"Is it getting closer?"

"No," said the man, and put a hand over the child's eyes, which was an odd thing to do. "Go to sleep."

*

The Watcher watched the funeral from the place that is not a place and did not look away.

It had given the Covenant a hundred and fourteen years. It had given Aurenvale six Hosts, a Golden Age, the glass spires, and an unbroken line of short brave generals. It had given ten wars to a town of sixty-six, and Nimnook was larger now than when the first farm burned.

"He was a good one," said the Maker.

It had come as quietly as always, and stood a little behind the Watcher, listening to the last of the mourning from the hill. It no longer held a cup. It looked at the star, low in the north-east, large and steady and the colour of an old wound.

"Fifteen," said the Watcher.

"Fifteen. Is it enough?"

"No."

"What would be enough?"

The Watcher was a long time answering. It was thinking of the generals, one after another, of the cloaks and the numbers carved in plaster, of the apprentice who had said *there is room*. It was thinking of Aurenvale's three souls in the fortieth year and the glass spires, and how in a hundred and eighty-nine years it had turned a handful of frightened villagers into one of the greatest realms in a world of many, and had not been able to turn one of the greatest into the last.

"I do not know. I think I believed, in the beginning, that if I did everything carefully enough, kept every promise and wasted nothing and bound my own hands, the ending would be earned. That a careful power would be spared." It looked at the star. "I see now that carefulness is not a currency."

"No," said the Maker.

"You never said it would be."

"No," said the Maker, with the faint dry amusement the Watcher had long ago decided was not cruelty. "I said what the terms were. You are the one who tied your hands."

"I did not ask you to."

"I know," said the Watcher. It watched the last torch go down the hill. "I would do it again."

The Maker said nothing. It looked for a moment as though it would, and then did not. The two of them stood looking down at the little lit realm with its green spires and quiet streets and grey-armoured garrison going home, and over it the star hung huge and red and unmoved, as if it had all the time in the world, and very little of it left.

In the morning a herald with an unsteady hand would bring forward the name of a new general, a woman called Isan, who would hold the cloak ten years and be the last. But that was ahead. For that night there was only snow on the hill, and lamps lit in a thousand windows, and a general's name freshly cut beside the number fifteen, and beneath it, in the cold clean light of the star, a great deal of room.
