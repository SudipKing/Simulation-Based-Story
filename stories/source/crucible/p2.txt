#B BOOK TWO|Ember and Gourd|Years 146 to 200

#C Chapter Seven: The Stranger in Red

#E "It did not want anything. That was the worst of it. You can bargain with a thing that wants."
(Testimony of a water-carrier of Ezo Zul, who did not survive the year)

He came in the ninth month of the Year 146, at the hour when the shadows in Ezo Zul were shortest and everyone who could be indoors was indoors.

The children at the well saw him first. A tall figure walking up out of the open sand from the south, where there was nothing, where there had never been anything. He wore a robe of deep red, the red of the Dune Scourge's own banner, but finer than any cloth the orcs could weave, and at his brow, binding the hood, ran a circlet of golden thread that caught the sun. No one could see his face. In his right hand he carried a staff of some grey metal, and the orcs of Ezo Zul, who had never seen metal of any kind, did not know what it was. At its head, held in a cage of the same grey, burned a stone the size of a fist, glowing the deep orange of a coal that has been blown on.

The children called out. An old orc came out of the nearest house to see who the visitor was, and lifted his hand in greeting, and the stranger lifted the staff.

What came out of the stone was not like any fire the orcs knew. It did not crackle or spread. It *poured*, like water from a jar, and where it poured, things simply became fire. The old orc. His doorway. The roof behind him. The stranger walked on, up the street, and lifted the staff again.

He did not hurry. That was the thing every survivor remembered, in the short time any of them survived. He walked the way a farmer walks a field at harvest, unhurried and thorough, and whatever stood in his way, he burned. Orcs who ran from him he let run. Orcs who barred a door against him, or stood in the street with a spade, or tried to drag a child out of his path, he burned. It was as though he had come to Ezo Zul to burn its houses, and the people were simply things that sometimes got in front of houses.

King Eto had twenty-two subjects and no soldiers. He had a crown he had taken for himself sixteen years before, and he did what a king does. He gathered every orc who could hold a tool and went out to meet the stranger in the street. That was in the Year 147. Eto lived five months under the Harbinger. On the last day of the fifth month he stood in front of his own hall with a wheat-flail in his hands, and the Harbinger came up the street, and Eto did not get out of the way.

They had a name for the stranger by then. The orcs of Ezo Zul called him the Harbinger, because it was all he ever brought. They said it in whispers, as if he might hear his name and come.

By the eighth month of the Year 147 there were four orcs left in Ezo Zul. One of them, a young herder named Ake, put on Eto's crown, because the Royalty had taught the north that a people without a crowned head was not a people, and Ake would not let Zeteurg die headless. He was king for six days. On the seventh, the Harbinger found the cellar where the four of them were hiding, and Ake went up the steps alone to meet him so that the others might get out the back. They did not get far.

By the first month of the Year 148, the city of Ezo Zul and the kingdom of Zeteurg had ceased to exist. Twenty-five orcs had lived there, counting the children born during the siege, and one stranger had killed every one of them. He had done it under the Cosmic Hold, which bound nations and not knives, and which he had walked straight through as if it were not there.

The Scribe searched the ledger for him and found nothing. He had not been placed. He was not one of the three peoples. He was not a people at all.

"Where did he come from?" asked the Scribe.

The Hand, for once, did not answer.

#C Chapter Eight: The Raiders' Hunt

#E "We marched out forty-four. We came home twenty-seven, and we did not kill it. Write that down too."
(Spoken by a Raider of Khar Zul)

The smoke reached Khar Zul before any messenger did.

It had been rising out of the south for a year, a thin brown smear above the dunes, and in the capital they had told one another it was Eto burning spinewood for charcoal, or a grass fire, or a grudge. But in the winter of the Year 147 the smoke grew thick and black and steady, and the wind brought the smell of it, and Queen Ates climbed the stone ring of Khar Zul and looked south for a long time. She was sixty-four, the third of Ezo's daughters to wear the crown, a short, square, unhurried orc who had spent her reign mending the damage of her siblings' quarrels. She did not like Eto. She did not like any of the breakaway kings. But the smoke over Ezo Zul was the smoke of orc houses, and she knew it.

"The Raiders," she said.

There were forty-four of them, and they had never fought anything. They had drilled for a war they were forbidden to wage, against enemies they had never seen. Now they marched south under the crimson banner, spears on their shoulders, in boiled-leather harness that creaked in the cold, and found at the end of their march a city of ash and a single figure in red walking among the ruins as if inspecting them.

They spent six months killing seventeen of their own trying to bring it down.

Spears did not reach it. The Raiders learned that in the first hour. They learned that fire poured from the ember stone farther than a spear could be thrown, and that it did not care about boiled leather. They learned to come at the Harbinger from several sides at once, from behind ruined walls, by night. They learned that the stranger did not sleep and did not eat and that, if he was wounded, the wounds did not slow him. They drove him out of Ezo Zul at last by the simple method of being too many and too stubborn, and he walked south into the sand with the Raiders behind him, burning them when they came close and walking on.

South, and south, for weeks. Further south than any orc had ever been. Until, in the late autumn of the Year 148, the dunes beneath their feet turned to hard grey clay with old wheat-stubble in it, and the Raiders understood, slowly and with a cold feeling in their stomachs, that they were in somebody's fields.

#C Chapter Nine: One Blow

#E "I saw armour, and I saw a thing in red running away from armour. I did not need to know anything else."
(Von Rrim, as remembered by the Hearthguard of Khazad Krum)

Von Rrim was twenty-four years old that autumn, a soldier of the Hearthguard of Khazad Krum, and he was walking the northern edge of the Cinder Forge because it was his turn to.

It was the dullest duty in the Hearthguard. There was nothing on the northern edge. There was the last line of grain pits, and beyond it the Ochre, and beyond the Ochre, everyone knew, more Ochre all the way to the end of the world. The old dwarves told stories of seeing smoke there sometimes, very far off, but the old dwarves told stories about everything. Von Rrim had volunteered for the northern patrol because he liked to be alone, and because he liked to think, and the northern edge was the one place in the Cinder Forge where nobody would ask him what he was thinking about.

He came over a low ridge with his axe on his shoulder and stopped.

Below him, coming fast across the stubble, was a figure in a long red robe with a burning stone on a staff. Behind it, a long way behind it, came a line of tall, heavy, grey-green people he had never seen before, in harness of hardened leather, carrying spears.

Von Rrim did not know what an orc was. He had never been told there were other peoples in the world. He looked at the line of grey-green strangers, and saw that they wore matching harness and held their spears all the same way, and thought: *soldiers.* He looked at the figure in red, and saw that it was running from soldiers, toward the grain pits of the Cinder Forge, toward Khazad Krum, toward his home. He thought: *then that is the enemy.*

That was the whole of his reasoning. The Hearthguard would later tell it as a marvel of judgement. Von Rrim himself never told it at all.

He went down the ridge at a run. The Harbinger turned, and lifted the staff, and the stone flared, and the dwarf did not slow down. Dwarves are quick on their feet, much quicker than orcs, and he was inside the reach of the staff before the fire had finished leaving it. He felt the heat take the hair off his left arm. He swung the axe once, flat and low, with his whole weight behind it.

And the Harbinger came apart.

Not as a body comes apart. There was no blood. The red robe folded in on itself and fell, and inside it there was nothing but a fine grey dust that blew away across the stubble in the wind from the north. The golden threads of the circlet lay in the dust for a moment, glinting, and then they were dust too. Only the staff remained, lying across the furrow, with the ember stone dimmed to a sullen orange and beating, very faintly, like a heart.

Von Rrim stood over it for a long time. Then he bent and picked it up.

The stone was warm. That was all, at first. Just warm, in the cold of the autumn evening, the way a hearthstone is warm in the morning after a long fire. But as he held it, he thought, and ever afterward he could not swear to this, that he felt something on the other end of it, the way you feel a fish take a line. Something very far away, and very patient, that noticed him.

When he looked up, the orcs had come up to the edge of the field. Twenty-seven of them, spears grounded, breathing hard, staring at the young dwarf with the Harbinger's staff in his hand.

Nobody moved. Nobody on either side had a word of the other's language, and if they had, there would have been nothing to say. The Hold lay over all of them, quiet and heavy, and in the stillness it made, they understood one another perfectly well. The orcs had hunted the thing for six months and lost seventeen. The dwarf had killed it in one stroke, on his own land. It was his.

The leader of the Raiders lifted his spear a little, point to the sky, and lowered it again. Von Rrim nodded. Then he turned and walked south toward Khazad Krum with the staff on his shoulder, and did not look back, and the orcs watched him go until he was a speck on the clay, and then they made certain there was nothing left of the Harbinger but the dust, and turned north for home.

That was how the three peoples first met: across a dead field in the Year 148, over a pile of ash that had once been something none of them understood.

The Hearthguard gave Von Rrim a feast. King Thane Varis, a hundred and fifty-nine years old, looked at the staff for a long while and did not touch it. "Keep it close," he said. "Keep it closer than your axe. Something that does not die does not usually leave its tools lying about by accident."

Von Rrim kept it close. He kept it close for a hundred and fifty years.

#C Chapter Ten: The Queen of the Ruins

#E "Usae of the Royalty, who built on ash, because ash was what the Harbinger left."
(Inscription at Usar, now lost)

Queen Ates died in her sleep in the Year 151, in Khar Zul, with the crimson banner over her bed. She had sent the Raiders south when the smoke came, and brought most of them home again, and she was remembered for it.

The crown went to Perde, who was sixty-eight, and the last living daughter of Ezo herself. The last link to the first mother of the world. The orcs of Khar Zul gathered to see her crowned the way people gather to see a comet, knowing they will not see another.

That same year another woman of the Royalty, a granddaughter of Daza named Usae, walked south to the ruins of Ezo Zul with twelve orcs behind her.

Nothing stood there. The Harbinger had burned it to the footings. But the seep still ran, and the wheat-hollows were still damp under the ash, and Usae was of the Royalty, and believed, as the Royalty always believed, that a crowned head made a people. She cleared the ash and built in the cleared places. She named the new town Usar. Then, because she did not wish to kneel to Perde any more than Eto had wished to kneel to Gazia, she declared herself a queen, and her twelve a nation, and named it Raier.

So the north had three crowns again: Perde in Khar Zul, Zan Tor in Gaz Zul, and Usae in the ashes. The Scribe drew the new borders with a sigh. The Hand said nothing at all. Its attention, by then, was fixed somewhere very far to the south, on a field of pumpkins.

#C Chapter Eleven: The Gourd Rising

#E "We split them and scooped them and threw the hollow shells on the midden. For a hundred and fifty years. It never once occurred to us that they were watching."
(Kerrim, Regent of the Cinder Forge)

The dwarves of Khazad Krum remembered it afterward as the most beautiful autumn of their lives.

The Year 153 had been wet. The clay held its water, and the pits brimmed, and the pumpkin vines under the walls of Khazad Krum ran riot. The gourds that year were enormous, great ribbed orange moons as wide as a dwarf was tall. The farmers went out among them with their sickles and their scoops, as dwarves had every autumn since Thane Varis first planted the vine, and cut them from the stem and split them open and scraped them hollow, and threw the hollow shells on the great midden north of the city. They sang while they did it. It was harvest.

It was the end of the year, and the first snow that ever fell in the far south was falling, when the shells on the midden began to move.

No one has ever explained it. The dwarves who lived told it many ways. That the vines had been fed on the ash of a hundred years of hearth-fires, and something in the ash had woken. That the Harbinger's staff had been brought into the city, and the stone in it had called something up out of the ground. That the pumpkins had simply watched, for a hundred and fifty years, how the dwarves treated growing things, and had learned the lesson, and had decided to return it. In Thalassia, much later, when the elves heard the story, they listened very quietly, and then went to sit near the Heart of Azure in the little hall by the water, and did not say what they thought.

Eighty-two of them rose.

They came up out of the midden and out of the fields, great hollowed shells with their split faces gaping and the cut stems sprouting new vine from every wound, and the vine was their arms and legs, thick as a dwarf's wrist and green-black and strong as cable. Where the scoop had carved them, light came out of them, a cold yellow-orange light like a lantern with no flame. And they came into Khazad Krum and did to the dwarves what the dwarves had done to them.

The Hearthguard fought. There were fifty-three of them, and they were the finest soldiers in the south, and they had never fought anything. Axes split the gourds and the gourds kept coming, held together by the vine inside. Arrows went straight through them. The Hearthguard learned, in that first night, that only fire stopped the vine from growing back, and that the dwarves of Khazad Krum, who had built their whole nation around a hearth, could not bear to set their own city alight.

#C Chapter Twelve: The Death of the Iron Beard

#E "He was the first of the world, and he fell in a pumpkin patch. The Watchers have a cruel sense of humour."
(Saying of Silta)

King Thane Varis Iron Beard died in the Year 154, in the great hall of Khazad Krum, in front of the first hearth.

He was a hundred and sixty-five years old. He had woken in the sand on the first morning of the world with nothing, and dug a hole and lit a fire in it, and everything the dwarves had or were had grown outward from that fire. When the gourds broke into the hall he took down the old digging-spade that hung above the hearth, the very spade that had dug it, which no one had touched in a century, and he stood in the doorway with it. The chronicles of the Cinder Forge say that he split eleven of them. The chronicles of the Cinder Forge were written by dwarves who loved him. But it is certain that it took more than one of them to bring him down, and that when he fell, he fell across the hearth, and the fire went out.

The fighting was so bad that it was four months before the Cinder Forge could crown his son. Esotar had been governor of Silta for sixty-two years. He came up from the western desert with every dwarf of Silta who could lift an axe, and they put the crown on his head in a guardroom with the door barricaded, and he walked out and went straight back to the fighting.

He held for three years. Three years of war in the streets and the cellars and the grain pits, the gourds rising, dying, rising again from the vine-ends left in the earth. The Hearthguard died one by one, all fifty-three of them, until there was no Hearthguard. The dwarves of Khazad Krum died in their hundreds. In the Year 157, the year the Cosmic Hold had been meant to break, King Esotar was killed in a cellar under the west wall by the last great knot of the gourds, and the throne of the Cinder Forge passed, two months later, to a child.

His name was Bers. He was two years old. He had a twin brother named Kor, also two, and the two of them, sitting in a single blanket in the dark of a storeroom, were the whole remaining royal blood of the dwarves.

It took four more months to burn the last of the pumpkins. They burned them in the end, and the fields, and the midden, and the great hall of Khazad Krum with them, because there was no other way. When the smoke cleared there were twenty-eight dwarves left alive in all the Cinder Forge. The second-greatest nation in the world, a hundred and seventy-seven strong only twelve years before, had become a village. Every one of its five remaining soldiers was from Silta.

And all the while, high above, the Reins had stayed tight in the Hand's grip.

"It is time," the Scribe had said in the Year 157, with the ledger open. "One hundred and fifty years. You promised them."

"I promised to release them," said the Hand. "Not to feed the dwarves to the orcs. Look at them. Look at what's left. If I let go now, the Cinder Forge will not last a season." The Hand was silent for a moment. "Hold. Until two hundred. Give the dwarves a chance."

So the Hold was kept forty-three years beyond its time, and the orcs and the elves never knew how near they had come to war, nor that they owed every year of their peace to eighty-two pumpkins.

#C Chapter Thirteen: The Regent and the Soldier

#E "A king must be grown, like anything else. Until then, someone must stand in front of the cradle."
(Kerrim of Silta)

A two-year-old cannot rule a nation, and the dwarves had no Unbroken Chain. What they had was Kerrim.

He was the governor of Silta, Esotar's lieutenant of many years, a lean and sun-dark dwarf with a stoop who had never wanted anything in his life except for Silta's wells to stay sweet and its walls to stay up. When the news of Esotar's death came, he called the twenty-eight survivors together in the ashes of the great hall, and stood in front of the twins in their blanket, and told them how it would be. He would rule. He would rule in the name of King Bers, and only until King Bers was grown enough to rule himself, and on that day he would put the crown into the boy's hands with his own. He made them all swear to hold him to it. They swore.

His first act was to give the five surviving soldiers a name. They were all Silta dwarves, all young, all that remained of a nation's strength, and he called them the Forge, because a forge is where broken things are made whole. His second act was to set them to rebuilding Khazad Krum. Not the hall, which was gone, nor the fields, which were poisoned with vine-root and would be burned and re-burned for years. The walls. The houses. The hearth. He had the first hearth dug out of the ruins and swept clean and lit again, with spinewood carried on dwarves' backs from Silta.

His third act was to send for Von Rrim.

The slayer of the Harbinger was thirty-five that year, and had come through the pumpkin war without a mark on him. That was the strangest thing about it, and it was whispered. He had fought in every street of Khazad Krum. He had been seen in the thick of the gourds, many times, with his axe in one hand and the Harbinger's staff in the other. Some said they had seen fire come from the staff in his hand, and some said they had not. But he had not been hurt, not once, in four years of a war that killed every other soldier in the city. He would not say why. He said very little about anything.

Kerrim made him governor of Krum.

"You do not trust him," Kerrim's wife said to him that night. "You are giving him the capital and you do not trust him."

"I trust him to keep it standing," said Kerrim. "That is not the same thing as trusting him. But it is what we need."

Von Rrim governed Khazad Krum for more than forty years. Under him the ruined city became a working town again: small, scarred, half-empty, rebuilt on the old footings. Its fields were burned clean every autumn and planted with wheat and nothing but wheat. No dwarf in the Cinder Forge ever planted a pumpkin again. And the staff of the Harbinger stood in the corner of the governor's house, with its stone glowing faintly in the dark, like an eye half-closed.

#C Chapter Fourteen: The Children of the North

#E "Our crowns pass to children now. A child does not remember why the last war began. That is a mercy, and it is also a danger."
(Saying of Khar Zul)

In the north, while the south burned, the crowns of the Royalty passed down the generations like buckets down a well.

King Zan Tor of Gaz Zul, the first orc in history to rule who was not of the Royalty, died in the Year 164. His son Az Geze was nine. Gaz Zul had not been much of a kingdom under Zan Tor and was even less of one under a boy, and the Royalty in Khar Zul waited, with folded hands, for the upstart city to crawl back. It did not. Az Geze grew up. And Gaz Zul, which had been a granary town, grew up with him. Its people were mostly farmers, as Zan Tor's family had been, and they took a quiet pride in having a king who had come up out of the wheat like themselves. Az Geze was thirty-seven by the Year 200, and he ruled a hundred and forty-three orcs, and, for the first time, he had soldiers: thirty-nine of them, big, slow, heavy-shouldered farmhands with spears, who called themselves the Brutes, and meant it as a joke, and were not joking.

In Usar, on the ashes of Ezo Zul, Queen Usae died in the Year 165. Her son Etan, twenty-two, took her crown and kept it for thirty-five years, ruling a people that never numbered more than a dozen. Raier was the poorest kingdom in the world, built on the graves of the Harbinger's victims, and its people were the proudest. They had little else.

And in Khar Zul, in the Year 169, Queen Perde died at eighty-four, and the last daughter of Ezo went into the earth. Her son Adens was crowned at twenty-one: the eighth ruler of the Dune Scourge, counting from Gorkha the Burned. Adens did not remember a time before the breakaway kingdoms. He had grown up with three crowns in the north and thought it an insult every day of his life.

He spent thirty years building an army. By the Year 200, the Dune Scourge had three hundred and fifty-one orcs, and a hundred and twenty-six of them were Raiders.

#C Chapter Fifteen: The Releasing of the Reins

#E Year 200: the Hand opened, and the Reins fell.

The Scribe opened the ledger in the Year 200 and read the world aloud, one last time under the Hold.

The Azure Imperium, ruled by its third sovereign, King Tua, aged one hundred and seventy-seven: three hundred and eighty-six elves. Three hundred and sixty-seven in Thalassia, with two hundred and eighteen Rangers. Nineteen in Thirefin, of whom eight were soldiers of the village's own small guard, the Vanguard. Tua had reigned a hundred and fifty-four years. He had grown thin and dry and very still. He still carried the stone spear.

The Cinder Forge, ruled by its third sovereign, King Bers, aged forty-four: ninety-seven dwarves. Eighty in Silta, with twenty soldiers of the Forge. Seventeen in Khazad Krum under Governor Von Rrim, with no soldiers at all. Kerrim had kept his word. He had put the crown into Bers's hands years before, on the day the young king came of age, and stepped back to Silta, and the dwarves still called him the Regent out of love, though he was regent of nothing.

The Dune Scourge, ruled by its eighth sovereign, King Adens, aged forty-eight: three hundred and fifty-one orcs, with a hundred and twenty-six Raiders in Khar Zul.

Nepokuh, ruled by its third sovereign, King Az Geze, aged thirty-seven: a hundred and forty-three orcs, with thirty-nine Brutes.

Raier, ruled by its second sovereign, King Etan: five orcs. No soldiers.

"You are certain?" said the Scribe.

"No," said the Hand. "But I said I would. Whatever they do to each other now is up to them."

And the Hand let go.

In Thalassia, in the little hall by the water, the elves who were sitting with the Heart of Azure felt a weight they had carried all their lives slide off the tops of their heads, and sat up, and looked at each other. In Khar Zul, King Adens woke in the night with a feeling like a held breath let out, and lay listening to his heart, and smiled. In Khazad Krum, Von Rrim sat by the fire in the governor's house and watched the stone on the Harbinger's staff brighten, just a little, as though something on the far end of it had leaned in.

The Long Hold was over. It had lasted a hundred and ninety-three years. The Scribe would chronicle only one more century of the world. It would be enough.
