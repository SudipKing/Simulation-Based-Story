#B BOOK TWO|The Orphan Kingdom|Years 40 to 75

#C Chapter Six: Children of the Hall

#E "The hall was built for six and held three, and it was never once cold, because somebody always kept the fire."
(Said by Edry, herald of Eddale, in her old age)

Above the world, in the place where the two powers kept their accounts, the Watcher counted the people of Aurenvale in the last month of the Year 40 and got the answer three.

It counted again, as one does with a sum that cannot be right. There had been six at the founding, and twelve at the height of the third decade, and the Watcher had thought of them, in its proud and careful way, as a lettered people, patient and chivalrous, a people who wrote their oaths on lintels. Now there were three, and one of them had a cough.

"Three," said the Maker, who had come to look, and who did not say it unkindly. He sounded like a man looking down a well and reporting the depth.

"I know the number."

"I only mean that it is a small number to hold a continent with."

"It is not holding a continent," said the Watcher. "It is holding a hall." And it turned its attention down to the highlands, to the one chimney in all that country from which smoke was rising, and did not say what it was thinking, which was that every decree it had ever given had seemed correct on the day it was given.

*

The three were Edmund, Edry and Anneth.

Edmund was twenty-six, the eldest of the children of the Year 30, long in the arm and short in the temper, a young man who had learned at fourteen that grown people could simply stop, and had decided never to stop himself. He had buried Maeold, whom a winter fever took in the Year 33, and Isric and Edric, who died of a fever from the south, and Maery, who sickened in the Year 38, and little Elry, who went out after a lamb in the thaw of the Year 36 and was found by the river. He had buried Tomry the soldier last, in the hard ground of that same month, the one grown man who had come home from Brookgate with two sleeping children under his cloak, and who had taught a fourteen-year-old to hold a spear so that it was a wall and not a stick. Edmund cut the letters on Tomry's grave himself. They were bad letters, but they were deep.

Edry was twenty-four and had the best singing voice anyone in Eddale had ever heard, a distinction like being the tallest stone in a quarry. She was small, dark, quick to laugh and slow to forgive, and she carried at her belt a brass horn the length of her forearm, green at the seams, which had been the first herald's, and then Marda's to keep.

Gerbert had been herald of Eddale from the first morning of the world. He had stood on the hall steps every third winter and spoken the decree, and after his death Marda had hung the horn on its peg and taken it down for no one, until the Year 30, when she lay dying, very old by the measure of that people, and pressed it into the hands of a girl of fourteen. Tomry had spoken the decrees in the years between, in a soldier's flat voice, and the horn had waited. "It is not hard," Marda had said. "You will not understand it, and then you will say it anyway. That is the whole office."

Anneth was nineteen and said little. She had been nine in the Year 30, and had learned from the long quiet of that year how to be unnoticed, and had never entirely unlearned it. She kept the granary. In the evenings she also took charcoal and a splintered board and drew: faces first, then the hall, then the faces again. She showed no one. Edry found the boards in the spring and said nothing and only moved them somewhere drier.

*

The first decree Edry spoke came with the first snow of that winter, and she did not hear it coming.

She had expected a voice. Marda had never described one, and Tomry had only shrugged. What came was a stillness in the hall at the hour before dawn, when Edry had gone to feed the fire: a settling, as when a bird lands on a roof and you know it without looking. The words were simply *there*, formed whole, as a song is there when you have heard it a hundred times. *Peace with Bolthul.* She stood with a log in her hand until her hand cramped.

A herald speaks to a village, and the village was a girl with a granary key, a young man with a spear, and the herald herself. Edry climbed the steps in the cold and blew Marda's horn, and the note went out over the empty fields and came back off the hillside cracked and thin. She spoke the words into the snow. Nobody answered. A crow went up off the thatch. That was the way of things for years afterward: the horn, the words, the silence, and the herald going in to sit with her hands shaking.

Two weeks later an orc war-band came up from the south and stood in the snow at the foot of the hill. There were thirty of them, big, tattooed, hung with the little yellow skulls of their grandfathers, and at their head a chieftain with a necklace of fingerbones who had been told Eddale was a fat prize held by weaklings. He looked up and saw a hall with its roof half patched and a single thread of smoke, and on the steps a girl in a man's coat holding a horn, and in the doorway a young man with a spear and his back very straight, and behind him another girl, hardly more than a child, with a charcoal-black hand pressed to the doorpost. He counted them. It did not take long.

Orcs have a saying about a drum struck on a grave. It is not a kind saying, but it is a firm one. The chieftain stood a long minute while a younger orc at his shoulder asked in Orcish whether they were going up. Then he lifted his hand, and the war-band turned and went away down the hill, and that was the whole of the peace with Bolthul. Nothing was signed. There was only a gesture in the snow, which Edmund would remember all his life without being able to say why it had seemed the most dangerous moment of his youth.

"Did we win?" said Anneth, very quietly.

"No," said Edmund. "I think we were pitied." And he went in, because he found he was shaking.

*

The wanderers came in the spring, and Anneth saw them first, bringing up the last seed-barley from the abandoned store at Brookgate: a long ragged line of people with handcarts on the south road. She ran harder than she had ever run in her life.

There were twenty-three of them, human, like the three of Eddale, and they had a God. Edry noticed that first, because Eddale had only ever had a faith, and the faith was a thing of oaths and order. The foremost of the strangers, an elderly woman in grey with a face like folded linen, did not greet them. She lifted two fingers to her brow and murmured something. They were the people of the Church of Caspian. They had left a country in the east where nobody would feed them, she said, and walked since the thaw looking for water that was not already somebody's, and had founded a village a day south of the Eddale hills. They called it Northshire, after a place they had left. They had thought someone might be near.

"There are three of us," said Edmund, with the dreadful honesty of a young man too tired to lie.

The woman looked at the hall on the hill, at the horn, at Anneth's stained hands, and laughed, the short dry laugh of someone who has stopped being able to be surprised. "Well," she said. "Then we have both been very brave."

Anneth married the thatcher in the Year 42, a brisk small wedding under the lintel, and Edry spoke the Oathbound words from memory and had to stop twice. The Year 43 saw the first birth, a girl, red-faced, enormous, furious, whom the whole hall carried about for a week like a loaf that might rise. By the Year 45 there were ten souls, and Edmund, passing at night, would listen to ten people breathing, and then go out and stand on the hill, looking up at the faint red star that hung low in the north, which the Eddish called Aurelan's Eye and which no one liked to look at for long.

*

In the Year 43 and again in the Year 46, Anneth painted a siege of Bolthul on the east wall of the great hall, in ochre and grey and an expensive blue that cost her three weeks of wool: a wall of orc timber, and below it forty men in the high-collared coats of the first years, going up ladders. Nobody in Eddale had ever laid siege to Bolthul. The one army of Aurenvale had broken against a spider and then a basilisk and never reached any wall. At the top of her wall, where the defenders should have stood, she painted only a pale band of sky. "They are all dead," Edmund said, standing before it an hour. "You have given them ladders." "They never had any," said Anneth. "I thought they might as well have something to climb."

*

The Year 49 was the year of the temple, and of the question of the village.

The temple to Aurelan, Lord of Order, rose in the eastern garden, of grey stone hauled from a quarry the Northshire men found, and it took four years. It had a tower the height of three tall men, and a bell cast by the Northshire smith with a voice a little flat. When it was first struck, in the autumn, every crow within three miles went up at once. Fourteen people stood in the courtyard and listened, counting the babies, and Edry stood in front with Marda's horn and thought: *I do not know what I am doing, and yet this is a thing being done.*

That same autumn an elf came up from the east.

He was the first of the Luriel folk anyone in Aurenvale had seen in thirty years who was not trying to kill them. He rode a grey horse, wore a silver-grey braid down his back, and bore the faintly pained look of a man introduced to a family whose manners he had been warned about. He addressed Edry in an Eddish so formal and correct that it took her a minute to realise it was her own language. The Realm of Luriel, which had twice made war on Aurenvale, wished it known that it was prepared for alliance. The elves had observed with interest that Aurenvale had survived.

Then came the question of the village. The Watcher, in the third week of autumn, set before her the thought of founding another, and Edry sat the whole night before the fire with her hands between her knees. Everyone knew the story of the Year 28 though no one told it aloud: the decree, the families who went out and did not come back, Brookgate a ring of tumbled stones, Roston grass. She had been twelve. She could not remember the decree. She remembered the quiet.

In the morning she did what no herald of Eddale had done. She stood on the steps with the horn in her hand and did not blow it. She said aloud, to the sky: "If you ask this again, I will speak it. That is the office. But I ask that it not be a week's journey away. I ask that it be a day. I ask that we be able to see the smoke."

Brookham was founded that winter, a mile and a half from the hall wall on a rise by a stream, in sight of the hall steps: nine houses, eleven families, every roof laid by Anneth's thatcher. The ground was good and the stream did not flood. It was the one village the Watcher founded that did not in the end break its heart. The people of Brookham said that was because it stood in sight of the hall, and the people of the hall said it was because a herald had had the nerve to argue.

#C Chapter Seven: Edmund's Spear

#E "A spear is only a stick with an opinion. The opinion is the soldier's."
(Attributed to Edmund, general of Aurenvale)

In the Year 51 Aurenvale had a general again, a man of thirty-seven with a bad shoulder and a gift for being tired in front of soldiers without losing them.

Edmund had not asked for it. The office had been empty since Gereth went out against the elves in the Year 28. But Eddale now had thirty-one souls counting Brookham, and a fighting line of fourteen, nine of whom he had taught, and when the decree came Edry went out on the steps and said *Edmund is made general*, and a roar went up that could be heard at the temple bell. He took it with a short nod, like a man asked to carry something heavy up a hill who wonders how far it is. Then he went to find his spear, Tomry's plain ash-shaft with its iron head, and put a new socket on it, because it gave his hands something to do.

"You do not have to say anything," Edry told him that night. She was thirty-five, with a line between her eyes. She had borne three children and buried none, which in that generation counted as luck beyond calculation. "Marda used to say the ones who speak well die first."

"I have never spoken well."

"No," said Edry. "That is a point in your favour."

The Nobmuck war of the Year 53 was what the old soldiers of the Year 20 would have called a country quarrel. Goblin raiders from the far western savanna, long-limbed and grinning in ochre paint, crossed the Dwaldun border to steal sheep and burn barns, and for two summers Edmund's fourteen marched up and down the western ridge behind a smoke that was always three miles ahead. He caught nine of them once, in a dry streambed, and a goblin boy of twelve with a string of bone beads at his neck sat there regarding him with scholarly interest. Edmund gave him a heel of bread and let him go, and could never say why. Peace came in the Year 55 and changed nothing at all. The Watcher filed it under *raids*, and began to suspect that this was what most of its wars would be.

*

In the Year 56 the dwarves came.

They came from the west on foot, a long string of carts and ironbound packs, carrying their fire banked in a clay brazier on a wheeled cart and a hundred hammers in canvas rolls. Two days' march south-west of Eddale they raised in a month a stone wall so straight that a soldier who laid a cord along the top found it true to a hair. They called their hold Dwalhalm. They worshipped Vurna Emberforge, god of the forge, and their generals, Thrarik and Balok, had arms like tree-trunks and said very little. They brought what Aurenvale could not make for itself, the way of a mailed shirt that turned an arrow and a helm that held, and Edmund walked down in summer with a loaf of bread, and by the end of the afternoon he and a grey-bearded dwarf shared a tray of nails, a dislike of mud and a handshake.

That year the old chroniclers marked in red, for Aurenvale passed into the Medieval Age. Nobody noticed on the day. There was no bell. The mail merely came up from Dwalhalm in carts, the masons began laying stone in courses instead of heaps, and the boys of Brookham played at being Edmund in their fathers' old helms.

*

It lasted a year.

In the Year 57, Luriel, the ally of the grey horse and the formal Eddish, attacked Dwalhalm. The Watcher had not foreseen it and could not have prevented it. The elves said the dwarves had cut trees on a border they did not own. The dwarves said nothing and went on forging. The alliance Edry had accepted in the Year 49 with the cold of a doubt in her stomach fell due like a bill, and in the first week of spring she spoke it on the steps. *We march with Luriel.*

Edmund led sixteen soldiers south-west in the rain, in dwarf mail, with spears of Tomry's pattern, the largest army Aurenvale had fielded since the Year 28. Fighting dwarves with dwarf mail was like fighting a man with his own knife, and nobody spoke. The war had one real day. The dwarves came out of the wall under Thrarik on the long green slope below the hold, and the two lines met, and the thing was over in the time it takes to boil water. Edmund took an axe across the ribs on the left, under the arm, where his mail was thin from the old repairs. He kept fighting. He killed the dwarf who had struck him, and a second, and a third, and only when Balok's bannermen broke off did he sit down in the wet grass, look at his spear, and say with some surprise, "Well. That is a good deal of blood."

He lived three days. They carried him home on a shield. He died in the hall, at the end of the long bench where he had eaten for sixteen years, with Edry holding one hand and Anneth the other, and Edry's eldest, a boy of seven, standing at the foot with his fists clenched. At the last Edmund said something about the lintel. Edry leaned close, and he said it again with effort. What he said was, "It was a good joke."

They buried him beside Tomry. Anneth cut the letters, and they were good letters. His spear they hung above the hearth in the great hall, its socket worn smooth by thirty years of hands, and for the rest of the age every new soldier of Aurenvale laid his palm on the shaft to take the oath.

Marric was made general.

*

Marric was a different sort of man. One of the twenty-three who had come up the hill in the spring of the Year 41, a ploughboy then, he was now a big, soft-spoken, deliberate man of thirty-seven, a believer in the Church of Caspian who had never quite let go of it in sixteen years in the Hall of Aurelan. He took the spear down from the hearth, held it a moment, and hung it carefully back. "I will not carry this. It is his."

Peace with Dwalhalm came in the Year 58. The dwarves, having lost thirty and killed eleven, seemed relieved to stop, and Thrarik sent a small cart up to Eddale with a bundle in oilcloth: a helm of the finest steel anyone in the highlands had seen, sent for the man who had killed his brother, who had been a good fighter. They set it on the bench beside Edmund's spear.

But Marric, who had stood on the slope below Dwalhalm and seen what Edmund's death cost, did something that winter that was either very brave or very foolish and was in the end both. He proposed that the army go and kill Viperscale.

The basilisk had broken the remnant army in the Year 31, and everyone in Eddale knew it as one knows the date of a funeral. It had a lair in the pine-hills to the north-west and a long yellow stare that turned things to stone. "It is in our country," Marric said in the hall. "It killed our own. If we do not take it down, our children will have to, and they will be fewer than we are."

Edry carried the question to the stillness in the hall. The Watcher did not say no. It said nothing, as usual. But the herald felt the stillness at her back, and heard herself say *march*, and went white.

They marched with eighteen men and a wagon of mirrors, the idea of the Dwalhalm smiths, who said the gaze might be turned back on itself. It failed. It was less a battle than a slow mechanical disaster in the pine-shadows: a flash of yellow, men turning to stone in mid-motion, mirrors cracking, and the soft heavy scrape of a body the size of a barn coming down out of a gully. Marric lost eleven. He came home with seven, and the shock in his big calm face was frightening to see.

That was the Year 60, in which the population of Aurenvale, counting Brookham and the hall and the children, stood at eighteen: the most it had been in thirty years. The Watcher regarded the number in the cold light of the early year with pride and dread, because it knew how fast eleven soldiers could vanish.

Nobmuck raided again in the Year 61, and peace was made the same year. The boy of the streambed, grown into a lanky raider in a goat-skin cap, was caught in a Brookham field watching the smoke and raised both hands like a man at market. "Bread," he said, in passable Eddish. "Good bread. I remember." The hall called him Streambed, and he took it for a title of honour.

*

In the Year 62 the thing that had not been possible was done. Viperscale was cut down in the summer, by a dull grinding sort of bravery, and the plan came from the Watcher, who had stood above the highlands through the three years since the failure and calculated. The basilisk could not be fought in the open and could not be tricked with mirrors. But its gaze was a thing of the eyes, and the Watcher had remembered something Edmund once said in the dark of the Year 56.

It gave no Sign. It was not yet bound by any Covenant, and would not have given one. It gave a decree, *march on the beast*, and the herald spoke it, and Marric tied veils of dark cloth dipped in pitch over the eyes of twenty soldiers, as the smiths of Dwalhalm suggested, and walked them up the pine-slope in a line, blind, each holding the belt of the man in front. At the head went one man who had lost his sight to a fever two winters before and could find the beast by hearing it.

Nobody remembered his name. It is in no record. He walked at the head of the line, heard the huge slow scrape of the thing in the gully, and called out, and the spears came down in the dark. When it was over he sat on the pine-needles and said, "I think it is dead. It is quiet now." He was right.

In the Year 65 they ended the Pale Eye, another beast, another gaze, another long grey day in the pines. By then Marric had mastered veils and silence, and it died in the middle of the harvest with five men hurt and nobody lost. When the veiled line came down the hill that evening, the whole of Brookham came out of its doors to meet them. Edry stood on the hall steps, a herald twenty-five years, and she did not blow the horn. She stood weeping and did not know why.

It was the best year of the Orphan Kingdom. The Watcher knew it even then.

#C Chapter Eight: The Kin-War

#E "To be asked to burn your own prayer-house for a promise made to a stranger. That is what an alliance is."
(From the words sung at the hanging of the "Lament for the Fallen of Dwalhalm")

It was Edry, not the Watcher, who first saw what the Year 64 would cost.

The alliance with Nobmuck was spoken on the usual steps, in the usual weather, to the usual small crowd, and Streambed the goblin stood at the front and clapped his long hands. It was a good alliance. It gave Aurenvale a friend on the western flank, where the raids came from. It meant, said Marric, that the burning would stop. It meant a market, a road, and the strange sweet black goblin-roasted nuts that Marric's children had begun to crave.

*

In the Year 66 the Watcher did what it would later count the most cruel kindness of the Orphan Years. It sent missionaries to Northshire.

The decree did not say so. It said only *the Covenant of Aurelan goes south.* Two young temple priests in grey and white walked down the south road in spring with a bell and a book and were met in Northshire by the elderly woman in grey who had walked into the Eddale hills twenty-six years earlier with a handcart. She was seventy-one. She heard the priests out for the better part of a day, and then she said something they did not understand until much later.

"It is a good faith. I have watched it twenty-six years. It is a faith for people who keep their promises, not for people who need to be forgiven. But I am old, and my children are tired, and I would like them to belong to something that has a hall."

In the autumn the people of Northshire put out the lamps at their doors one by one, the bell of Aurelan rang over the village, and the Church of Caspian was gone. It was also, the Watcher reflected through the next cold spring, a thing it had done for a reason it had not told even itself: it had wanted the Northshire people to be its own, so that if the hall ever fell, someone would be left who kept its rites.

"Now they will fight for you," said the Maker.

"They are not mine. They are their own. I have only taken away what made them different."

"That," said the Maker, "is what conversion is."

*

That same year, a dwarvish rider came up from the south-west with a long-handled mace and a red sash and laid before the hall steps, with perfect courtesy, a hammer wrapped in black cloth, which was the dwarvish way of declaring that a god had been insulted. Vurna Emberforge did not care to share the highlands with an Order that built stone temples without a forge in them. It was a holy war.

And in the middle of it came the war that was not supposed to be possible.

*

It was spring of the Year 67 and the rider who came to the hall was no stranger: a young Northshire man in white and grey, an Aurenvale sash tied about his arm, who stood in the courtyard with the look of someone sent to say something he had not been consulted about.

"Northshire has declared a holy war. On Nobmuck."

Edry stared. "Why?"

"The goblins are idolaters. They worship Brogmar Bloodtide. They keep skulls. Our priests say the Lord of Order has spoken, and that the heathen in the west must be brought low. It is the first thing Northshire has done in the name of the Covenant." He swallowed. "We thought you would be pleased."

"Nobmuck is our ally," said Edry. "We swore. Then we are bound to fight Northshire." She had said it aloud and it was still impossible. In the doorway Marric said very softly, "They are our own faith. We took their lamps."

She did not sleep. She sat with her back to the great hearth and Marda's horn across her knees and asked aloud, as one asks a stranger across a room, *what do you want me to say?* The dawn came grey through the east window. The decree was the plainest she had ever received. *Honour the alliance.*

In the Year 67 the Watcher's pride and its own words met in a single morning, and the Maker, watching, said only, "You keep signing the same kind of paper."

"I know."

"Do you mean to stop?"

"I mean to keep my word," said the Watcher. "I do not know what else a word is for."

On the steps in the cold, Edry raised the horn, and it came out flat and cracked over the roofs of the hall and the temple and Brookham, and she said in her level borrowed voice: *Aurenvale marches against Northshire.* Nobody cheered. In the courtyard Marric took off his helm and stood bareheaded in the cold, and one by one the soldiers did the same, until the whole line stood in the stone yard with their heads bare, like men at a graveside.

*

The war with Northshire was fought in the Years 67 and 68, and it was the worst thing Aurenvale did in the whole of the Orphan Age.

The army of Northshire was small and brave: thirty men in white tabards singing the Hymn of Aurelan, which they had learned that year, in different keys. Aurenvale met them on the south road with levelled spears. It was hard for Marric, who had been a Northshire boy himself. There were men in that line who had been ploughboys beside him in the Year 41. Before the first battle he rode up and down and called their names across the field, one by one, until a voice from the other side answered: *Marric, don't.*

He did.

At the third battle, at a ford in the Year 68, a boy in a Northshire tabard dropped his spear in the water and stood with open hands, and a soldier of Aurenvale who was twenty, and had been told all his life that the Northshire folk were cousins, hesitated for a heartbeat. In that heartbeat a sergeant behind him struck the boy down. The soldier sat in the cold water and wept. His name is not in the record.

In the autumn of the Year 67, after a night in which twenty-one wounded were laid on the long benches, Anneth began a painting no decree had asked for. She could not paint the Northshire war. She painted the dwarves: the slope below Dwalhalm in the rain of the Year 57, thirty dead dwarves lying in rows with their helms laid aside, and above them one figure in a grey cloak holding a lamp. She called it *Lament for the Fallen of Dwalhalm*. It was the only work of art in the history of Aurenvale made in honour of an enemy's dead. "They are not our dead," said Marric, before it in the firelight. "No," said Anneth. "That is why someone should."

*

They slew Gloomleg in the Year 69. It was a great black spider that had spun a grey web across the birch-wood below Eddale where the children gathered nuts, and when it took the child of a Brookham miller the village came to the hall in the dark with lanterns and stood before Edry's door.

The army had twelve men. They went out in the autumn with torches and fire-arrows and a long chain, and it was the last great hunt of the age. The last blow was struck by a thin, quiet soldier with a hare-lip, whose name the record did not keep, who climbed into the birch with an axe and cut the web's anchor-cable and brought the whole black weight down into the fire. Gloomleg burned an hour. People said the sound it made was like a kettle.

They lost four: the miller himself, who had gone after the thing alone, two soldiers they could least spare, and a boy of sixteen named for Edmund. And that was the year Edry's eldest, the boy of seven who had stood at the foot of Edmund's bed, came home from the war with the Northshire men. He was nineteen. He had been at the ford. He said nothing then or later, but the night he came home he took the brass horn down from its peg, held it a while, and put it back, and his mother watched him and thought: *I will not ask.*

He was dead by the first snow, of a fever that came up with the army, and they buried him beside Edmund.

*

That winter Edry made a lullaby to quiet an infant who would not stop crying, a tune that went up and paused, as if listening, and came down slowly, with words of a hall in the snow and a fire that was kept. Anneth carved the words round the temple door and called it the *Hymn to Aurelan*. Of everything made in the Orphan Age, it was the only thing that outlived the world.

Peace came in the Years 71 and 72. First Northshire: the two armies met at the ford in the rain and nobody could think of anything to say, and Marric walked across the water alone and shook the hand of the white-tabarded captain, who was weeping.

Then Dwalhalm, in the Year 72, the same spring that dwarf wanderers went up into the far north near Dobwhistle and founded a hold called Torzul, a place no one in the highlands knew yet, which would one day trouble Aurenvale very much. The dwarves of Dwalhalm came to the hall under a flag of truce and stood before the mural of their own dead. Balok the general stood a long while with his arms crossed. Then he said something in Dwarvish, and the interpreter said: "He says it is a good likeness of the slope. He says it is not accurate about the weather." "It was raining," said Anneth. "He says it was drizzling." That was the last joke between the two peoples, for by then most of those who might have laughed at it were dead or dying, and the Orphan Kingdom had begun to run out of grown people.

#C Chapter Nine: The Empty Hall

#E "We did not die of a monster. We died of a long, quiet subtraction. Nobody could say which year was the last one that mattered."
(From the journal of Anneth of Eddale, found in the hall in the Year 75)

The giants of the world woke up in the Year 73, and Aurenvale was not among them.

The Watcher saw it from above in one sweep, as one sees the edge of a storm across a plain. In the east the elves of Luriel passed into the Age of Engines, a thing of smoke and iron and mill-chimneys, and the elves, who had never in their long history hurried, found themselves building railways. In the south-west the forges of Dwalhalm were loud with new hammers. Everywhere there was a thin continuous hum of making, and everywhere the red star, a little larger than last year, burned low and steady in the evening sky. In Aurenvale, still Medieval among the engines, the shepherds had begun to speak of something large and many-legged on the north ridge, and to call it Shrikka.

*

There was a good harvest in the Year 73, and the last art of the Orphan Years: a tall chair of white birch in the great hall that Anneth called *The Radiant Throne*. Nobody was to sit in it. It was, she said, to be left empty for whoever came. She carved it with wheat and fruit and little hammers, and on its back the figure of Aurelan with a hand lifted, and round the base, very small, a row of names. She cut the last name in January of the Year 74. It was Marric's.

He was fifty-four, and had been general seventeen years. He went out on the north road in the first week of the thaw to look into a rumour of something in the pines, and came back with a cough, which he said was nothing. It went to his chest. He died in the long room above the hall with a grey-bearded dwarf from Dwalhalm sitting at the foot of the bed, who had walked all that way and did not say a word in three days.

*

The war with Northshire had taken some, and the war with Dwalhalm more, and Gloomleg four, and the fevers the rest. The people of Aurenvale found themselves in the autumn of the Year 74 reduced, by something that was not a battle and had no day, to a handful. The children were many. The grown were few. And in the high cold afternoons the Watcher watched a woman with a white streak in her hair walk out on the hall steps with a brass horn in her hand and a small boy on her hip, and look at the sky.

It had begun to think about the unthinkable.

*

"I could do it," said the Watcher.

It was the first week of the Year 75, a still bright winter night with a thin moon, and the powers were alone above the world. The Maker sat on a stool he had conjured for the occasion. The Watcher stood with its hands behind its back and did not look at him. It looked at the hall.

"Could do what?"

"Put my hand down. I could take every child in that valley and set them behind a wall of ice. I could bring every beast of the Spine down on Zathixal in a night. I could burn Bolthul's clover, or call the dead of Dwalhalm up out of their slope. I have not done any of it, because I always thought it beneath me. It was a thing a lesser power might do. It was what a god would be, if a god were what people say."

"Yes," said the Maker. "And should you?"

"If I do, there is no game. There is a god and its puppets. You said, *fight wisely*. You said, *make them prosperous*. You did not say, *keep them safe at any price*. If I cut every thread then all they ever did was a thing I let them do, and what they built was nothing and what they were was a play. And if I do not--"

"Hm," said the Maker. He considered, not at all alarmed. He appeared deeply interested, like a man who has waited long to see whether a thing would be done and does not much mind which way. "So you will do something in between."

"I am going to bind myself," said the Watcher, and turned and looked him full in the face, which it had not done in years. "I will say what I will do and what I will not. I will rule by the word, one decree each three years, as before. I will not raise a hand against any other people. I will not curse. I will not make anyone believe what they do not. I will not declare a war for another. But I will give my own people a thing. Rarely. Once in a decade at most. A Sign, to them and no one else, never against anyone. And whatever else happens, wars will be won by their soldiers, not by me."

"A Covenant."

"Yes. With myself, and with you, if you will take it. I would like it witnessed."

The Maker stood and looked at it for a long moment with his head to one side, and for the first time there was something in his face other than amusement. It was not quite respect. It was what a man feels when he sees a child do something right and foolish at the same moment and cannot tell which it is.

"It is a very handsome promise," he said. "Is it honour, or is it pride?"

"I do not know. I would like to say honour. But I cannot tell the difference from inside."

"That," said the Maker, "is the most honest thing you have said in seventy-five years. Swear it, then. I will witness. And at the end, when the Stone comes, or does not, you will tell me which it was."

It was sworn on the third night of the Year 75, in no place, to no sound. The Watcher said the words aloud though there was no air, and the Maker listened with his chin on his hand. There were three, as there had once been three people in a hall. *Only by decree. One Sign in a decade, to my own and no other. Wars are won by soldiers, not gods.* They were not long. They did not seem, once said, to change the world in the slightest.

And yet, in the place where the accounts were kept, a line was drawn.

*

In the great hall at Eddale, at that same hour, the fire had burned down.

The six children of Aurenvale lay asleep on the long benches in the old grey cloaks. There was Jomund, ten, the eldest and the loudest, with the hands of a Brookham soldier who had fallen at the Northshire ford when the boy was three. There was Annric, nine, thin and quiet with a stubborn jaw. There was Robert, seven, who talked in his sleep. There was a small dark girl named Elry, for the one who went after the lamb, and a taller pale one named Elora, and a round sunny child called Tombert, who had no mother and slept with his thumb in his mouth. They had gathered through the long autumn out of houses emptied one by one, as birds gather in a barn.

Edry was not asleep. She sat in the high chair at the head of the table with the horn in her lap, and she was dying, though she was only fifty-nine. Anneth, who had gone before her in the first snow, quietly in her sleep, had left one sheet of paper on the table by the empty throne, the last page of her journal. *We did not die of a monster. We died of a long, quiet subtraction.*

"It is not fair," Edry said to the fire, because there was no one left to hear it.

The stillness at the back of the hall did not move. But the old herald looking up thought for a moment she heard it, faint, the way you hear a thing you have listened for all your life: an attention turned toward her, huge and gentle, like a hand held a little above a flame.

"I do not know what you are," said Edry. "Marda did not know either. But I have carried your word thirty-five years, and I spoke it every time. I do not ask for anything. I only want you to know that it was done."

She got up. It took some time. She went to the door in her grey cloak with the horn in her hand and went out on the steps, under a sky black and enormous with stars and the red star low over Brookham, larger than she had ever seen it.

Then she put Marda's horn to her mouth and blew. It was not a decree. It was only a note, long and clear and true, the best sound she had made in thirty years, and it went out over the sleeping roofs of hall and temple and Brookham, over the frozen fields and the cairn on the north road and the grass where Roston had been. For once it did not crack at all. She lowered it, and in the great dark behind her the fire in the hall, which had been nearly out, took a sudden golden breath and burned up.

*

It was Annric who found her in the morning, a thin brown boy of nine in a man's cloak, who came out on the steps at dawn and stood quite still. She sat on the top step against the doorpost as if she had sat down a moment to rest, her face turned south, the horn in her lap and frost on her hair. Her eyes were open, and in them was a look he had never seen on a grown person: a vast quiet, like someone who has finished a long task and set it down.

The others had come to the door behind him. Jomund, stocky and furious, clutching a blanket. Robert, wide-eyed. Elry with her hand over her mouth. Elora and Tombert side by side.

"Is she asleep?" said Tombert.

"No," said Annric.

"Is she dead?"

"Yes."

"Who is going to do the horn?"

It was a child's question and the only one that mattered. Annric stood a long moment on the cold step. Then he stooped and lifted the brass horn from his aunt's lap and found it heavy, green at the seams, and warm, as if it had been held. He did not know how to blow it. He had only ever heard it from a distance. He stood with it in both hands, a boy of nine in the winter dawn, and in the silence the five others waited.

He lifted it to his lips.

And in the hall behind them a light came on.

*

It was not firelight. It was a pale clear even light, like morning that has come through a wall, and it fell across the benches and the table and the lintel and the empty white throne. The children turned. In the middle of the great hall, where the flagstones were worn, the air had thickened, as it does above a road in summer, and in the thickness, one by one, as though stepping through a curtain, there were people.

There were six. They were grown, tall and lean and weathered, in hard leather and bright mail, with swords and spears in their hands, and every face wore the same faint dazed resolute bewilderment of soldiers wakened in a strange camp. A broad dark woman with a scar through one brow looked down at her own hands as though she had never seen them. A long thin man with a spear turned in a slow circle, staring at the rafters. A young woman put a hand to the mail at her breast and drew a quick breath. A big fair man with a lopsided grin shook out his shoulders and said in plain Eddish, to nobody: "Well. This is not the barracks."

Then the broad dark woman took a step forward and went down on one knee on the flagstones before a boy of nine, and said low, as one gives the first oath of a new beginning: "I am Maeora. I do not know how I came here. But I know whose hall this is." She looked up. "Tell us what you need."

Over the white hills, in the thin light of the winter morning, the red star set, and the sun came up on the Year 75.

In the place above the world, the Watcher, who had raised no hand against anyone and had done only what it swore, turned and looked at the Maker.

"Well," said the Maker softly. "That is the First Host. You will want to name them."

"Maeora," said the Watcher, "and Elric, and Annwin, and Edwin, and Edora, and Corry."

"And what are they for?"

"They are soldiers. That is all I will say. The rest is theirs."

And below, in the hall of Eddale, Jomund, who was the eldest and had decided it, gently took the old brass horn from Annric's hands, set it on the table, and lit the lamp.
