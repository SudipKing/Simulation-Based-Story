Prologue: The Two Who Watch

Before the first king was crowned, before the first mountain fell, there were only the Watchers, and the long silence of a world that had not yet been told what it was for.

In the beginning there was a world with no name, and it was whole.

It lay like a green-and-golden coin upon the dark, unbroken from edge to edge: a single continent of forest and jungle, of rock and river, ringed by an ocean that no ship had ever crossed because no ship yet existed. The wind moved over it and found nothing to stop it. The rain fell and found no roof. And above it, in a place that is not a place, two Watchers bent over the world the way a pair of players bends over a board, and each of them chose a side.

The first Watcher chose the Enchanted Wood, where the trees grew silver-barked and tall, where the light came down in slow green ribbons, and where every leaf seemed to remember the song that had made it. To that wood the Watcher gave a people: the Elves, long-limbed and keen-eyed, light of foot and heavy of memory, who loved the bow and the quiet places and the old green magic that hummed in the roots beneath their feet. The Watcher gave them a kingdom and called it Aethelgard, the Sovereign Realm of the Enchanted Woods. The Watcher gave them a capital, Sylvanor, rising at the heart of the wood like a held breath. And the Watcher gave them a king.

His name was Sylvas, and he was called the Sun-Watcher, for it was said that he rose before the dawn each morning to stand at the highest balcony of Sylvanor and greet the light as an old friend.

The second Watcher chose the Jungle. It was a wetter, louder, hungrier land, thick with vine and fern and the sudden violence of living things. To the jungle the second Watcher gave Humanity: quick-handed, short-lived, ferocious in their love of one another and in their appetites for everything else. They called their realm the Jungle Federation. They raised their capital, Anaconda, on a bend of green water where the great snakes were said to sleep beneath the roots of the city. And they set a king upon the throne whose name was Baron, and who took the crown the way a man takes a heavy tool, to use and not to admire.

Between the Enchanted Wood and the Jungle, a single mountain range stood, tall and unbroken and pitiless, a wall built by no hand. It ran from the northern cliffs to the southern shore, and no elf nor man could pass it. On one side the elves heard the jungle drums only as a rumor of thunder. On the other, the humans saw the silver light of the Enchanted Wood only as a faint glow against the evening clouds, and told one another stories about the witches and the ghosts who must dwell there.

The Watchers looked at the mountain for a long while.

"When shall we take it down?" asked the second.

And the first, who had learned patience from the elves, said: "Not yet. A fire laid too early is a fire that burns out. Let them grow. Let them become the kind of peoples who will be worth the war."

So they let the mountain stand. They did not know, those two, that they were writing the first line of a story that would run seven hundred and twenty-five years. They did not know that the story would outgrow them. They did not know that kings would rise and die in it like waves, that whole races would be born from the ash of burned cities and whole races would vanish like breath on cold glass, that a necromancer would crawl from a mine, that a ghost-cat would raise an army of the dead, that a pumpkin could be a monster, or that the last page of the world would be written not in the blood of a conquered people but in the strange, quiet bargain of survivors.

They knew only that the mountain stood, and that the year was One, and that the world was beginning.

They let the sun rise.

Chapter One: The Mountain Between

"A wall keeps two peoples safe from each other only until the day one of them grows tall enough to see over it."

(Elven proverb of Aethelgard)

The first eighty years of the world were years of green growing.

In the Enchanted Wood the elves multiplied slowly, as the long-lived do, and the birth of every child was a festival in itself. They raised Sylvanor stone upon silver stone, with wide courtyards open to the sky and windows cut to catch the first and last light. They built Nile on the river and Mystic in the whispering glades, and Ember, a city of red-gold lanterns and forges where the elven smiths worked, and Treelun, a village of the high canopy where bridges of woven root joined one great tree to the next and children learned to walk before they learned to fear heights. Five cities, small but proud. King Sylvas walked among them in his green cloak, and the people said that in his time there had never been a famine and never a winter without a fire.

Yet Sylvas was a king who watched more than he acted. The elves of Mystic muttered that their Sun-Watcher watched so carefully that he sometimes forgot to do anything at all. He took counsel from the old trees. He studied the maps of the east until the edges curled. He ordered bows to be strung and arrows to be fletched in numbers that seemed excessive for a land at peace, and when his council asked why, he answered only, "Because the mountain will not stand forever."

In the jungle, King Baron did not think about the mountain at all. He thought about the next harvest, the next flood, the next raid of the jungle cats on the outer farms. Anaconda swelled to a hundred and eighty-seven souls within its palisades, and three more villages sprouted around it like mushrooms around a stump: Basalt, built on a black outcrop where the stone was hard enough to make good blades; Qant, a river-town of traders and boatmen; and Byron, a hill-fort that looked west and sulked. The humans burned, hunted, farmed, and married young. They fought among themselves over water and over women and over slights that no one could later remember. By the Year 80, King Baron, who had lived with a hard heart and an iron stomach, was dead, and the throne had passed through the hands of a second king whom history has forgotten, and then to a third.

The third king was Chalice. He was a gentle man for a human monarch, which is to say he preferred to be loved rather than feared, and in the jungle that preference is a dangerous one. He ruled for a short time and a quiet one, and the people mourned him genuinely when he passed. Yet it was his death that opened the way for the one the humans would remember above all others.

Her name was Eden.

She was a queen made in the mold of the storm, quick to anger, quicker to command, and utterly without patience for the mountain that cut her people off from half the world. She stood on the walls of Anaconda in the Year 120 with her hair bound in a copper band and her wiser advisers murmuring behind her. Across the border of the mist, her scouts told her, the elves of Aethelgard were at last a power. Four hundred and eighty-eight of them. Two hundred and seven in arms. And the humans, for all their quickness, were four hundred and sixteen, with a hundred and forty-six who could hold a spear.

"They outnumber us," said her general.

"They outlive us," said Eden. "It is not the same thing as outfighting us. Let the wall come down."

And so the Watchers did what they had promised.

Chapter Two: The Day the Mountain Fell

The Year 120: the mountain that had stood since the first sunrise ceased to exist between one breath and the next.

It did not crumble. It did not fall. It simply was not.

Where the great range had stood, a plain opened under the morning light, rolling and bare, like the palm of a hand that has been shown after a long clenching. The wind that had been trapped on one side of the world for a hundred and twenty years rushed through it with a sound that every living thing in both lands heard and none would ever forget.

On the western edge of the new plain, King Sylvas, a hundred and thirty-eight years old and as straight as a young ash, drew his silver axe from its sheath. It was a heavy weapon for an elf, and an old one, and he had carried it more often to ceremonies than to battles. But he carried it that morning, and his soldiers saw him carry it, and the sight steadied them.

On the eastern edge of the plain, Queen Eden, ninety-one, with a silver spear in her fist and her face bright with a terrible joy, raised the weapon above her head, and four hundred humans roared.

Between them, for the first time since the world began, there was nothing but air.

"For Aethelgard," said Sylvas, softly, and the elves of the front rank heard him, and the words went back along the lines like a ripple on a pond. And then, from the high ground behind the plain, came the sound of ten thousand strings being drawn at once, a sound like a held breath in a vast hall, and the sky went dark with arrows.

The War of the Fallen Wall had begun.

It was not a war of one battle. It was a war of a hundred small ones, fought in ferns and creek beds and the burned timbers of farmsteads. The elves were the better archers and the better marchers, and they kept their lines and held their ground, while the humans were fiercer and quicker to rage and quicker to die. For a time the numbers favored one side, then the other. The elves took heart from the sight of their king in the front, silver axe in hand; they did not know that Sylvas, after the first great charge, withdrew to the rear and commanded from the shelter of the lines, as he would do for all the years of that war. Some would call him a coward. Others would call him wise. History, which is neither kind nor unkind, simply wrote down that he lived.

In the fourth year of the war, the human capital learned what grief could cost. Queen Eden fell. She did not die on a great field with her banner flying. She died as people most often die in wars, in the middle of a muddy afternoon, to an arrow no one saw loosed. Her armies fought on beneath her successor, Queen Georgia, ninety-four years old and calm as stone, who took the crown in the middle of a retreat and held the broken line together with nothing but the steadiness of her voice.

In the same hard year the elves paid for Treelun. It was the loveliest of their villages, the canopy city with its bridges of woven roots, and the humans reached it on a night of heavy rain and set it alight from below. The great trees burned like torches. The children who had learned to walk before they learned to fear heights were carried down on the shoulders of soldiers, crying, and the elves of Mystic who saw the glow on the horizon said afterward that the sky itself looked wounded.

Yet the elves did not break. They took the black-rocked village of Basalt in the same season, and fifteen of its humans, who had grown weary of a war not of their making, laid down their spears and joined the elves of their own free will. By the end of that fourth year the armies were small: two hundred elves, with forty-six soldiers, against a hundred and forty-nine humans who had only sixteen left who could fight.

The war went on for eight more years, grinding down toward its end. Qant burned, and Basalt, which had changed hands more times than anyone could count, burned with it. Byron and Anaconda fell, one after the other, under the green banners of Aethelgard. When it was over, in the twelfth year, the elves held the field. They had two hundred and thirty-eight souls left. Thirty could still lift a bow.

On the last day, King Sylvas, a hundred and fifty-one years old and the last living king of the first age, walked into the throne room of the Jungle Federation and found the great seat empty. No queen sat there. No banner hung. What remained of the human people had scattered into the ruined streets of Anaconda, a bare thirty of them; some would follow the elves into the Enchanted Wood, and some would creep into the corners of the jungle and swear that one day the green banners would fall.

Sylvas stood a long while in that hall. The Sun-Watcher had won the world. He found that it tasted of ash.

"It is only a matter of time," his advisers told him, "before the humans are gone."

"Yes," said Sylvas. "That is what I am afraid of."

And above the world, the two Watchers looked at what they had made, and at the thirty humans, and the two hundred and thirty-eight elves, and the empty throne, and one of them asked the other, gently, whether the story should end here.

"No," said the other. "Not yet. A world needs more than one song."

Chapter Three: The Sealed Mountain and the Poisoned Wastes

In the northwest, behind a wall of stone with a single passageway sealed shut, a people had been born without any elf or human knowing it.

They came out of the earth the way water comes out of rock: slowly, then all at once.

At the top-left corner of the world lay the Rocklands, a vast, ragged highland of granite and iron-veined slate, cut off from the Enchanted Wood by a mountain wall of its own. There was only one way in or out, a narrow gap, and the gap had been sealed with stone. No elf passed it. No human knew it existed. Inside, in a city of hammered halls called The Rock, lived thirty-seven dwarves.

They were short, wide, thick-fingered, and slow to speak. They had beards like braided cable and hands like anvils, and they believed with absolute conviction that a thing worth doing was a thing worth doing twice, once to make it and once to make it better. They called their empire Metallica, and they bowed to a king named Bishop, who was old, stern, and proud.

In the opposite corner of the world, to the northeast, a different birth took place. Where the human city of Qant had burned, nothing grew anymore. The ground was black and cracked, and a greasy yellow mist hung over it by day and glowed faintly by night. Out of that wasteland, as if conjured from its sour breath, came twenty orcs: gray-green, broad-shouldered, scarred by the land that made them, with eyes like coals. They raised a hard city of black stone called Orklan, and they hailed as king a warrior named Tang, and they called their nation Bahrain.

The wasteland was so fierce, so toxic, that no elf could cross it. Sylvas, who by now had begun to feel his great age settling around his shoulders like a cloak, learned of the two new nations only through whispers, and understood at once what the whispers meant: Aethelgard was walled in. The elves could not go north through the dwarven mountains. They could not go east through the orc wasteland. They could only go where they had always gone, deeper into their own forest.

The dwarves were the first to tear themselves apart.

In the sixth year of his rule, King Bishop was struck down in his own halls. The assassin's name was Grim, a dwarven lord of the deep mines, ambitious and cold, who had planned the deed for years. He stood over the old king's body with a bloody hammer in his hand and expected, perhaps, the dwarves of The Rock to cheer.

They did not. They turned on him. Bishop's son, Yeth, a young dwarf with his father's stubborn jaw and none of his father's patience, drew his axe and drove Grim out of the Rock, along with five loyal companions. They fled through the dark tunnels with the war cries of Metallica behind them. Of twenty-one dwarves who remained in the great halls, Yeth became king.

Grim did not die in the mountain. He and his five found another cavern and another cold hearth, and there Grim, who had once planned to rule all of Metallica, declared a new nation: Rakas. He gave the crown to his own son, a small boy with solemn eyes called Gri, who was, like Yeth, six years old on the day he became a king.

Neither child knew what the other's father had done. Neither would know, for a long time, that his own father had killed the other's.

In those years the elves entered a strange, quiet decline. In the Year 167, only twelve humans remained, all of them in Anaconda, pinned there by memory and by habit. Byron, with none left to tend it, was abandoned to the moss. Ember, the golden forge-city of the elves, had paid too high a price in the long war: so many of its young had died that the survivors could not keep the furnaces lit, and one grey winter the last lantern guttered out. Sylvas founded a new village, Istar, to rekindle hope, but it too failed, and the elves, who had been four hundred and eighty-eight at the war's beginning, dwindled to two hundred.

Across the world, in the dwarven mountains, a pattern had begun to emerge. In the east, King Tang of Bahrain had died and his son Ara had taken the throne of the orcs. In the north, Yeth was building up The Rock while Gri, grown now into a stern young king, was nurturing the new mines of Rakas. Everything was in balance. Everything, as in the tense moment before a cough, was waiting.

In the Year 180, Sylvas, then a hundred and ninety-odd years old by the elves' reckoning, rebuilt Istar. This time it took root, and it grew to forty souls. He founded another city called Ember Al, in honor of the golden city he had lost. The first sparks of the second age were growing.

Chapter Four: The Crumbling of the Walls

In the Year 200 the world held more peoples than at any time since its making, and not one of them knew the others were there.

By the Year 200, the world had changed its shape.

Anaconda had revived, improbably. Out of its twelve ragged survivors, a hardy line of humans had multiplied, until the city held fifty souls, more than the elves of Aethelgard could muster in the whole of the jungle. Sylvas's people numbered three hundred and twenty-four. The orcs of Bahrain, with their strange metabolism of wrath, had grown to a hundred and forty-one, building a second city called Orkiel under Ara, who died soon after, and left the throne to his son Monso. And in the northern mountains, the dwarves had flourished: Metallica with a hundred and ninety-seven, ruled by Yeth, and Rakas with a hundred and twenty-one, ruled by Gri, who had gone on to build a stronghold called the Iron Fort. Yeth, in answer, had raised a second city, The Stone.

Yet the old walls were failing. The seals that had held the dwarven passage shut for two hundred years were crumbling from the inside, as stone does when time and weather work on it. Within a generation, the elves and the dwarves would meet.

Nobody knew whether they would embrace or draw swords.

In the Year 230, the last wall fell.

The dwarves came out cautiously. First one of Yeth's patrols, then another; their helmeted heads emerging into the green light of the Enchanted Wood like moles into the day. The elves, who had been watching the passage for thirty years, held their bows lowered. Nobody fired. Nobody shouted. Both sides stared at the other across the little clearing, the dwarves squinting against the sun and the elves marveling at the thickness of their arms, and after a long moment, a dwarven sergeant took off his helmet in an awkward, ceremonial gesture, and the elven captain, uncertain what the gesture meant, bowed.

That was the beginning of peace.

For years, the dwarven armies marched out of the mountain and back again, and nothing happened. Gri, now called Grih by his people, went further: he walked alone, one morning, into the elven cities, with no weapon but a pipe in his teeth and an open hand, and was met with no hostility at all. The elves fed him honeyed bread. The dwarf said it was the best bread he had ever eaten and asked for the recipe, and the elves laughed. It was the first time anyone in either people had laughed in the presence of the other.

Yeth did not walk out. Yeth remained inside the mountain, building his new city of The Boulder, and Grih built another called Mith. Sylvas, for his part, built Treeluna, a city named in honor of the burned Treelun, and a second called Alstair, which unnerved his people because it lay so near Orkiel and its black gates. And in Anaconda, two hundred humans now lived beside seventy elves, learning to share a market, a river, and a long, shaky truce.

On the orcish side, the long decline of kings began. Monso died and left the throne to his son, another Ara, who made the city of Orkason. Ara reigned nineteen years, and then he too died and left the crown to his son Owen. The other cities looked at Owen and saw a weak king, and in the Year 238 the great empire of Bahrain broke apart into pieces. Every city became its own tiny nation. They squabbled, as such things do, and counted the days until someone would unify them or devour them.

By the end of the Year 238 the elves were twelve hundred strong, the dwarves of Metallica had reached four hundred and seventy-eight and those of Rakas three hundred and thirty-one, and the orcs, scattered and bickering, numbered a little over seven hundred between Bahrain, Orkiel, Orkason, and Orkasta. The world was a pot with the lid still on.

In the Year 253, the lid came off.

The Watchers, perhaps, grew bored. Perhaps they only wished to see what the world would do under a hammer. In any case, the sky opened and stones fell burning from the heavens. They struck the world's mountains, and only the mountains. One to the south, where the great rampart between the elves and the dwarves had stood: there it blew a vast new gateway, so that the two peoples could meet without a thin tunnel between them. One in the east, which sheared away the stone wall between The Boulder and Orkasta so that dwarf and orc were neighbors at last. Now there were three ways out of the dwarven mountain instead of one.

Peace held, for the moment. The elves and the dwarves looked up at a sky that had tried to kill them and had missed, and shrugged, and returned to their markets. But there was a smell on the wind that old Sylvas knew. He had smelled it in the Year 120.

Chapter Five: The War of Many Banners

"When the walls come down, the knives come out. This is the oldest law of the world, older than the mountains and older than the kings."

In the Year 256, the world went to war.

It began with a declaration, flat and formal, from Aethelgard: Kingdom Aethelgard declared war upon the orcish city of Orkiel. Within the same hour, a second declaration came from the north: Kingdom Metallica declared war upon the orcish city of Orkasta. Neither Sylvas nor Yeth had coordinated the strikes, but neither was surprised by the other's. The orcs had always been the easy prey, divided, poisoned by their own land, and a thousand elves and dwarves wanted what the orcs had: the black soil, the dark ore, the place to expand.

In Orkiel, Owen, the weak king, did not live to see the first siege. A soldier of Orkiel named Topo, who loved his city more than he loved his king, struck him down in his own hall, and Owen's son Namasor, a hard, red-eyed young orc, took the crown of Bahrain in the middle of the carnage. It did not help. Two months into the war, Rakas, the other dwarven kingdom, also marched upon Orkiel, and the city that had been a nest of orcs for two centuries was suddenly a nest under three armies.

Orkasta fell first. Of the whole city, only eight orcs were left alive when Yeth's soldiers marched in, and he granted them no mercy, and the city became part of Metallica.

Then, in the Year 260, something happened that none of the Watchers had planned. In the town of Istar, where the elven farmers tended their fields with the weary efficiency of a people at war, a farmer was cruel to a plant. Perhaps it was the way a seed was crushed under a boot. Perhaps it was the way a wounded vine was cut away and left to wither. Whatever it was, the plants of Istar remembered. In the very center of the town, at dawn, the earth split, and from the cracked soil there rose a thing that had no business existing: a gigantic orange pumpkin, as large as a house, with veins like black rope and a hollow, grinning mouth.

It screamed. It was not the scream of a living thing. It was the scream of something that had been very patient.

The elves of Istar, who had never seen a monster, did not know whether to run or to laugh. They ran. And the pumpkins, because they were many, and had always been many, because the soil of the Enchanted Wood had never forgotten its grudges, came behind them, rolling and bounding and dripping seeds.

At the same moment, with only a single orc left alive in the city, Orkiel fell to Sylvas. The Sun-Watcher had won a war in the east and was losing one in the west.

For three years the elves and the humans of Anaconda, summoned to the fight by desperate messengers, battled the monstrous pumpkins. The human soldiers, recruited because the elven ones were away at Orkiel, fought with torches and iron sickles, and they fought better than anyone could have predicted. The pumpkins were eradicated in the Year 263. But the cost was dreadful: the elves, who had been twelve hundred strong, were now eight hundred, and the humans of Anaconda were down to a hundred and forty. The dwarves, who had stayed out of the pumpkin war, continued to grow.

It was too early to rest. In the Year 267, pumpkins burst from the earth again, in Anaconda this time. At the same moment Sylvas and Yeth, united in a rare moment of alliance, declared war upon the surviving orc kingdom of Bahrain. And Grih of Rakas, who had been watching from his mountain hold with a thoughtful expression, challenged the last orc city, Orkason, to single combat.

The orcs would never have won. Everything in the war was against them.

In the Year 268, Bahrain retook Orkasta in a surprise attack, only to lose it again to the dwarves of Metallica within the season. Alstair, the elven city that had unnerved so many for lying beside the orc gates, fell to the orcs, who burned it. After a year of grim struggle against the pumpkins, the soldiers of Anaconda broke the monster horde and turned their eyes eastward. They joined the elven line, and together they stormed Orkiel and destroyed it. King Namasor, last true king of the orcs, was struck down by an archer from The Stone's military in the heat of the assault. His daughter Naera took the crown for a single, bitter morning, and was killed.

In the Year 271, the war ended. Bahrain was gone. Orklan and Orkiel lay in ruins. The thirteen orcs who remained in Orkason surrendered at the gate and knelt, and Grih, looking at the small crowd of trembling green-skinned captives, did something that surprised everyone: he did not kill them. He accepted the surrender, claimed the city as his own, and let them live.

It might have been mercy. It might have been calculation. A dwarf, as a rule, does not waste what can be put to work.

The wasteland, with its orcs thinned to a handful, was still too poisonous for the elves or the dwarves to colonize. But perhaps, some said, if the orcs were allowed to live, they might one day make the land fruitful again, as the humans had done in the jungle of Aethelgard. Only time would tell.

And time, as the Watchers noted with some interest, was only beginning to speak.

Chapter Six: The Secret of the Two Fathers

There is an old saying among the dwarves: a debt of blood does not die. It only sleeps, as the roots of the mountain sleep, and wakes when the ground grows warm.

Peace, after so many wars, felt like a strange coat that did not quite fit.

In the quiet that followed the orc wars, a thought began to move among the keener minds of the court at Sylvanor, and it was a thought that no one wished to say aloud. It was this: that the king of Metallica, Yeth, and the king of Rakas, Grih, were the sons of two men who had once stood in the same hall, with a hammer between them.

Grim had killed Bishop. Bishop's son was Yeth. Grim's son was Grih. And both boys had been made kings at the age of six, shielded from the story by the same swirl of blood and confusion that had given them their crowns.

"Does Yeth know?" Sylvas asked his counsellors one evening, with the lamp light low on his cheek. "Does Grih? Has it been so long that the memory has crumbled into dust? Or does it sit in them like a coal in a pile of ash, waiting for a breath of wind?"

No one answered. No one knew. In the dwarven halls the old ones who had witnessed the killing were long dead, and the young ones had been raised on a tidy lie: that the two nations of Metallica and Rakas were cousins, split by a squabble over mining rights.

In the Year 273 Yeth and Grih did something that made every elf in Aethelgard hold its breath. They signed a treaty.

It was a thick, heavy thing, written in the blunt script of the dwarves on a slab of polished granite. They called it Forged Steel. It joined Metallica and Rakas into a single alliance, the two dwarven realms standing shoulder to shoulder. Together they counted eight hundred and thirty-three dwarves. Aethelgard, at the same moment, counted eight hundred and twelve elves.

Eight hundred and thirty-three to eight hundred and twelve. The dwarves, for the first time, outnumbered their neighbors.

"A foreshadowing," whispered the elves of Mystic. "Or a coincidence."

Sylvas, who had lived through enough coincidences to distrust them, began to look at the map.

Chapter Seven: The War of Forged Steel

"The elves strike first. This is how the elves always die: by being the first to be brave."

In the Year 284, King Sylvas, who had survived the war against the humans by standing at the back, who had survived the long cold peace by watching the maps, and who had survived two hundred and some winters by trusting his own sense of when a storm was coming, did a thing that no one expected.

He struck first.

He declared war on Rakas. Not on Metallica, not on the alliance as a whole, but on Rakas alone, the smaller of the two dwarven nations, the one led by the unreadable Grih. It was a gamble. If Yeth honored the alliance, the elves would face both dwarven kingdoms in a war they could not win. If Yeth did not, Sylvas could shatter Rakas and then deal with Metallica on his own terms.

A hundred and thirty-four soldiers marched from Mystic, Istar, and Nile toward the mountains where the empires met. A hundred more, with Sylvas himself in the center of their ranks, rolled out from the capital, the old king bundled in his green cloak and carried in a litter, for his knees no longer served him as they once had. The horns of Aethelgard sounded across the Enchanted Wood. Birds fled from the trees.

The dwarves answered. Seventy-one dwarven soldiers of Storm, a fortress-city of Rakas, poured into Mystic like water down a drain. Sixty-three from the Iron Fort and forty-six from Mith held the front line against the elves. And the alliance, so quickly tested, held: Yeth charged out of The Rock with sixty-six of his own troops, the very core of Metallica, and crashed into the elven line at the gates of Mystic. He mobilized eighty-two from The Stone to support the weary front at the Iron Fort and Mith.

It was war on three fronts, and the elves were outnumbered, outgunned, and outraged.

And yet, as it turned out, the dwarves had not planned for the elves to fight so cleverly.

The first surprise came in the Year 286, when the elves marched into Orkason, an orc-city that Grih had claimed years before, and found it empty. The orcs had gone. All of them, the thirteen who had knelt at Grih's gate, had left in the night, whether into the wastes or into some darker place no one knew. The elves took the city without a blow.

In the Year 288, the second surprise came. Sylvas's army crashed into Mith. By then only a single dwarf stood to guard it, an old man with a hammer, who looked at the oncoming army and laughed, and was cut down. Mith was taken. The Iron Fort, stripped of its defenders, was razed. Rakas, with only forty dwarves left in all its halls, and none of them soldiers, was a hollow shell. Meanwhile, the elves of Aethelgard counted six hundred still, a hundred and twelve in arms, and sixty of those already marching on the front, their eyes set on Storm.

"The alliance," murmured Sylvas, gazing at the distant mountains, "has meant very little."

It was true. Yeth had retreated. At the first sign of the elves' resolve, the king of Metallica, who had signed Forged Steel with so much ceremony, pulled his soldiers back from Mystic and dug in. Sylvas, secure in his own judgment, withdrew to Sylvanor to command from afar. And Yeth, too, withdrew, the king of the dwarves and the king of the elves each pulling back from the battle as if from a fire, though the elves stood now at the very threshold of Metallica.

Then came the third surprise, and the cruelest.

Mith was razed, and then Storm fell as well, the elves taking it with the last of their strength. Rakas was destroyed. The alliance was no more. Forged Steel, which had been signed in granite, dissolved like sugar in rain. And then Yeth saw his chance. He looked at the elven army, ragged and exhausted, only twenty elves at the very front, and he did what a king does when his enemy has gone limp. He turned around.

He took Storm. It became a part of Metallica. And with a bellow that echoed down the mountain, he commanded his last eighty soldiers to crush the twenty exhausted elves in front of him.

It might have ended there. It might have ended in a slaughter, the elven front line trampled, the road to Mystic open, Aethelgard cut in half. But the elves of Istar, a little band of soldiers who had been sent to guard the flank, did something that no general would have written down in any manual. They ran. They fled the front line, not out of cowardice but out of brilliance, and swung around the dwarven flank like a hand closing, and fell upon Orkasta, the city Yeth had taken from the orcs, and took it from the rear. Then, with the swiftness of a wind, they pressed on to The Boulder, Yeth's proud new city, and when his scattered troops rushed to defend it, they lost it too.

Istar's soldiers came back to the front in triumph, but they were too few to hold what they had taken. With no one left to live in it, Orkasta was destroyed. And in the maelstrom of the fighting, The Boulder and Storm were both torn from the map, burned so thoroughly that not even ruins remained.

That was the war's turning point, and its tragedy. The elves had won more cities than they could hold, and lost more than they had ever dared to risk.

In the Year 292, the war entered its final act, and it began with a funeral.

The great King Sylvas, the Sun-Watcher, who had stood on the high balcony of Sylvanor at every dawn for nearly three centuries, died in his bed, with the first light of morning on his face. His age had finally caught up with him. He had outlived three human kings and a dozen orc ones; he had broken a wall and made two new worlds, and at the end he did not seem frightened, only tired, as a man is tired who has come to the last page of a very long book.

At the same time, on a field in the north, the elven armies, now commanded by his marshals, charged at last toward The Rock itself. The city was almost empty. Only three soldiers stood in the gates. The Stone still had seventy-five who had never left its walls. And in the hours when the elves broke the first line of the Rock's defense, Yeth, the king who had killed no one with his own hand and had kept his hands clean for nearly two centuries, rode out to the front to face them. He was killed there, in the mud, struck down by an elven spear.

The elves, with the capital almost in their grasp and both kings dead, surged forward.

And then, in the last desperate minute, the soldiers of The Stone arrived. Seventy-five dwarves who had stayed behind their own walls came racing along the mountain road, shields locked, voices bellowing a war-song that was older than the nation. They slammed into the side of the elven army and the elves were forced to turn. The Rock was saved.

And then came the strangest thing of all.

In the fields outside The Rock, in the cold light after the battle, an old farmer straightened up from his plowing and leaned on his stone hammer. He was tall for a dwarf, and gray, and tired. He looked out over the battlefield, the bodies of elves and dwarves lying in the stubble, and he said nothing. People who saw him thought he was just another peasant, come to scavenge for armor.

It was Grih.

The king of Rakas, who had lost his entire nation, who had lost Storm and Mith and the Iron Fort, who had lost everything, had simply disappeared into the countryside and taken up a farmer's plow. He had been alive the whole time. He had been working the fields of The Rock for years, in the shadow of the city whose last king had just died.

"Yeth is dead," someone told him.

Grih looked at the hammer in his hand. Then he looked at the hall of the Rock, where the great doors stood open.

"Then I suppose," he said, "that I had better go in."

Chapter Eight: The Farmer King

"He did not forget his power," the chroniclers would write. "He had only put it down, as a man puts down a heavy thing, to rest."

The queen of the elves was a hundred and eleven years old, the only child of the dead king, and she had been waiting for her father's crown since before the Year 200.

Her name was Dostier.

She took the throne of Aethelgard in the shadow of her father's funeral pyre, and her first act as queen was to stare at the map. The elves held most of the north, now: Mystic, Istar, the ruined fields of Orkason. The dwarves were broken, their kings dead, their cities burned. The war, by any measure, had been won.

But the war was not over. It was not the sort of war that ends when someone wins. It was the sort that ends when someone is tired.

Grih strode into the great hall of The Rock with the stone hammer on his shoulder, in the same mud-caked boots he had worn in the fields. The nobles of Metallica, who had expected a king to come to them, wearing a crown and carrying a banner, stared at him in astonishment. Then someone, an old dwarf with a white beard, knelt. Then another. By the time Grih reached the throne, the entire court was kneeling.

The farmer sat down upon the throne of Bishop and said, "Bring me the maps."

He took command of the army, such as it was. He did not wait. He did not sue for peace. He sent his entire strength, every dwarf who could carry a weapon, against Mystic and Istar, straight at the heart of the elves' conquests, and he did it with the cold fury of a man who had lost everything once already.

It was a terrible mistake. The elves, who had spent the last six years learning to fight dwarves, destroyed his army utterly. The front line shifted to the right entrance of the mountain, and there, in the shadow of the stone, Grih's survivors marched through the ruins of Orkason, putting it to the torch, until the final city of the orcs was gone.

Both sides were exhausted. Both sides had run out of soldiers and of hope. On the plain, in the smoke of the burned cities, Grih and Dostier, a farmer-king and a daughter-queen, who had never met, sent messengers to one another. They met in the ruins of Orkason, with the wind blowing ash across the ground between them.

"I killed your father's army," said Dostier. "You killed my father's. Neither of us has an army left."

"No," said Grih.

"Shall we stop?"

Grih looked at the ruins, and at the dead, and at the hammer in his hand.

"Yes," he said. "I think we shall."

And so the war of the elves and the dwarves ended, in the Year 292, in a peace that no one had planned and that no one believed would last. The elves had a hundred and ninety-one souls, thirty-eight of whom could still fight. The dwarves of Metallica, a hundred and thirty-six, only three of them soldiers. The cities were burning. The fields were ash. The great alliances had been broken, and the great kings were dead.

Nine years later, the sky itself would pass judgment.

Chapter Nine: The Age of Despair

Snow fell on the world for the first time in the Year 301, and it did not stop falling.

It began as a few white flakes drifting out of a grey sky, and the elves, who had never seen such a thing, held out their hands and laughed. The dwarves, who had heard of snow in their old songs, kept their hoods up and their eyes narrow. Within a month, the laughing had stopped.

The snow came in sheets, then in storms, then in a great white silence that lay over the land like a lid. The sun was a pale coin behind the clouds. The crops died in the frozen fields. The forest of Aethelgard, which had never known a hard winter, locked itself in ice. The rivers, which had flowed since the first year of the world, froze to the bed. Starvation moved through the halls of both peoples like a quiet guest. Hypothermia took the old and then the young.

The Age of Despair had begun.

By the end of the Year 301, only eighty-eight dwarves and seventy-seven elves were left alive in the whole world. They hunkered in their cellars and burned their furniture and ate their seed corn. Neither people had enough strength to fight, or enough hatred to remember why they should.

Even in that dark, there were those who tried.

In the Year 309, Grih, who had been a farmer and a king and was now an old man with a stone hammer and a stubborn heart, founded a new village at the edge of the northern ice. He called it O'Raka, an echo of the lost nation of his father's making. It was a small, fierce, bitter place. It would be a long time before anyone knew whether it would survive.

And in the same year, something worse than the snow came up out of the earth.

It crawled from the mouth of an abandoned mine beneath Ember Al, the city Sylvas had founded in honor of his lost golden Ember. It had been a man once, a mage, perhaps, of some forgotten human order, who had gone into the dark in search of forbidden knowledge and had been changed by what he found. His name was Gran Kal. He was a necromancer, and his skin was gray as candle wax, and his eyes were pits of cold green light.

The soldiers of Anaconda, passing the mine on patrol, saw him. They did not stop to ask his business. Their spears found his heart before he could finish his first spell, and Gran Kal died with a rattle and a look of mild astonishment.

But he had already woken something. A handful of skeletal soldiers, dragged from their ancient graves by the last pulse of his magic, rose up around the mine and fell upon the weakened, defenseless city of Ember Al. The elves who lived there, starved, frostbitten, and unarmed, were cut down without a fight.

Ember Al fell. For the second time in the history of the world, a city called Ember was lost. The people said it was cursed.

Chapter Ten: The Thaw

And then, in the Year 340, the snow began to melt.

It melted slowly, the way a thought dissolves. Little streams ran out from under the drifts. The ground softened. A green shoot, so small it could hardly be seen, pushed up through the black mud of the Enchanted Wood.

Spring had come at last.

The people who had survived the winter crept from their cellars like ghosts. Elves, thin and silent, looked at the sky and wept. Dwarves, hardier, laughed and stamped the dirt and began to dig. In the north, in the small bitter village of O'Raka, the survivors stood on the walls and watched the ice retreat, and somebody, perhaps the old king himself, said quietly, "We are still here."

The numbers were small. The elves had fallen to fifty-four; the dwarves, thanks to the harshness of the northern hills, had grown to eighty. But the spring brought a boom, as springs do. In the Year 347, the dwarves had risen to a hundred and thirty-seven, while the elves, who bear children as rarely as they bear grudges, had added only three.

But there was one gift the snow had left behind, and it was a great one.

The wasteland was gone. The poisoned soil of the northeast, the black land that had given birth to the orcs, had vanished under the frozen white, and when the snow melted, the toxic mist did not return. Where the orcs had once dwelled, the jungle came creeping back, green and fierce and glad, and the northwest became habitable for all.

It was a bitter consolation, but a consolation all the same. The world had been scoured. In the long, cold hand of the Age of Despair, the old poisons had been washed away, and the survivors, thin and hungry and almost without hope, found themselves standing in a clean place.

O'Raka, the village at the edge of the ice, stood among them. It carried, as its founder had intended, the flame of Rakas.

Chapter Eleven: The Rebellion of Dua

She was the daughter of a king and the lover of a rebel, and her heart was too large for the world she had inherited.

By the Year 363, Anaconda, the old jungle capital of the humans, had passed under the nominal rule of Aethelgard. The elven queen Dostier, in order to seal the peace with her human subjects and secure their loyalty, had given the governorship of the city to a man named Dua, who was a human of the old jungle line, and who had, to the surprise of the whole court, become her lover.

Dua was a clever man, a restless one, handsome in a rough way. His city had twenty-three humans and seven elves living within its walls. Few in number, but humans have always known how to make a few seem like many. He listened to the old stories, the legends of the Jungle Federation and its kings; he heard the humans muttering at night about the days when their people had ruled half the world. And one day, in a moment that the humans of Anaconda would remember for generations, he stood up in the marketplace, and he proclaimed himself king.

He named his new nation Bierth.

Dostier heard the news in her throne room, and her face turned white. Aethelgard declared war to retake it, as it was bound to do. But the war was a pathetic thing. Aethelgard had twenty-four elves with four soldiers. Bierth had thirty humans with six soldiers. On the other side of the mountain, Metallica had grown to two hundred and seventy-four, with fifty soldiers, and the elves watched, nervous, as the dwarves held their swords still and did not move. It would have been so easy for them to take advantage. It would have been so easy to finish off the dying elves for good.

They did not move. Not yet.

The elven queen, who had loved a man and been betrayed by him, did something that no one anticipated. She mounted a horse and rode alone into Bierth, to challenge Dua to single combat in the great plaza of his new kingdom.

Dua did not come out. He sent his soldiers.

In the Year 365, Dostier, queen of the elves, who had held her father's throne for seventy-three years, was killed by a soldier named Oreero. By a bitter irony, Oreero's father, Dadier, was Dua's oldest and best friend.

She died with her hand on her sword and her eyes on the door through which her lover should have come.

The twist that followed, the Watchers agreed, was the cruelest of their long game.

The crown of Aethelgard did not pass to a rival, a general, or a distant cousin. It passed to a young man named Ambe, the only child of Dostier and Dua, half elf and half man, born of the love that had broken a kingdom. He took his dead mother's throne. His father was the enemy of his people.

Year after year, border skirmishes bled both sides. By the Year 375, twelve years into the war, Aethelgard was down to twenty-six, with no soldiers left at all. Bierth had twenty-two, with three who could fight. The land between them was a patchwork of ambushes.

In the same year, in the mountains where the elven city of Mystic had once stood, King Grih of Metallica built a new city. He called it Yeth.

No one asked why. Everyone wondered. It was said in the taverns of The Rock that the old king had never forgotten the war, never forgotten the slain Yeth, whose father had been murdered by his father, and that in naming a city for him, he was making a kind of apology that he could never say aloud. It was said, too, that he was merely a sentimental old dwarf. The truth was probably both.

Chapter Twelve: The Dragon and the Traitor

In the Year 387, the last great power of the old world awoke, and it was not elf, nor dwarf, nor man.

In the far northeast, beyond the places where any map had ever been drawn, a dragon slept.

It had slept for a very long time, under a mountain of cold stone, coiled around the memory of a fire that had been lit before the first sunrise. Nobody knows who woke it, whether the Watchers or the shifting of the earth or some distant dream. But in the Year 387, its eyes opened, and the lids rolled back from two pits of molten gold.

It rose. It stretched its wings, and the mountain cracked. It flew over the sea of forest, an ember falling through the dusk, and the elves of Nile looked up from their fishing nets and saw a thing in the sky that had no name.

The dragon burned Nile. It burned the docks and the lanterns and the fishing nets, and then it turned its great head toward Sylvanor, the capital, the oldest city in the world.

For months the elves fought it. They had so few soldiers that the old and the young took up bows. They shot arrows that skittered from golden scales. They poured oil and lit it. They dug pits. They sang the old green songs, and the trees themselves, as old as the world, reached down with their branches and tried to hold the beast. At last, in a final desperate hour, a band of archers found the one soft place beneath the monster's wing and loosed a hundred arrows at once. The dragon screamed and fell, and lay across the city like a mountain.

Six elves were dead. The dragon was dead. It should have been a victory.

In the middle of the fight, as if the world could not bear to be so kind, an earthquake struck the center of Sylvanor. Houses fell in streets. Old walls cracked. The great hall of the Sun-Watcher, the balcony from which Sylvas had greeted every dawn, groaned, shifted, and began to lean.

King Dua of Bierth, who had been watching the dragon fall from across the border, did not hesitate. He marched on Nile while its people were weeping over their dead, and took it without a blow. Then he gathered his small army and turned them toward Sylvanor.

In Sylvanor, King Ambe, half elf and half man, stood on the cracked balcony and watched his father's banners approach. He was, for a moment, perhaps, very young, and very afraid.

He did not have long to be afraid.

A soldier of his own guard, an elf named Tohil, stepped out of the shadows, and killed him.

No one has ever learned why. Some said Tohil had been bought by Dua's gold. Some said he was mad. Some said he was the kind of man who has no loyalties, who had waited all his life for a chance to burn something, and finally found it. Whatever the reason, Ambe died on the balcony of his mother's palace, and the crown of Aethelgard passed, by a law that no one had thought to question, to the nearest heir.

That heir was Emy, a child of three, the son of Tohil himself.

Tohil looked at the baby on the throne. He looked at the cracked ceiling. He looked at the last of the elves of Sylvanor, a few dozen old men and women, standing in the great hall with the ghosts of their dragon-slain neighbors in their eyes.

And then, in a moment that would haunt every elf who ever heard it told, he killed the king.

He killed his own son. He killed the elves who stood watching, one by one, with a calm, methodical thoroughness that no one could stop. And when the hall was silent, he walked out into the streets and set the city on fire.

Sylvanor burned. After three hundred and eighty-two years, the great capital of the elves, the city of the Sun-Watcher, the city where the first dawn had been greeted, was ash. There was no one left to mourn it, and the wind blew the cinders out over the forest like a flock of dying fireflies.

Aethelgard, the Sovereign Realm of the Enchanted Woods, had been murdered from the inside.

Chapter Thirteen: The Slow March

"We did not conquer them," the dwarven general Ones would say, years later. "We merely arrived."

In the Year 400, Grih, king of Metallica, did what he had not done in a hundred and eight years of rule. He declared war on a man.

Bierth was all that remained of the jungle's human pride. It held thirty-one humans and seven elves, with Dua at its head and only four soldiers at his back. Against it Grih sent one hundred and ninety dwarven soldiers, marching out of the Rock under banners of black and silver.

It should have been a quick war. It was not. The dwarves were old, and in no hurry, and they had a proverb: the mountain does not run. Sixty of the soldiers marched toward Nile, and they did not march so much as stroll, for it was a very long way, and the sun was warm, and the roads were soft after the long thaw. The elves of Nile, who had a handful of men and women with bows, watched the dwarves come and decided not to die.

There was no fight. The dwarves arrived, stood in the square, looked around, and Nile, by the sheer force of a presence it could not hope to match, surrendered. Tohil, the traitor of Sylvanor, who had been expected by all the world to make a last stand, did nothing at all. He watched, hooded, from a window, and did not lift a hand.

Then the dwarves walked on to Anaconda.

And here the story turned once more, for Anaconda did not surrender. The humans, whom the elves had called weak and short-lived and foolish, took up spears and axes and fought with the fury of men who had nothing left but their name. In the narrow streets of the old capital, twenty-three humans killed seventeen dwarven soldiers. It was a ratio that old dwarven generals would study for years. But it could not last. The numbers were too uneven.

In the heart of the city, in the great hall of the Jungle Federation, a dwarven general named Ones fought King Dua in a grim, close struggle, and killed him.

It was the Year 405. The war had lasted five years, though only a few months of it had been spent fighting.

When it was over, there were two humans left in Anaconda and two elves left in Nile, and both cities, those last pockets of an older world, were under the rule of Grih. Metallica, a two-hundred-and-fifty-seven-year-old empire, was the sole nation in all the world. And its ruler, Grih, was two hundred and fifty-seven years old himself. He had been on the throne for a hundred and twelve years. He had lost Rakas, his father's old kingdom, and everything in it, but he had never stopped.

In the Year 406, the soldiers returned to the Rock. There were fireworks over the black granite towers. There were feasts, and songs, and a thousand dwarves in the streets, drunk on ale and glory.

Grih, the old farmer-king, stood on the highest balcony of the Rock and looked out over the world he had won. It was nothing but dwarves, now. The elves were two. The humans were two. The orcs were long gone.

"Is this world over?" some young dwarf asked, in the happy tumult of the feast.

No one answered.

And somewhere above the clouds, where the Watchers sat, one of them said, quietly, to the other: "Should we close the chapter here?"

The other thought of a king named Sylvas, who had once conquered the world and stood in an empty hall tasting ash.

"No," said the second Watcher. "Let us spice it up."

Chapter Fourteen: The Last of the Humans

Humanity was the shortest-lived of all the peoples of the world, and perhaps for that reason the most brightly burning.

For sixty-six years the world was quiet, and the quiet was the loneliest sound it had ever made.

In Anaconda, the old jungle capital, the humans grew in number for a time. A few married, and a few more were born, and for a season the narrow streets echoed again with the cries of children and the haggling of the market. But their lives were brief, and the span of one human was less than a dwarf's long afternoon. Grih's ruling councils watched them with a curious, detached sympathy, as a gardener watches the cycles of the quick-blooming flowers. Soon, one by one, the humans began to perish: of fever, of age, of the simple fact of being human.

In the Year 472 the last of them, a woman named Aria, stood in the empty marketplace of Anaconda and declared a new empire. She called it Ense. It was a nation of one. She was old, and her hair was white as the old snow, and she carried the weight of five hundred years of a people she had never met. Her coronation was a short one. A month later, in a small room under a window that looked out on the jungle where her ancestors had once hunted, she closed her eyes, and the humans were gone from the world.

There was no great funeral. There was no great song. A few dwarves in the garrison laid flowers on her grave and then went back to their posts.

In Nile, four elves remained, fishing the river as they always had. In seventy years, only two new elves had been born there. The dwarves of Metallica, who now numbered more than two thousand, looked at that small village and decided, with the kind of indifference that is the most dangerous mercy, that it was not worth the trouble to destroy. King Grih, rapt in his old triumphs, let it stand.

It would prove the most costly decision of his long life.

Chapter Fifteen: The Storm That Broke the World

In the Year 473, the sky began to weep, and it did not stop.

It began with thunder, and thunder is nothing. But the thunder did not pass. It rolled over the continent for days, a continuous dull roaring like a beast with the whole world in its jaws, and then the rain began. It was not the rain of the old jungle, warm and generous. It was cold and heavy and falling in sheets, hour after hour, week after week, until the ground itself could not drink any more.

The Age of Tears had come.

The great storm, a hurricane the size of a nation, hung over the heart of the world, and in its eye a terrible thing happened. The continent, which had been one unbroken sheet of land since the first sunrise, began to split. The mountains that had once stood between elves and men, destroyed in the Year 120 and turned to desert, had left the center of the land weak and sandy. The water found it. The storm carved a channel through the world, diagonally, from one corner of the ocean to the other, and the ocean rushed in.

When the thunder at last died, the world was in two halves.

On the left, to the north and the west, stood the dwarves: The Rock, The Stone, O'Raka, the cities of Metallica, all on a single stretch of land. On the right, to the south and the east, stranded across the new water, were Nile, with its four elves, and Anaconda, with its three dwarven garrison. The two shores were separated by a tide of cold green water that no one had ever crossed.

And the storm was not done. The enchantments of the old woods, the silver-barked trees, the blue-lit glades, the moss that glowed at midnight, were pulled out of the soil like teeth. The jungle, which had stood for four centuries, turned to a mire. The murky waters stank of rot. The mud was thick as porridge. The land, which had once been the loveliest in the world, was now a swamp.

Dwarves are creatures of stone. They love dry ground and deep tunnels, and a marsh is hateful to them. The three dwarves in Anaconda, left behind on the wrong side of the world, huddled in their wet fort, knowing they were probably dead. And in Nile, where four elves had been quietly ignored for seventy years, something began to stir.

Grih, in the Rock, understood too late what his mercy had cost him. Metallica could not cross the water. The elves, whom he had let live, might do whatever they chose.

Chapter Sixteen: Embera's Rebellion

"A kingdom is not made of towers. A kingdom is made of those who refuse to leave."

Her name was Embera, and she was the leader of Nile, a small, unremarkable woman with fishing-rough hands and a gaze like a drawn bow.

She stood on the bank of the new river in the Year 491, eighteen years after the great storm, and looked across the water at the dwarven mainland. She had spent those eighteen years watching, waiting, counting the dwarven patrols that never came. It was a different thing from Aethelgard. It was smaller and meaner, born not in the glory of the Enchanted Wood but in the wet misery of the swamp. But it had a flag, and a name, and a will. The whole city of Yeth, in a gesture that no one could have foreseen, followed them. The old king's soldiers came across the narrow part of the water and fell upon them. There was no surrender, and so there was annihilation. And in Anaconda, in the swamp that was no home for any dwarf, something happened that no one expected. Two children were born.

They were small and pale, and they coughed in the damp. But they lived.

Chapter Seventeen: Boats, Bog, and Ancient Ruins

The war ended in the Year 502 because there was nothing left for anyone to do.

The two sides, exhausted by a conflict neither could win, simply stopped. There was no treaty, no parley, no ceremony. The soldiers of Metallica laid down their spears on the western shore. The elves of Aethel lowered their bows on the east. Across the river, for the first time in eleven years, no one was shooting at anyone.

The world went on, and progress was made.

In Aethel, the elves, a few at first and then a few more, began to master the one craft that the water gave them. They were an old people, and they remembered how to listen to timber. By the Year 552 they had grown to twelve, a small, stubborn number, but growth is growth. They had built boats: small fishing craft, with sails of woven reed, tough little vessels that rode low in the swamp-water. They were not warships yet. But those who had eyes to see could already tell what they would become.

Anaconda, too, was twelve. No one understood how. The land, a seething swamp of mosquitoes and rot, was uninhabitable for any dwarf. The garrison who had been stranded there had grown into a community, and the children of the two born in the war had grown, and the swamp-born dwarves had adapted to their strange home, with webbed hands and leather lungs and a loathing for the stone halls of their ancestors.

Metallica, meanwhile, was reaching for a peak it had nearly touched a century before. It had almost reached two thousand, a hundred years earlier, only to be dashed by the rebellion and the living plants. Now it crossed the limit. By the Year 552 the dwarves of the mainland numbered two thousand and a hundred, the highest of their long history. They did a thing that frightened the elves, who watched from across the water with their long memories. They removed the ruins.

All of them. Every broken wall of Anaconda's old human quarter. Every cracked pillar of Nile. They scoured the mainland of its past, tearing up the stone, filling in the foundations, leaving only the new. It was as if, in the dwarven mind, history was a kind of dirt. Chapter Eighteen: The Crocodile Kingdom

In the Year 555, something woke in the swamp.

The crocodiles had lived in the marshes since before the first sunrise. They were old, cold-blooded, patient creatures, the oldest living things on the continent, and for a thousand generations they had done nothing but lie in the mud with their eyes just above the surface of the water, waiting for something to happen.

Something did. Perhaps it was the swamp itself, thick with the stagnant magic of the storm that had torn the Enchanted Wood apart. Perhaps it was the gaze of the Watchers, who had grown fond of strange experiments. In any event, in the Year 555, the crocodiles of the great swamp mutated.

They stood. They found that they had hands. They found that they had words.

Within a single generation, they were building. Not with the grace of the elves or the stubbornness of the dwarves, but with a patient, brutal efficiency. They raised mud-brick halls and woven-reed longhouses. They developed songs, ancient and low, that vibrated in the water. They had a culture of their own, and a code, and a flag of green-black stripes.

The greatest of them was named Ali. He was fifteen feet long and heavy as a boulder, with a gaze that could stop a charging boar. He was proclaimed the first king of Ali Land, and he raised its capital in the center of the marsh and called it Ali City. He ruled fifteen others.

Fifteen. In a world where elves counted twelve and dwarves of the swamp counted twelve, fifteen crocodiles was a respectable nation.

And so, for the first time since the dwarves had crossed the mountain, a new civilization had entered the world. It was a civilization of hunters, of carnivores, of beings whose ancestors had eaten anything that moved. The elves and the dwarves, who watched from their respective shores, did not know what the crocodiles would do when they discovered the other peoples of the world. Would they live in harmony? Or would the old primal hunger rise, and eat them all?

It was a question that would take a long time to answer.

Chapter Nineteen: The Return of the Pumpkins

"We are bad at farming. We are very, very bad at it."

(Attributed to King Ali of Ali Land, shortly before his death)

In the Year 572, the pumpkins returned.

Ali, who had tried to teach his people the arts of agriculture, was not a good farmer. Nor were any of his subjects. They planted seeds in the wrong soil and watered them with the wrong water, and they turned the earth with the claws of hunters rather than the patience of growers. The crops sprouted, bent, and twisted. They grew in ugly knots, black-veined and swollen. And one grey morning, the fields of Ali Land came alive.

The monsters rose out of the earth in a screaming tide, great orange horrors with eyes of burning pith. Ali and his fifteen crocodiles fought them with tooth and claw, but pumpkins do not bleed. Ali was struck down in the field he himself had planted. The great king of the crocodiles died with his jaw clamped on a stem, and his people, six in number, fled to the center of the swamp, led by a crocodile named Azer, who took the throne.

And the pumpkins, which would not be stopped, rolled on.

It was then that a stroke of luck, strange and almost comic, saved the swamp. A tornado, an enormous black funnel, dropped out of the clouds and picked up the horde of pumpkins like a child gathering toys. It carried them into the sky, over the river, over the water, and dropped them on the far shore, in the half-colonized jungle on the dwarven mainland.

The pumpkins fell on Metallica like a rain of fruit.

The dwarves of The Stone marched out to meet them. It was a terrible battle. Each monstrous pumpkin was worth four dwarves in strength, and the soldiers of Metallica, who had never fought anything like them, died in the dozens. After a month, they won, but the price was over a hundred dwarves. The jungle fringe was covered in orange gore.

In Aethel, meanwhile, the elves had improved their boats and were stuck at fourteen. Anaconda was growing. And the river, between all the nations, kept flowing.

Chapter Twenty: The Azer Legion

In the Year 582, six peoples sat at the edge of the world, and a seventh was about to be born.

Anaconda had risen to twenty-eight. Aethel had fifteen. Ali Land, to everyone's delight, had risen to seventeen: the crocodiles, whatever else could be said of their farming, were good at breeding.

Yeth, and Grih. The dead king and the living one, joined in a single name. The people of Metallica said nothing. But all of them, to a dwarf, wondered the same thing, whether the old king was finally old enough to feel the weight of everything he had done.

And in Nile, something remarkable had happened. The ruler of the elves was no longer a stranger. The old leader Embera had passed away, and her daughter had taken the crown. Her name was Ember.

She was a patient, clever woman, with a long, sharp gaze, and the habit of making a plan and then saying nothing for years. At her side, a very old elf, ancient beyond memory, who had somehow survived every war and every winter since the fall of Sylvanor. He was Tohil. The traitor. The kin-slayer. The pumpkins did not forget, and neither did the earth.

Chapter Twenty-Two: The Warship Fry

In the Year 611, the first warship in the history of the world was launched, and it fired its first cannon on a people who had never done it any harm.

She was called Fry. The elves of Aethel built her on the muddy docks of Nile, a ship of black timber and green sails, with weapons that no ship had ever carried: heavy bolt-throwers on the prow, and a fire-pot on the stern. Queen Ember watched her launch from the shore, her face a mask of calm. When the ship was ready, the elves tested her.

They tested her on the people of O'Raka.

From the coast, a safe distance, Fry fired her bolts across the water and into the harbor of the northern village. Five dwarves died. There was no warning, no declaration of war. The dwarves ran for cover and the elves watched them run, and the queen said nothing.

It seemed, then, that the world had its own opinion about warships.

Chapter Twenty-Three: The Cats

In the Year 618, a new people opened its eyes in the burning light of the southern jungle.

They were small things at first, only fifteen of them, lean and tawny and silent, on the jungle coast of the dwarven mainland. Their queen was named Cater.

She was a slim, golden cat with a gaze that held the memory of ten thousand hunts. She gathered the others and raised a nation. She called it the Kitten Empire, which made the dwarves of Yethigrih snort when they heard it, and she built a capital called Meow. Chapter Twenty-Four: The Gathering Storm

(Said to have been written on the wall of Cat Town in the last days)

The cats had joined the Azer Legion. The Legion held the swamp, and the swamp held the south, and the south held the only coast from which a fleet could ever threaten Metallica. A map is a very plain thing. It does not care what any of its people wish.

So war was declared, and the web of the alliance, which had been woven in a time of peace, snapped taut.

Bound by the oath of the Azer Legion, the three peoples came to the cats' defense, and thus Gris found herself at war, not only with a nation of eighty cats, but with Ember of Aethel, and Cater of the Kitten Empire, and Raar of Ali Land. It was the most dangerous coalition the dwarves had faced since the days of Sylvas.

In the Year 686, a ghost died.

It was Uncater, the ghost of Queen Cater, who had been the last great shadow of the Kitten Empire. She had haunted the burned jungle for twenty years, a pale, glimmering shape in the blackened stumps, a cat's thin cry on the wind. She had watched the world go on without her. She had waited. And in the Year 686, with a long and weary sigh, she unmade herself.

Her death was not an ending. It was a key.

The ground of the burned jungle trembled. The ash, which had lain undisturbed for twenty years, rose in long, smoking threads. And out of the black earth of Cat Town came four hundred shapes, gaunt, armored, and silent, with the hollow eyes of the dead and the blackened axes of the war that killed them.

They were the dwarves who had burned.

The four hundred soldiers who had died in the fire of Gris's own kindling, in the Year 666, in the jungle. They had lain in the ash of their own pyre for twenty years. Now they were up again, not as ghosts, nor as zombies, but as something stranger and worse. They had been killed by fire, and fire had marked them. They were fireproof. They walked in the flames and were not burned. They could not be struck by the fire-staves of the old mages. They could not be driven out by the burning of the grass.

And wherever they walked, the land died.

Every step they took left a footprint of scorched, glistening, acid-green earth. The jungle, which had grown back in the years since the fire, withered at their approach. The soil turned sour. The streams ran yellow. In their wake there was nothing but a wasteland, and the wasteland was the same land that had given birth to the orcs, so many centuries before.

They were called the Ash-Walkers, and there were four hundred of them, and they were marching on the Rock.

Gris, queen of Metallica, who had won the world in the Year 676 and had held it for ten years, heard the news in her hall and went white. She had two thousand and two hundred dwarves. She had armies, and weapons, and the dim memory of a hundred wars. But she had no way to burn an enemy that could not be burned.

"Can the army stop them in time?" her generals asked.

"No," said Gris. "But we will have to try."

Chapter Thirty: Nedhel at the Pass

In the Year 687, the queen died, and a soldier took her crown.

After forty-five years on the throne of the dwarves, Gris died in her bed in the Rock, as her father had. She was a long-lived queen, with the cold eyes of a general and the unbending will of a ruler who knew what the world cost. She had burned a jungle. She had broken the elven queen. She had given orders that sent four hundred of her own to their deaths. In her last hour, it was said, she asked only that the window be opened, so that she might hear the wind.

The wind, that night, smelled of ash.

Her successor was a warrior named Nedhel. He was not of the royal blood. He was a general, a soldier, a hard, square, silent dwarf with a scar through one eyebrow and a habit of standing very still when others panicked. He had been a part of the fleet that sailed to Nile in the Year 675, one of the hundred and fifty on Biten and Bita, and he had seen, with his own eyes, what a clever enemy and a poor position could do to an army. He was perhaps the one person in all of Metallica who knew that an army is only as good as the ground it stands on.

He took the throne in the first week of the Year 687. He studied the maps for three nights. And on the fourth morning he gave an order that surprised the entire court.

"We will not meet them in the open," said King Nedhel.

The jungle was gone. Meow, the old cat capital, and Cat Town, rebuilt in the years since, were destroyed, eaten by the acid. The trees of the southern jungle were dead. The toxic wasteland spread north each day, a creeping wave of poisoned earth, and if the Ash-Walkers reached the farms of the Stone and the Rock, Metallica would starve whatever happened in the battle.

But there was a place, in the mountains, where the world narrowed. A single pass, between two walls of black granite, so tight that a man with outstretched arms could touch both sides. A bottleneck. The ancient passage that the dwarves of the old days had sealed, and that had cracked open in the Year 253 when the stars fell.

Nedhel moved the whole of Metallica's archers into the cliffs.

In the Year 690, the Ash-Walkers came to the pass.

They came as they always did, in silence, trailing ruin. Their blackened axes were notched, their armor pitted and rusted. They did not have to fear fire. They did not have to fear death. They could not be burned out of the pass, nor could they be starved. They marched straight into the gap between the cliffs, forced by the walls into single file, and the dwarves above, hidden among the rocks, drew their bows.

The first volley fell like a black rain. The arrows were heavy, tipped with the hammered iron of the Stone. They did not pierce the Ash-Walkers' armor, but they shattered their bones. A dwarven arrow does not kill a skeleton. It breaks it. The marching line staggered, tangled, fell apart. And the next volley fell, and the next.

It was not a battle. It was a harvest.

Row after row the skeletons advanced into the pass, in the narrow darkness, forced into rows by the rock, and the dwarven archers picked them off one by one. The Ash-Walkers lost their numbers in the dark. The dwarves lost fifty, in all, to the acid and the occasional axe that came too close. Four hundred became two hundred. Two hundred became a hundred and fifty.

"Doesn't feel like much of a threat," said a young archer, to no one in particular.

"No," said Nedhel, from the cliff-top. "It never does."

Chapter Thirty-One: The Madness and the Tremor

The world had one more twist in it. The world always did.

In the Year 696, the dwarves of The Rock went mad.

No one has ever explained it. Perhaps it was the strain of the war. Perhaps it was the poison of the ash on the wind, or the memory of the Year 611, when dwarf had slaughtered dwarf for no reason anyone could name. Perhaps it was merely the weight of seven hundred years of history, settling on a people who were not made to carry it. In the middle of a cold spring night, in the streets of the capital, a quarrel broke out. A blade was drawn. And then another.

By morning the streets of the Rock were slick with blood. Brothers had killed brothers. Husbands had killed wives. Fathers had struck at sons with the same hammers that had broken the skeletons in the pass. It lasted three days. When it was over, the Rock was quiet, and the dwarves who survived could not meet each other's eyes.

In the Year 697, the earth shook.

It was a dreadful quake, centered under the Stone, and it ran through the whole mountain like a shudder through a great animal. Houses split. Walls fell. The granite roads that the dwarves had laid for centuries cracked like dry mud. The empire's granaries, its smithies, its storehouses, were flattened. The logistics of a nation went to pieces in an hour.

The empire reeled. Between the madness and the earthquake, the dwarves of Metallica fell, in the Year 699, from two thousand and two hundred to eighteen hundred. Four hundred souls had died by their own hands, or by the hand of the ground itself, without the Ash-Walkers firing a single shot.

And yet the pass held.

That was the great and terrible irony of the war. The Ash-Walkers had not broken Metallica. Metallica had broken itself. But the narrow road between the cliffs had not been touched. Behind it, the archers still stood on their cliffs, and every day, they loosed their arrows into the dark.

By the Year 699, only forty Ash-Walkers remained. Three hundred and sixty of them had been pulverized into dust, a pathetic remnant shuffling blind and slow into a wall of arrows that would not stop.

"It is almost done," said King Nedhel. "We only need to be patient."

And it was a long patience. It lasted fourteen years.

Chapter Thirty-Two: The Last Skeleton

"When you have been dead for so long, you can't be killed twice. But you can be finished."

The years after the great madness were quiet. Quieter than anyone had dared hope.

In the Year 700, the warship Fry 2, the old elven vessel that had survived two centuries of storms and sieges, was retired. Her timbers were salvaged, her figurehead carved into a small statue that stood, for many years, on the docks of Nile. In the Year 710, a surge of madness, like a ghost of the old plague, swept through the streets of Anaconda. It was brief, a single night of cold sweats and shaking hands. And this time, the dwarves were ready. They had learned. The madness was suppressed before a single life was lost.

In the Year 713, the last of the Ash-Walkers was killed.

It was the final skeleton of the original four hundred, a thing of bone and rust, still dragging its notched axe along the stones of the narrow pass. It had not known fear. It had not known anger. It had not even known why it was walking. It had only known that there was a place it should go, and a thing it should do when it got there.

An archer on the cliff, a young woman who had not been born when the war began, drew her bow and loosed a single arrow. It struck the skeleton in the center of the chest. For a moment the creature stood, swaying, and then it came apart, bone by bone, falling into a small pile of white dust on the black stone.

The pass was silent.

No one cheered. They only stood there, on the cliffs, in the grey morning, and looked at the little pile of dust. After twenty-seven years, the Ash-Walkers were gone. The dwarves, who had suffered every kind of horror that the world had to offer, were finally free.

They had recovered. Metallica stood at two thousand again. The farms of the Rock and the Stone were green. The scars of the acid wasteland were slowly healing, the jungle creeping back over the dead earth as it had after the Age of Despair, as it always would.

In the Year 714, the great ship Biten, the proud iron flagship that had carried the dwarves to the swamp and the fall of Aethel, sprang a leak in a calm sea and sank. It went down in a great slow gyre of foam and rust. No one was killed. It was the last ship of the great war.

Chapter Thirty-Three: Gri Ali

"It is a very small price to pay, for being allowed to live."

(Attributed to the crocodile-queen of Ali Land, on the renaming of her city)

The Year 725 dawned over a different world.

It was a world with no cats. They had gone in the fire of Cat Town, in the long reign of the Ash-Walkers, in the quiet, decisive hand of Gris and the poison of the ash. The last of them had died, one by one, in the years after the great war, their whispering voices fading from the jungle. The Kitten Empire, born in the Year 618, was a story that dwarven grandmothers told their grandchildren on winter nights.

It was a world with no humans. They had gone long before, in the Year 472, with Aria and her one-woman empire of Ense, and in all the long years since nobody had seen so much as a human footprint.

It was a world with no orcs, and no dragons, and no necromancers. They, too, were gone.

But it was a world with crocodiles. In the Year 725, ninety-three of them lived in the marshes of the south. They had boomed, in the years since the great war ended, bred like rabbits in the warm mud. They had built a new city on the ruins of the old one. They had learned to farm, a little, and to fish, a great deal. And they had learned to be pragmatic.

When the dwarves of Metallica, in the Year 725, proposed to rename their capital as a gesture of goodwill, the crocodiles accepted. Ali City, the city of King Ali, who had been killed by the pumpkins, was renamed Gri Ali. It was a name that blended the two peoples, the name of the first dwarf king of the old line, Gri, who would later be called Grih, and the name of the first crocodile king.

It was a small thing. A name. But the crocodiles understood it perfectly. It was a very easy price to pay for the right to live, to build, and to breed in the shadow of the greatest empire in the world.

And it was a world with elves.

There were thirty-three of them, in the Year 725. Thirty-three, up from the fifteen who had stood in the ruins of Nile in the days of Gris, and the thirty-three were more than any elven people had been in two hundred and fifty years. They lived in Nile, on the river, in houses of wood and clay. They fished. They sang. They remembered, quietly and without bitterness, the old green songs.

And the ruler of Nile, in that year, was not a dwarf.

His name was Carthen. He was an elf, tall and grey-eyed, and thoughtful, and a good fisherman. He was the husband of a woman named Helen. Helen, the dwarven soldier who had burned the great warship Yeth 1, who had killed the queen Ember with her own hand and been given a medal and a house for it, had never used the house. She had gone back to a fisherman's hut. She had stayed. She had borne her husband a daughter, small and dark-haired, with a dwarf's thick hands and an elf's long gaze.

The dwarves of the Rock thought it was strange. The elves of Nile thought it was stranger. But the new King of the Dwarves, Nedhel, who had faced the Ash-Walkers in the pass and learned that no victory is ever final, shrugged and let it be.

It was a fragile thing, that peace. It was a pragmatic peace, an arrangement of survivors, as unlikely and as stubborn as a weed in a crack in the stone. The dwarves held the mainland. The crocodiles held the marsh. The elves held the river. Nobody loved anybody. Everybody lived.

Epilogue: The Quill Set Down

And above the world the two Watchers looked at what they had made, and, for the first time, neither of them spoke.

It had been seven hundred and twenty-five years.

They had begun with a mountain, and a wood, and a jungle. They had let the sun rise on a king named Sylvas and a king named Baron. They had broken the wall, and watched the elves win, and watched the elves lose. They had watched dwarves climb out of the stone, and orcs rise from the poisoned earth. They had watched pumpkins rise from soil that remembered, and snow fall for forty years, and a dragon wake in the north. They had watched a man named Tohil kill the only king he ever swore to protect, and a queen named Ember run through a swamp. They had watched a farmer-king rule for three hundred and forty-eight years. They had watched a cat become a ghost, and a ghost become an army, and the army fall, one skeleton at a time, in a narrow pass.

They had watched the humans go, and the cats go, and the orcs go. They had watched the world shrink to a river, a marsh, and a mountain.

And they had watched, in the end, not the death of a people but the quiet, difficult, improbable birth of a bargain.

"Is it over?" asked the first Watcher.

"For now," said the second. "This chapter is closed."

"And the next?"

The second Watcher looked down at the small world, glowing in the dark like a coin dropped in a deep well. A dwarven child, in the Rock, was asking her mother why the sky was red. A crocodile, in Gri Ali, was teaching its hatchlings to swim. An elven woman, in Nile, was singing to a baby who had a dwarf's thick hands and an elf's long gaze.

"It will write itself," said the second Watcher. "It always does."

They put down the quill.

The year was seven hundred and twenty-five, and the sun was rising over the world.

It was the same sun that had risen on the first day. It lit the green of the Enchanted Wood that was no longer there, and the blue of the river that had split the world, and the black granite of the Rock, and the grey mud of the swamp where the crocodiles slept. It lit the bones of kings and the ashes of cities, and the little hut by the river where an elf and a dwarf woke up together and listened to the wind.

It lit everything.

It had always lit everything.

And the story, which had begun with a mountain, and a wood, and a jungle, ended, as all true stories end, not with a victory, but with morning.
