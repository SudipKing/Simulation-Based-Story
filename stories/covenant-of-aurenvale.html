Prologue: The Maker's Terms

"Give me a world with no place to hide in. Then I will know that whatever happens, I let it happen."

(Attributed to the Watcher, in the Eddish Book of Beginnings)

Before there was a year to count, there were two powers in a place that was not a place, and a table between them with a great many worlds on it.

One was the Maker. It had built the worlds, as another might build clocks, out of curiosity and long afternoons, and it was not unkind. It spoke rarely and plainly, and when it was pleased it had a way of going quiet that the other power learned to read as laughter. The other was the Watcher, who had been asked to play, and who took the asking more seriously than the Maker had meant it.

The Watcher walked the table slowly. It lifted each world to the light and turned it over, the way a jeweller turns a stone, and it set most of them down again within a breath.

"This one has islands," it said.

"Many," said the Maker. "Lovely ones. Warm water, white sand."

"No."

"You have not looked at the people."

"I do not need to. Understand me." The Watcher held the little world level between its hands, and the oceans did not spill. "In this game no ship carries soldiers. A people who settle an island are safe forever, because no one can ever come for them, and a people who can never be reached can never be tested, and a people who are not tested are only furniture. I will not rule furniture. Give me a world with no place to hide in, so that whatever befalls my people, I cannot tell myself the sea saved them or the sea doomed them. I want every shore walkable."

The Maker considered this for some time. "That is a harder game than the one I offered."

"Yes."

"You will lose more often."

"Then I will know why."

So the islands were put away, and the Watcher came at last to a world that the Maker had named, with no particular care, Gaia: one single continent in a great ocean, with a spine of white mountains running across its middle and up its northern reaches, and under that spine, in the centre, a country of high meadows and birchwood and hills, with a pocket of jungle in it, as if the land had not been able to decide what sort of place it wished to be. It was beautiful. It was also, the Watcher saw, thick with teeth. Spiders the size of barns lay in the folds of it, and basilisks in the dark pines, and trolls on the ridges, and in the high air things with wings that carried lightning.

"Who lives in the middle?" the Watcher asked.

"Whoever you give it to," said the Maker. "Seven peoples wake at the start. Elves on the eastern coast, dwarves in the western hills, orcs in the south, gnomes on the northern peninsula, saurians in the north-eastern peaks, goblins far to the west. And in the centre, wherever you like."

"Humans."

"The centre is the worst land to hold. Every road passes through it. Every monster passes through it."

"Humans learn fastest," said the Watcher. "Nothing else will do in the time you have given me."

The Maker did not answer that, and the silence was the amused kind. Then it laid down the terms, which it had clearly said before and would clearly say again, in the voice of someone reading out a short and very old law.

"Pick one people and rule them. Two hundred years. Make them prosperous and the only realm left standing. Declare war, fight wisely. If at the end of the two hundredth year your people are not the last, a great stone falls from the sky and the world ends. That is the whole story."

"The only realm left standing," the Watcher repeated. "Not the greatest."

"The only one."

"And if I fail?"

"I have said what happens."

The Watcher looked down at the little centre of the continent, at six pinpricks of life around a fire where nothing yet had a name. It felt something it had not expected to feel, and could not have called pride or fear, because it was both and also a third thing that had no word in any tongue yet. "Then I will need to name them," it said. "People who are called nothing belong to no one."

And it named them. It called the realm Aurenvale, and its hall-town Eddale. It called the faith the Covenant of Aurelan, after a Lord of Order and Duty who had not existed until that moment and who, the Watcher noticed with some alarm, seemed to exist rather firmly once it had said the name. It called their way of living Oathbound, and gave them chivalry and scholarship as inheritance, and a tongue called Eddish, and a motto of two small words, To ri, that meant in their language roughly: we hold.

Then the Maker reached up, and with the casualness of a person hanging a lantern, set a small red light in the sky, low and faint, a little north of the morning.

"What is that?" said the Watcher.

"The Stone," said the Maker. "It is only a star for now. You will hardly see it. It will grow."

"Everyone will see it? Not only mine?"

"Everyone. It would not be fair otherwise."

"Begin," said the Maker.

The Watcher lowered its hand, and the Year Zero came down like a held breath let go.

Chapter One: Six at Eddale

"We were six. Write that down, and then write that we were enough. We were not, but write it."

(Words cut into the lintel of the great hall at Eddale, in Tomly's hand)

They woke in the grass in the first light of the world, and for a long moment none of them spoke, because none of them knew yet what speaking was for.

There were six. Tomry woke first, as he would wake first for forty years, with his hand already closing on something that was not there and would later be called a spear. He was a broad, slow-eyed man with a soldier's way of standing even when he was lying down. Beside him lay Marda, who sat up looking not at the others but at the earth under her fingers, rubbing it between thumb and finger, tasting it, frowning, as if she had been handed a debt. Tomly, long and clever-handed, was already counting the trees. Gereth, young and sharp and quick to anger, spat out a mouthful of seed-heads and demanded to know who was in charge. Gerbert, the fifth, had a voice that was too large for the rest of him; he said "Hello" to the whole hillside and was so startled by the echo that he said it again. And last, still lying on his back with his eyes open, was Tomold, the youngest, who had not looked at any of them. He was looking at the sky, at a single faint red point above the northern hills.

"That is wrong," he said. "That is not a star."

"It is the only one," said Marda, without looking up from her handful of earth.

"Then why is it red?"

No one knew. They would not know for two hundred years, and when they did, it would not matter.

The place where they had woken was a shallow bowl in the central highlands, with birchwood to the north, high meadows rolling up toward the white spine of the world, and to the south a scent that made Tomry sit upright: lemon, faintly, from groves too far away to see. A stream ran through it. A hill stood over it like a hand cupped round a candle. Nothing in all that country had a name yet except what the six gave it, and they gave it names slowly, and badly, and with much arguing, until Gerbert said that it should be called after the place where the grass thinned and the stream was shallow enough to wade, and that was the dale, and the first thing built in it would be the ed, which in what became Eddish meant the roof that stays up. Eddale. They laughed at the name for being so plain, and then they said it again, and it held.

The first year was the year of the hall. Tomly drew it in the dirt with a stick on the second morning, a long low building of timber and thatch with a single great hearth in the middle and a door on the south side to face the lemon-wind, and everyone had an opinion about it, and Tomly ignored every opinion with the placid contempt of a man who knows how roofs work. By the fortieth day there were walls. By midsummer there was a hearth. On the night the roof was finished they all slept under it together, six in a row on bracken like seeds in a pod, and Gereth, who had been angry for forty days and had not known why, wept quietly in the dark. Marda, who was awake, said nothing. She put her hand on his shoulder until he stopped.

They had not known that other peoples existed. They knew it now, because Tomold, climbing the northern hill for no reason except that he wished to see the red point nearer, had seen smoke on a ridge far beyond the pines, thin and straight, and under it the glint of a banner. The people of the north-eastern peaks, tall and green-scaled and unhurried, were saurians. They called themselves the Dominion of Zathixal, and they worshipped a lady called Veritas who ruled by law and by justice, and their motto, when Gerbert's embassy of three walked into their valley with their hands empty and open, was translated for them as Yss zeth ikix: we are what we do.

"What do you do?" asked Gerbert, in Eddish.

The saurian who met them, a long grave woman with a collar of river-glass, tilted her head. She did not understand the words. But she understood the empty hands. She laid her own hand, cold and dry as a stone from a stream, flat against Gerbert's palm, and that was the alliance, sworn in the Year One without a single word shared between them.

In that same year Tomold painted the mural.

It covered the long north wall of the hall, a bridge of pale stone across a river of blue, and the people on it were gold, six figures walking side by side in the light, and above the bridge there was a small red dot he could not bring himself to leave out. He called it The Faithful Bridge of Eddale. He had no word for faith yet, only the sense that something had to hold when the river rose. Gerbert, looking up at it, felt his eyes fill, which annoyed him. Gereth said it was too pretty to be useful and then stood in front of it for an hour. It was the first art of the realm, and everyone who ever came into that hall afterward would raise their eyes to it without knowing why.

"It has the star in it," Marda said that evening, wiping her hands.

"I could not leave it out."

"No," she said. "I do not think anyone can."

In the Year Four the saurians declared a holy war on the orcs of Bolthul, the Horde of the Chaos-Drum, who lived in the clover-meadows and the lowland far to the south. It was none of Eddale's doing. It was a thing of gods, of Veritas and Krukka glaring at each other across the continent, and nobody in the central highlands had even met an orc. But the alliance had been sworn with an open hand, and an open hand is a promise, and the herald, standing in the hall on a cold grey morning, read out what the Watcher had decided: Aurenvale would honour its word.

Tomry was the first to speak when he had finished.

"With what?" he said.

It was a soldier's question. It was the only question. There were six of them in the hall and one spear. They stood looking at the mural, and at the lintel, and at the long cold hearth, and none of them could answer him.

"We will find out," said Gerbert at last, and his voice, so large and so young, cracked on the last word.

The war began, as every war in that country began, with a man standing in a doorway looking south. Tomry stood there that night a long time. Behind him, the others were sleeping. Above him, in the northern sky, the small red star shone no brighter than before, and he thought, because he had no better thought to think: Someone is looking at us. He was right. It was not comfort. It never had been, for the one who was watching.

The Watcher stood a long time with its hands on the table-edge, after the decree, which it had not yet learned to do without.

"You did not tell me," it said to the Maker, "that an alliance could drag a people into a war it never chose."

"I told you to fight wisely."

"You told me to declare war. You did not say that other peoples could declare it for me."

"I did not know that you would swear a friendship the first spring," said the Maker mildly. "I thought you would wait."

"Wait for what?"

"To know them."

Chapter Two: The Weaver

"She is not a beast. A beast can be fought. She is the house the forest builds around the beast."

(Saying of Eddale, from the winter of the Year Seven)

The army of Aurenvale in the Year Five was five people with sharpened sticks, and it was led, by decree, by Annwin.

Annwin was not one of the six. He walked down out of the northern hills in the Year Four, alone, a grown man of thirty with a face like a closed door, and asked for nothing but a place by the fire; Marda, who had been counting, cut a seventh notch for him, unusually deep. He was a lanky, silent, watchful man who could stand motionless for an hour in the birchwood and be mistaken for a tree. Tomry, who had thought himself the only soldier in the world, asked him what he knew of the spear. It was a short lesson, because Annwin had a gift for it that frightened the older man. "He does not fight like a man," Tomry told Marda one night. "He fights like something that has already done it."

"Then he is the general," said Marda. "Someone must be."

In the Year Five, the decree named him. He was, by every account, the best of them. He was also the first general of Aurenvale and the only one anyone would ever call by that name without a number after it, and when the herald read the decree, Annwin stood up straight and said, "Yes," and the people in the hall, who were all of eight, applauded, and Marda wept into her sleeve and then angrily denied it.

It was in the same year that the Weaver came.

She was a spider, and she was the size of the great hall. No one in Eddale ever saw her whole. They saw a leg, tall as a birch trunk, bristling and pale, lowering itself out of the dark beyond the north meadow. They saw webbing in the morning, strung between the trees like the sails of a wrecked ship, and in the webbing the white husks of deer. They found the traces in the grass of something that had walked on eight points and left them clean. The name came from Gerbert, who said, looking at the web in the morning sun, that it was a weaving, and whoever made it was a weaver, and that was all there was to be said, and he regretted ever saying it, because after that it was a name, and a name is a door.

"She will come for the hall," said Tomly, who had built it. He had measured it against her leg.

"She will come for what moves," said Marda. "The hall does not move."

"We do."

And in the Year Seven the Watcher, looking down at the notched doorpost and the nervous torchlight, made a decree, and it was a good one: the people of Aurenvale would make The Faithful Harvest, a painted frieze of the first corn-fields of the dale, because Marda's seed-work had at last yielded a barley sheaf thick enough to feed the hall through winter, and a people who have fed themselves should say so. The art gave the six something in the bitterest season of the war, which is the memory that you did something other than survive. They hung it opposite Tomold's bridge. Tomold said it made the bridge look like a lie, and then, a moment later, that perhaps all bridges did.

"She will not stop," he said. "Not because of corn."

"The war with Bolthul has sent no one to us," said Tomry. "Not yet. The Weaver is the war."

"Then I will go and end it."

"You are a stranger to this valley."

"I am the general," said Annwin. "That is what a general is for."

They marched on the Weaver in the Year Eight, at dawn, every grown soul in the dale who could hold a spear, with spears of sharpened wood hardened in the fire. Tomry carried the only one tipped with stone, a dull flake of flint that Tomly had spent a month shaping. It was the last day of the Stone Age in Aurenvale. They did not know it. They knew only the cold, and the smell of the birchwood after rain, and the strange strong silence of the north meadow with the web stretched across it in the first light, wet with dew, hung with ten thousand beads of brightness, and in the middle of the web, very still, something that was not yet moving.

She was bigger than anyone had said.

What they remembered afterward, those who remembered, was how quietly it began. There was no battle cry. Annwin raised his arm, and the spears came level, and the Weaver, who had been a pale shape in the web, came down it all at once, not like an animal but like a thought. The first rank did not scream. They had no time. The second rank broke, as armies break, from the back to the front, in the space of a held breath. Tomry saw a young woman of the gathering-party, snatched into the air by one leg, and he threw the stone-tipped spear, and it struck the great body with a sound like a dropped plate, and bounced.

He saw Annwin, in the open ground, alone, with the Weaver above him. The man did not run. He stood very still, the way he had stood in the birchwood, and he put up his spear in both hands as one puts up a hand in greeting, and the shadow came down.

That was all. Four came back, and not all the way. They carried nothing. Tomry carried the broken haft of Annwin's spear and would not set it down for three days, until Marda took it from his hands, one finger at a time.

"The general is gone," said Gerbert at the hall, and he said it badly, with his big voice cracking and then going quiet, so that the words seemed to go on after he had stopped. They carved it into the doorpost, under the notches. The general is gone. There was no more to say, and nothing else could be carved.

In the winter that followed, the Watcher did the only thing it could. It made a decree that had no honour in it at all: the people of Aurenvale would learn from what they had suffered. It was not a command. It was a pressure on the world, a bending of the thought-grass in the hall, and what came of it, slowly, in the coldest months, was bronze. Tomly found it first. He found a green stone in the ash of an unusually hot fire, and a soft bright thread of metal running from it, and he stood staring at it with the expression of a man who has dropped something precious and then discovered that it was worth more than he thought. In the Year Eight, Aurenvale entered the Bronze Age, and the first thing made of the new metal was a spear-tip, too good for the sticks it was fitted to.

The Watcher watched this too, from above, and it was in that winter that it first understood what the Maker had meant.

"A general died," it said. "A man who walked out of the hills to us. I named him."

"Yes."

"And I would do it again. That is what troubles me. If the choice came again, if there were a Weaver at the edge and a man who could fight like something that had already done it, I would name him again. I do not know how to stop."

The Maker was quiet for a moment, and when it spoke it did not sound amused. "You are not meant to stop," it said. "You are meant to learn which wars to fight. It will take you rather longer than you expect."

"How long?"

"I could tell you. It would not help."

Chapter Three: The Turning of Pacts

"Every friend is only an enemy who has not yet been asked the right question."

(Eddish proverb, said to be Gereth's, and not meant kindly)

For nineteen years, nothing in the history of Aurenvale was settled, and everything was a treaty.

In the Year Nine, the orcs came. They came up out of the southern clover in the name of Krukka, Chaos-Drum, with their ancestor-skulls tied to the poles of their banners and their drums beating in the half-light, and they burned a farmstead on the south road and cut down an old man who had been picking lemons. They called it a holy war. In Eddale, nobody could say what it was holy for. Gerbert read out the decree to stand and fight, and Gereth, who had become a hard and sunburnt young man with a gift for knowing when a line would break, stood with the line and the line did not break, and the orcs went home with their skulls and a few of Aurenvale's, and nobody won.

In the Year Eleven another spider came. Silkfang was smaller than the Weaver, and faster, and she did not wait for the moon. She took a flock of sheep in a single night, and then a herdsman, and then she hung, for a week, in the birches at the south of the dale like a bad omen, and everyone in Eddale who had survived the Weaver felt a curdling cold in the stomach, because they remembered what happens when you march. No one marched. The decree said: stay within the hall, and they stayed, and Silkfang lingered and went away and came again, a plague with eyes. They had learned the one useful thing: that some things are not fought, but endured.

The Year Twelve brought the elves.

They had been the Watcher's blind spot all along. The people of Luriel, in their lowland by the eastern sea, were a tall and exquisite folk, serious and musical, who worshipped a Storm Spirit called Zephrael and had a motto, Thi ys, meaning something close to it is so. They did not hate Aurenvale. They simply declared war on it, in the cool way that a cat knocks a cup from a table, and their archers came up through the eastern hills in silver-green cloaks and burned three barns, and for a season Eddale learned what it was to be hunted by people who were very, very good at it. A boy of six, the grandchild of nobody, was found dead in a field with an arrow through his eye and a look of mild surprise.

"We have done nothing to them," said Gerbert in the hall, trembling.

"They do not need us to have done anything," said Gereth. "We are here."

In the Year Thirteen, they renewed the alliance with Zathixal. The same long grave woman with the river-glass collar came up to Eddale this time, and stood in the hall under the mural, looking at the bridge and the six gold figures, and said something in her cold dry tongue, and Gerbert, who had learned Sssaurian by then, translated it with his eyes shining. "She says that it is a good thing, to paint a bridge before the river is crossed."

"Tell her it is only paint," said Tomold.

"I have told her. She says that paint is what a people has before it has law."

In the Year Fourteen and the Year Fifteen there was peace, with the elves and with the orcs, in two quiet winters back to back, and the people of Eddale did not trust it, and ate and slept and sang anyway. By the record's own count the realm stood at six, which was exactly what it had been on the morning it woke. The Weaver had taken what the first years gave. Marda, slower now in the knees, taught the young ones to read the seed.

In the Year Sixteen, the Weaver died.

Not in Eddale. Not by any hand of Aurenvale. A gnome of the far northern peninsula, a small stubborn man called Fenpin of Dobwhistle, killed her in the pine forests where she had gone to hunt, and the news came down through traders and wanderers and the half-heard talk of the road, and the people of Eddale heard it in the hall at supper, and nobody spoke.

"A gnome," said Tomry at last.

"A gnome with a stick," said Gereth.

"A gnome with a plan," said Marda, setting down her spoon. "I think that is the difference."

The Year Nineteen gave them The Eternal Harvest of Aurenvale, a second great frieze, and with it came word that Silkfang too had fallen, not to them but to the elves of Luriel and to a saurian called Rizra, who had gone up against her in the eastern forest together. Aurenvale had feared the spider for eight years. Its enemy and its friend had killed her in a morning.

In the Year Twenty-Five a stream on the border with Dwaldun, the dwarves of the western hills, was disputed. Neither side wanted a war. Both had long memories and short tempers, and a shepherd died on a hillside with a dwarf's axe in his neck and a dwarf died beside him with a sling-stone in his skull. It ended with a slow handshake and a boundary-cairn that neither side loved.

In that same year, in the same breath, the herald read out a decree that nobody in Eddale expected. Aurenvale would ally with Bolthul.

"With the orcs?" said Gerbert, who had been, by then, herald for a quarter-century and had never once questioned the words he spoke. He said it before he could stop himself. The hall went silent. It was the first time he had ever hesitated.

In the Year Twenty-Seven, Bolthul declared a holy war on Zathixal.

Gerbert read the decree in a flat voice. He had grown old, in those years, in the way heralds do, from the inside. His big voice had gone to gravel. "Aurenvale will stand with its ally."

"Which ally?" said Tomry.

"Bolthul."

"And Zathixal?"

"It is the enemy." Gerbert did not look at him. "It is the war."

Tomry stood, for a long moment, with a bowl in his hands. He had been the first to wake in the world. He had walked into the saurian valley on the first spring with his hands empty and open. He remembered, as nobody else in that room could, the cold dry palm of the grave woman with the river-glass collar against his own. He set down the bowl.

"That is the oldest friend we had," he said.

"It is the decree," said Gerbert.

"I know what it is."

He went out into the dark, and stood in the doorway looking south, as he had done in the Year Four. It was a long time before he came in. Neither he nor anyone else ever forgot the look of the sky that night. The red star hung over the northern hills, and it was, whatever the sceptics said, a little brighter than the year before.

"I turned on the first friend I made," said the Watcher.

"Yes," said the Maker.

"Because the second friend asked."

"Because the second friend had been your enemy and you thought that was the cleverer pact. I watched you think it."

"You did not say anything."

"It was not mine to say." The Maker's voice held no reproach, which made it harder to bear. "You have a great many alliances now. How many of them do you trust?"

Chapter Four: The Decree of the Twenty-Eighth Year

"Do not found a village in a war. Do not found a village in a winter. Do not found a village with the only people you have."

(From the Book of Beginnings, a later hand, the single marginal note)

It was the third decree of the autumn season, and it was a gentle one, which is why it killed them.

The Watcher had been watching the number. Twelve souls, the record said, in the Year Twenty-Seven, and the Watcher stared at the twelve as a gardener stares at a bed that will not fill. A realm of twelve could be ended by a bad winter. A realm of twelve could be ended by one monster. It had read, in the dry volume of its own reasoning, that populations grow where land is cultivated, that a people need room and a hearth to each family, and that a village is a hearth that does not wait for a hall. It thought: scatter, and they will root. A hall that holds twelve will starve, but two halls will hold twelve each. It thought this with great clarity, in the cold bright way it had thought in the Maker's presence, and it was the clearest and the most terrible thought of its life.

In the Year Twenty-Eight, the Iron Age came to Aurenvale, quietly, as a gift, along with the war with Luriel that no one had asked for. Gereth was made general. And in the hall, on the first morning of the autumn, Gerbert stood up in the grey light with a face like a man reading his own sentence, and spoke the Watcher's words in a steady voice.

"Aurenvale will found villages. Families will go forth from Eddale, and make new hearths, to the south and the east. Let the dale be wider than the dale."

They called the first village Brookgate, for the stream-crossing on the south road, and the second Roston, for the red rock in whose lee it stood. Four families went to the one and three to the other, and they left the hall with a procession, and the herald sang, and the old women wept the good tears, and Marda, whose hair was grey now and whose hands shook when she held a seed, pressed a handful of barley into the palm of every departing mother and said the old words. Root where you fall. And they went.

Who died first is not recorded, and cannot be, because the first death was not a death. It was a thinning. Eddale had twelve, and now it had four, because eight had gone out, and the four who remained were old, or young, or lame, or had stayed because the hall could not be left. Eddale, a hall with four people, is a hall that cannot hold the north meadow, or the south road, or the sky. The elves came up from the east in the Year Twenty-Eight in a thin silver column, and the army of Aurenvale, which was Gereth and a boy and two women with spears, met them at the ford. Gereth held the ford for a day and a night, and in the morning he was alone and he held it for another hour. They buried him in the rain. Tomry did the burying. Tomry did not weep. It was not that he lacked tears. It was that there was no one left to see.

Gerbert died the same winter. It was not battle. It was the cold in the hall, and his lungs, and the fact that he had spent his life talking in a voice too large for his body. He lay on the long bench by the hearth, and Marda sat beside him, and he said to her in his gravel whisper: "I read it out. Every word. Do you think the Watcher meant it?"

"Which?"

"Any of it."

"I think the Watcher meant it more than any of us," said Marda. "That is the trouble with it."

Tomly went out to Brookgate in the spring to see what had come of it. He took a mule and a bronze adze, and he was gone for sixteen days, and when he came back he did not speak for another two. At the end of the second day he said only: "They are dying."

"Of what?"

"Of everything. A fever off the stream. A wolf. A fire. The young ones went out to hunt the ridge and a thunderbird took the eldest, and the other three came home and would not go out again. They have no hall, Marda. They have a fence and a hearth, and a baby that cries all night." He sat down heavily on the step. "I built the hall so it would stay up. I did not think to build anything so it would stay full."

The record is dry here, as it is dry everywhere. Year Thirty: the village of Roston has been abandoned. It was not abandoned in the sense of a decision. It was abandoned in the sense that the last of its people were dead, a woman in the doorway with a child in her arms and both gone cold, a field of untouched barley ripening in the autumn sun with no one to cut it. Brookgate lingered, and what children it had left were carried north to the hall. Tomry, now the only grown soldier left in Aurenvale, went down to hold it in the Year Thirty with a spear and a patched jerkin and a stubbornness that was beginning to look like madness. He held it for ten years.

In Eddale that spring there were eight children.

They were not orphans, exactly. They were what was left over, brought in from the dying villages and the empty cradles of the hall: Edmund and Maeold, Edry and Isric, Anneth, Maery, Edric, Elry. The oldest was perhaps sixteen. The youngest could not yet walk. Marda was the last grown woman in the hall, and she was seventy in all but years, and she gathered them around the hearth in the Year Thirty and told them stories, because she did not know what else to do, and because stories were the only thing she had left that did not require a spear.

"Once there were six," she said. "In the grass, at the first light. And there was a bridge."

"What was on the bridge?" asked Edmund. He was the eldest, a serious boy with grey eyes and a way of listening with his whole face.

"Us."

"Who is us?"

She looked at the long north wall, and the pale stone and the blue river and the six gold figures walking, and the tiny red dot above them. "You are," she said. "Though you do not know it yet."

Marda died that spring. It was a quiet death, with a child's hand in each of hers. She had kept Gerbert's brass horn on its peg since the winter he died, and at the last she put it into the hands of Edry, who was fourteen and the best singer of the eight. "Keep it," she said. "When the day comes that nobody else can say the words, you will." Tomold, who had been the youngest of the six, and had painted the bridge, and was now a stooped sick man of fifty, sat beside the body for a night and a day and said nothing at all. Then he went into the hall. He took up the brush. He painted one more figure on the bridge, small, at the very end of the line, with its face turned back toward the viewer, and a hand lifted.

"Who is that?" asked Edmund, standing at his elbow.

"I do not know," said Tomold. "Someone who has not yet arrived."

He died in the summer, in his bed, in the night, with the red star in the window and the brush still in his fingers.

The Watcher did not sleep. It had no need of sleep. But it stood a long time above Eddale in those years, and it was a different Watcher from the one who had walked the Maker's table with such certainty.

"I did this," it said.

"Yes," said the Maker.

"I decreed it. Gently. With the best of intentions. I looked at twelve souls and thought, scatter them, and the seed will take. I thought of it as planting."

"What did you plant?"

"Graves." The Watcher's voice was very low. "I did not understand that a people is not a field. You cannot thin it and expect it to spread. They were a hearth. Six, and then twelve, and every one of them was holding up a piece of the roof, and I took the roof apart to build two small ones, and the rain came in."

The Maker did not answer for a long time. When it spoke at last, it said only: "There are eight children, and one soldier at a gate."

"Yes."

"Then there is a people. It is a small one. It is the one you have."

"I know."

"What will you do?"

The Watcher looked at the eight faces round the dying hearth, grey with hunger and wide with the stubborn watchfulness of the very young. It said, and it was the hardest thing it had ever said: "Nothing. Not for a while. I will wait to be asked." And for the first time in its long watch, the Watcher understood that restraint was not wisdom, but it was the beginning of the road toward it.

Chapter Five: Three Souls

"We were eight, and then we were fewer. We kept the fire. A fire is not a people, but it is where a people can begin."

(Scratched into the hearth-stone at Eddale, in a child's hand)

The basilisk came in the Year Thirty-One, and the army of Aurenvale, such as it was, went out to meet it, because no one had told them they could stay home.

It was a thing out of the dark pines to the south, a long grey serpent-lizard the height of a horse at the shoulder and the length of three, with a crest of bone and eyes that did not blink. The record names it Viperscale. The children called it the Grey Dragon, because they had never seen a dragon and did not know the difference. It did not hunt, in the way a wolf hunts. It simply moved through a country, and what it passed through went still. By the Year Thirty-One it had reached the lemon groves south of Eddale and lay coiled in the heat among the fallen fruit, and Tomry, who had come up from Brookgate with five scrapings of men, two of them boys no older than fifteen, stood at the edge of the grove and looked at it for a very long time.

"I do not like it," said a boy. "It is not looking at us."

"No," said Tomry. "That is what I do not like either."

He counted the army. Five, with himself. He had told the Watcher, in his own way, in the silent habit of a man who had talked to a red star for thirty years, that it would not work. He told the star again, as he led them forward through the lemon trees with their bronze-tipped iron-hardened spears and their small, patient fear. It did not work. The army broke against Viperscale in the second quarter of an hour, and of the five who went into the grove, two came out. One was Tomry. The other was the boy, who stumbled into the dale at nightfall and said nothing for two days, and then said only: "It looked at me."

He died that winter. It was not the wound. It was a stillness. He had been looked at, and something in him had gone quiet, and he sat by the hearth with his hands open until the last of the light left them.

The eight children grew. They had to. Edmund, who was the eldest, stood in the dale each morning with a wooden spear he had whittled, and drilled the younger ones, in rows, as he had seen Tomry do. Maeold, who had arrived small and furious, grew into a tall and furious girl who kept the granary with her teeth. Anneth, who was gentle, kept the hearth. Edry and Isric, who were sister and brother, argued about everything and never once about the fire. The little ones, Maery, Edric and Elry, ran in the long grass, and they were the last of the children to learn what the hall had been before them.

"Why is there a chair under the picture?" Elry asked in the Year Thirty-Six.

"For the one who is not here," said Edmund.

"Who?"

"I do not know," said Edmund. But he looked, for a long time, at the empty throne, and the painted bridge, and the small figure at the end of the line with its hand upraised. And he thought, as he would think all his life, that it looked remarkably like a person who had been waiting.

In the Year Thirty-Seven, the Watcher ended a long silence and allied again with Zathixal, the oldest friend it had ever turned upon. Tomry, who had spoken the Watcher's decrees since Gerbert's death and had come up from Brookgate that autumn for the winter, spoke the words himself in the empty hall, in the flat voice that had once belonged to a young man waking in the grass. In the south, that same year, a party of Bolthul's orcs set out upon the sea in boats of skin and driftwood, and found new shores, and founded a place called Kragdak. Aurenvale heard of it only as rumour.

In the Year Thirty-Nine, the orcs of Bolthul attacked Aurenvale.

It was not a holy war this time. It was a tired, ugly thing, a raid against a hall that held a soldier and a handful of children, and every orc on the south road could see how it would end. It did not, because on the third day, out of the north-east, came the saurians, tall and grave, with a collar of river-glass at the head of the column. They had been betrayed, and they came anyway. The Bolthul warband broke on a line of green-scaled spears, and in the evening the saurian general, a long grave woman with a river-glass collar, climbed to the hall and stood under the mural.

"It is a good thing," she said, in her own cold tongue, "to paint a bridge before the river is crossed."

Edmund did not understand a word. He understood the hand. When she laid it flat against his palm, cold and dry as a stone from a stream, he did what he had seen Tomry do, and did not take his hand away.

But the dale was emptying. That was the truth that the saurians' help could not mend. Brookgate had lasted ten years with Tomry's patience and no one else's, and by the Year Forty it had two people in it, then one, then none. The record says it in a line: Year Forty: the village of Brookgate has been abandoned. Tomry came home to Eddale for the last time in the early autumn. He walked up the hill with a sack on his back and a spear gone grey with age, and stood in the doorway of the hall, and the children, who had grown, and the ones who had not, and the ones who had died, and the ones who had not been born, all seemed to stand with him in that light.

It was the Year Forty, and the winter came down on Eddale with four souls under its roof.

Not eight. The record is exact. Somewhere in those winters, in fevers and falls and the slow subtraction of a hard land, five of the children had gone, and the hall, which had been built for six and held thirty, held only Tomry, and Edmund, who was twenty-six by then and gaunt as a spear-shaft, and Edry, with Gerbert's brass horn at her belt, and Anneth, who kept the hearth. They did not hold a feast. They did not hold a council. They sat in the evening by the long fire, four people, and listened to the wind in the thatch, and looked at the painted bridge, and said nothing, because everything had already been said.

And then, in the south, something stirred. It was not a rescue. The Watcher did not send it, and could not have. In the southern meadows, between the lemon groves and the sea, a band of human wanderers, who had been walking for years, cold and sorrowful and unlooked-for, stopped by a river, and laid down their packs, and said to each other, Here. They built a village. They called it Northshire. They worshipped a god called Caspian, who was not Aurelan, and had no connection to the Watcher or the hall. They were strangers. But they were human, and they were close, and in the first evening of their fires, one of them looked north to the smoke of a single hearth on a far hill, and said: Someone is alive up there.

In Eddale, with the first snow of that winter, the Watcher spoke at last. The decree was not for glory or for gain. It was peace with Bolthul. Tomry, who had no breath left to spare, looked at Edry, who had kept the brass horn ten years against this day. She went out on the steps, blew it, and spoke the words into the snow, and the long war of thirty years went out of the hall like a lamp.

When she came in he was sitting heavily on the bench. Edmund brought him water. Anneth added a log to the fire.

"What do we do now?" said Edmund, quietly.

Tomry looked at the young man, who would one day be a general, and the two girls, one with a horn at her belt and one with ash on her hands, and the long north wall, and the six gold figures and the seventh at the end with its hand upraised. The red star, through the roof-gap, had grown no larger, but it was there.

"We keep the fire," he said. "Then we see who comes."

He lived until the last night of that year. He asked to be carried to the doorway, and they set him on the bench there with a blanket over his knees, facing south, as he had stood on the night of the first war. "Someone is looking at us," he said to Edmund. "I never minded it. Tell the star I held the door." Then, with his hand still open on the grey spear, he stopped. They buried him in the frozen ground beside Gereth's cairn, and when the sod was laid, three were left under the roof: Edmund, Edry and Anneth, and the horn, and the fire.

The Maker came to the table that night, and for once it did not sit across from the Watcher. It stood beside it, and they looked down together at the single small light on the hill.

"Three," said the Maker.

"Three."

The Watcher considered the question, which it had not been asked before, and which it had no right to answer. It thought of Tomry's grey spear, and Edmund's grey eyes, and the two girls, one with a horn and one who kept the fire. It thought of the six in the grass at the first light, who had looked at the sky and found it already marked.

"I think," it said, slowly, "that a people is whatever remains when the one who made it has been wrong. And that this is what remains."

The Maker said nothing. But it was the amused kind of silence, or something very near it, and far above the world, in the north, a little red star kept its place.

Chapter Six: Children of the Hall

"The hall was built for six and held three, and it was never once cold, because somebody always kept the fire."

(Said by Edry, herald of Eddale, in her old age)

Above the world, in the place where the two powers kept their accounts, the Watcher counted the people of Aurenvale in the last month of the Year 40 and got the answer three.

It counted again, as one does with a sum that cannot be right. There had been six at the founding, and twelve at the height of the third decade, and the Watcher had thought of them, in its proud and careful way, as a lettered people, patient and chivalrous, a people who wrote their oaths on lintels. Now there were three, and one of them had a cough.

"Three," said the Maker, who had come to look, and who did not say it unkindly. He sounded like a man looking down a well and reporting the depth.

"I know the number."

"I only mean that it is a small number to hold a continent with."

"It is not holding a continent," said the Watcher. "It is holding a hall." And it turned its attention down to the highlands, to the one chimney in all that country from which smoke was rising, and did not say what it was thinking, which was that every decree it had ever given had seemed correct on the day it was given.

The three were Edmund, Edry and Anneth.

Edmund was twenty-six, the eldest of the children of the Year 30, long in the arm and short in the temper, a young man who had learned at fourteen that grown people could simply stop, and had decided never to stop himself. He had buried Maeold, whom a winter fever took in the Year 33, and Isric and Edric, who died of a fever from the south, and Maery, who sickened in the Year 38, and little Elry, who went out after a lamb in the thaw of the Year 36 and was found by the river. He had buried Tomry the soldier last, in the hard ground of that same month, the one grown man who had come home from Brookgate with two sleeping children under his cloak, and who had taught a fourteen-year-old to hold a spear so that it was a wall and not a stick. Edmund cut the letters on Tomry's grave himself. They were bad letters, but they were deep.

Edry was twenty-four and had the best singing voice anyone in Eddale had ever heard, a distinction like being the tallest stone in a quarry. She was small, dark, quick to laugh and slow to forgive, and she carried at her belt a brass horn the length of her forearm, green at the seams, which had been the first herald's, and then Marda's to keep.

Gerbert had been herald of Eddale from the first morning of the world. He had stood on the hall steps every third winter and spoken the decree, and after his death Marda had hung the horn on its peg and taken it down for no one, until the Year 30, when she lay dying, very old by the measure of that people, and pressed it into the hands of a girl of fourteen. Tomry had spoken the decrees in the years between, in a soldier's flat voice, and the horn had waited. "It is not hard," Marda had said. "You will not understand it, and then you will say it anyway. That is the whole office."

Anneth was nineteen and said little. She had been nine in the Year 30, and had learned from the long quiet of that year how to be unnoticed, and had never entirely unlearned it. She kept the granary. In the evenings she also took charcoal and a splintered board and drew: faces first, then the hall, then the faces again. She showed no one. Edry found the boards in the spring and said nothing and only moved them somewhere drier.

The first decree Edry spoke came with the first snow of that winter, and she did not hear it coming.

She had expected a voice. Marda had never described one, and Tomry had only shrugged. What came was a stillness in the hall at the hour before dawn, when Edry had gone to feed the fire: a settling, as when a bird lands on a roof and you know it without looking. The words were simply there, formed whole, as a song is there when you have heard it a hundred times. Peace with Bolthul. She stood with a log in her hand until her hand cramped.

A herald speaks to a village, and the village was a girl with a granary key, a young man with a spear, and the herald herself. Edry climbed the steps in the cold and blew Marda's horn, and the note went out over the empty fields and came back off the hillside cracked and thin. She spoke the words into the snow. Nobody answered. A crow went up off the thatch. That was the way of things for years afterward: the horn, the words, the silence, and the herald going in to sit with her hands shaking.

Two weeks later an orc war-band came up from the south and stood in the snow at the foot of the hill. There were thirty of them, big, tattooed, hung with the little yellow skulls of their grandfathers, and at their head a chieftain with a necklace of fingerbones who had been told Eddale was a fat prize held by weaklings. He looked up and saw a hall with its roof half patched and a single thread of smoke, and on the steps a girl in a man's coat holding a horn, and in the doorway a young man with a spear and his back very straight, and behind him another girl, hardly more than a child, with a charcoal-black hand pressed to the doorpost. He counted them. It did not take long.

Orcs have a saying about a drum struck on a grave. It is not a kind saying, but it is a firm one. The chieftain stood a long minute while a younger orc at his shoulder asked in Orcish whether they were going up. Then he lifted his hand, and the war-band turned and went away down the hill, and that was the whole of the peace with Bolthul. Nothing was signed. There was only a gesture in the snow, which Edmund would remember all his life without being able to say why it had seemed the most dangerous moment of his youth.

"Did we win?" said Anneth, very quietly.

"No," said Edmund. "I think we were pitied." And he went in, because he found he was shaking.

The wanderers came in the spring, and Anneth saw them first, bringing up the last seed-barley from the abandoned store at Brookgate: a long ragged line of people with handcarts on the south road. She ran harder than she had ever run in her life.

There were twenty-three of them, human, like the three of Eddale, and they had a God. Edry noticed that first, because Eddale had only ever had a faith, and the faith was a thing of oaths and order. The foremost of the strangers, an elderly woman in grey with a face like folded linen, did not greet them. She lifted two fingers to her brow and murmured something. They were the people of the Church of Caspian. They had left a country in the east where nobody would feed them, she said, and walked since the thaw looking for water that was not already somebody's, and had founded a village a day south of the Eddale hills. They called it Northshire, after a place they had left. They had thought someone might be near.

"There are three of us," said Edmund, with the dreadful honesty of a young man too tired to lie.

The woman looked at the hall on the hill, at the horn, at Anneth's stained hands, and laughed, the short dry laugh of someone who has stopped being able to be surprised. "Well," she said. "Then we have both been very brave."

Anneth married the thatcher in the Year 42, a brisk small wedding under the lintel, and Edry spoke the Oathbound words from memory and had to stop twice. The Year 43 saw the first birth, a girl, red-faced, enormous, furious, whom the whole hall carried about for a week like a loaf that might rise. By the Year 45 there were ten souls, and Edmund, passing at night, would listen to ten people breathing, and then go out and stand on the hill, looking up at the faint red star that hung low in the north, which the Eddish called Aurelan's Eye and which no one liked to look at for long.

In the Year 43 and again in the Year 46, Anneth painted a siege of Bolthul on the east wall of the great hall, in ochre and grey and an expensive blue that cost her three weeks of wool: a wall of orc timber, and below it forty men in the high-collared coats of the first years, going up ladders. Nobody in Eddale had ever laid siege to Bolthul. The one army of Aurenvale had broken against a spider and then a basilisk and never reached any wall. At the top of her wall, where the defenders should have stood, she painted only a pale band of sky. "They are all dead," Edmund said, standing before it an hour. "You have given them ladders." "They never had any," said Anneth. "I thought they might as well have something to climb."

The Year 49 was the year of the temple, and of the question of the village.

The temple to Aurelan, Lord of Order, rose in the eastern garden, of grey stone hauled from a quarry the Northshire men found, and it took four years. It had a tower the height of three tall men, and a bell cast by the Northshire smith with a voice a little flat. When it was first struck, in the autumn, every crow within three miles went up at once. Fourteen people stood in the courtyard and listened, counting the babies, and Edry stood in front with Marda's horn and thought: I do not know what I am doing, and yet this is a thing being done.

That same autumn an elf came up from the east.

He was the first of the Luriel folk anyone in Aurenvale had seen in thirty years who was not trying to kill them. He rode a grey horse, wore a silver-grey braid down his back, and bore the faintly pained look of a man introduced to a family whose manners he had been warned about. He addressed Edry in an Eddish so formal and correct that it took her a minute to realise it was her own language. The Realm of Luriel, which had twice made war on Aurenvale, wished it known that it was prepared for alliance. The elves had observed with interest that Aurenvale had survived.

Then came the question of the village. The Watcher, in the third week of autumn, set before her the thought of founding another, and Edry sat the whole night before the fire with her hands between her knees. Everyone knew the story of the Year 28 though no one told it aloud: the decree, the families who went out and did not come back, Brookgate a ring of tumbled stones, Roston grass. She had been twelve. She could not remember the decree. She remembered the quiet.

In the morning she did what no herald of Eddale had done. She stood on the steps with the horn in her hand and did not blow it. She said aloud, to the sky: "If you ask this again, I will speak it. That is the office. But I ask that it not be a week's journey away. I ask that it be a day. I ask that we be able to see the smoke."

Brookham was founded that winter, a mile and a half from the hall wall on a rise by a stream, in sight of the hall steps: nine houses, eleven families, every roof laid by Anneth's thatcher. The ground was good and the stream did not flood. It was the one village the Watcher founded that did not in the end break its heart. The people of Brookham said that was because it stood in sight of the hall, and the people of the hall said it was because a herald had had the nerve to argue.

Chapter Seven: Edmund's Spear

"A spear is only a stick with an opinion. The opinion is the soldier's."

(Attributed to Edmund, general of Aurenvale)

In the Year 51 Aurenvale had a general again, a man of thirty-seven with a bad shoulder and a gift for being tired in front of soldiers without losing them.

Edmund had not asked for it. The office had been empty since Gereth went out against the elves in the Year 28. But Eddale now had thirty-one souls counting Brookham, and a fighting line of fourteen, nine of whom he had taught, and when the decree came Edry went out on the steps and said Edmund is made general, and a roar went up that could be heard at the temple bell. He took it with a short nod, like a man asked to carry something heavy up a hill who wonders how far it is. Then he went to find his spear, Tomry's plain ash-shaft with its iron head, and put a new socket on it, because it gave his hands something to do.

"You do not have to say anything," Edry told him that night. She was thirty-five, with a line between her eyes. She had borne three children and buried none, which in that generation counted as luck beyond calculation. "Marda used to say the ones who speak well die first."

"I have never spoken well."

"No," said Edry. "That is a point in your favour."

The Nobmuck war of the Year 53 was what the old soldiers of the Year 20 would have called a country quarrel. Goblin raiders from the far western savanna, long-limbed and grinning in ochre paint, crossed the Dwaldun border to steal sheep and burn barns, and for two summers Edmund's fourteen marched up and down the western ridge behind a smoke that was always three miles ahead. He caught nine of them once, in a dry streambed, and a goblin boy of twelve with a string of bone beads at his neck sat there regarding him with scholarly interest. Edmund gave him a heel of bread and let him go, and could never say why. Peace came in the Year 55 and changed nothing at all. The Watcher filed it under raids, and began to suspect that this was what most of its wars would be.

In the Year 56 the dwarves came.

They came from the west on foot, a long string of carts and ironbound packs, carrying their fire banked in a clay brazier on a wheeled cart and a hundred hammers in canvas rolls. Two days' march south-west of Eddale they raised in a month a stone wall so straight that a soldier who laid a cord along the top found it true to a hair. They called their hold Dwalhalm. They worshipped Vurna Emberforge, god of the forge, and their generals, Thrarik and Balok, had arms like tree-trunks and said very little. They brought what Aurenvale could not make for itself, the way of a mailed shirt that turned an arrow and a helm that held, and Edmund walked down in summer with a loaf of bread, and by the end of the afternoon he and a grey-bearded dwarf shared a tray of nails, a dislike of mud and a handshake.

That year the old chroniclers marked in red, for Aurenvale passed into the Medieval Age. Nobody noticed on the day. There was no bell. The mail merely came up from Dwalhalm in carts, the masons began laying stone in courses instead of heaps, and the boys of Brookham played at being Edmund in their fathers' old helms.

It lasted a year.

In the Year 57, Luriel, the ally of the grey horse and the formal Eddish, attacked Dwalhalm. The Watcher had not foreseen it and could not have prevented it. The elves said the dwarves had cut trees on a border they did not own. The dwarves said nothing and went on forging. Fighting dwarves with dwarf mail was like fighting a man with his own knife, and nobody spoke. The war had one real day. The dwarves came out of the wall under Thrarik on the long green slope below the hold, and the two lines met, and the thing was over in the time it takes to boil water. Edmund took an axe across the ribs on the left, under the arm, where his mail was thin from the old repairs. He kept fighting. He died in the hall, at the end of the long bench where he had eaten for sixteen years, with Edry holding one hand and Anneth the other, and Edry's eldest, a boy of seven, standing at the foot with his fists clenched. They buried him beside Tomry. Anneth cut the letters, and they were good letters. His spear they hung above the hearth in the great hall, its socket worn smooth by thirty years of hands, and for the rest of the age every new soldier of Aurenvale laid his palm on the shaft to take the oath.

Marric was made general.

Marric was a different sort of man. One of the twenty-three who had come up the hill in the spring of the Year 41, a ploughboy then, he was now a big, soft-spoken, deliberate man of thirty-seven, a believer in the Church of Caspian who had never quite let go of it in sixteen years in the Hall of Aurelan. He took the spear down from the hearth, held it a moment, and hung it carefully back. "I will not carry this. It is his."

Peace with Dwalhalm came in the Year 58. The dwarves, having lost thirty and killed eleven, seemed relieved to stop, and Thrarik sent a small cart up to Eddale with a bundle in oilcloth: a helm of the finest steel anyone in the highlands had seen, sent for the man who had killed his brother, who had been a good fighter. They set it on the bench beside Edmund's spear.

But Marric, who had stood on the slope below Dwalhalm and seen what Edmund's death cost, did something that winter that was either very brave or very foolish and was in the end both. He proposed that the army go and kill Viperscale.

The basilisk had broken the remnant army in the Year 31, and everyone in Eddale knew it as one knows the date of a funeral. It had a lair in the pine-hills to the north-west and a long yellow stare that turned things to stone. "It is in our country," Marric said in the hall. "It killed our own. If we do not take it down, our children will have to, and they will be fewer than we are."

Edry carried the question to the stillness in the hall. The Watcher did not say no. It said nothing, as usual. But the herald felt the stillness at her back, and heard herself say march, and went white.

They marched with eighteen men and a wagon of mirrors, the idea of the Dwalhalm smiths, who said the gaze might be turned back on itself. It failed. It was less a battle than a slow mechanical disaster in the pine-shadows: a flash of yellow, men turning to stone in mid-motion, mirrors cracking, and the soft heavy scrape of a body the size of a barn coming down out of a gully. Marric lost eleven. He came home with seven, and the shock in his big calm face was frightening to see.

That was the Year 60, in which the population of Aurenvale, counting Brookham and the hall and the children, stood at eighteen: the most it had been in thirty years. The Watcher regarded the number in the cold light of the early year with pride and dread, because it knew how fast eleven soldiers could vanish.

Nobmuck raided again in the Year 61, and peace was made the same year. The boy of the streambed, grown into a lanky raider in a goat-skin cap, was caught in a Brookham field watching the smoke and raised both hands like a man at market. "Bread," he said, in passable Eddish. "Good bread. I remember." The hall called him Streambed, and he took it for a title of honour.

In the Year 62 the thing that had not been possible was done. Viperscale was cut down in the summer, by a dull grinding sort of bravery, and the plan came from the Watcher, who had stood above the highlands through the three years since the failure and calculated. The basilisk could not be fought in the open and could not be tricked with mirrors. But its gaze was a thing of the eyes, and the Watcher had remembered something Edmund once said in the dark of the Year 56.

It gave no Sign. It was not yet bound by any Covenant, and would not have given one. It gave a decree, march on the beast, and the herald spoke it, and Marric tied veils of dark cloth dipped in pitch over the eyes of twenty soldiers, as the smiths of Dwalhalm suggested, and walked them up the pine-slope in a line, blind, each holding the belt of the man in front. At the head went one man who had lost his sight to a fever two winters before and could find the beast by hearing it.

Nobody remembered his name. It is in no record. He walked at the head of the line, heard the huge slow scrape of the thing in the gully, and called out, and the spears came down in the dark. When it was over he sat on the pine-needles and said, "I think it is dead. It is quiet now." He was right.

In the Year 65 they ended the Pale Eye, another beast, another gaze, another long grey day in the pines. By then Marric had mastered veils and silence, and it died in the middle of the harvest with five men hurt and nobody lost. When the veiled line came down the hill that evening, the whole of Brookham came out of its doors to meet them. Edry stood on the hall steps, a herald twenty-five years, and she did not blow the horn. She stood weeping and did not know why.

It was the best year of the Orphan Kingdom. The Watcher knew it even then.

Chapter Eight: The Kin-War

"To be asked to burn your own prayer-house for a promise made to a stranger. That is what an alliance is."

(From the words sung at the hanging of the "Lament for the Fallen of Dwalhalm")

It was Edry, not the Watcher, who first saw what the Year 64 would cost.

The alliance with Nobmuck was spoken on the usual steps, in the usual weather, to the usual small crowd, and Streambed the goblin stood at the front and clapped his long hands. It was a good alliance. It gave Aurenvale a friend on the western flank, where the raids came from. It meant, said Marric, that the burning would stop. It meant a market, a road, and the strange sweet black goblin-roasted nuts that Marric's children had begun to crave.

In the Year 66 the Watcher did what it would later count the most cruel kindness of the Orphan Years. It sent missionaries to Northshire.

The decree did not say so. It said only the Covenant of Aurelan goes south. Two young temple priests in grey and white walked down the south road in spring with a bell and a book and were met in Northshire by the elderly woman in grey who had walked into the Eddale hills twenty-six years earlier with a handcart. She was seventy-one. She heard the priests out for the better part of a day, and then she said something they did not understand until much later.

"It is a good faith. I have watched it twenty-six years. It is a faith for people who keep their promises, not for people who need to be forgiven. But I am old, and my children are tired, and I would like them to belong to something that has a hall."

In the autumn the people of Northshire put out the lamps at their doors one by one, the bell of Aurelan rang over the village, and the Church of Caspian was gone. It was also, the Watcher reflected through the next cold spring, a thing it had done for a reason it had not told even itself: it had wanted the Northshire people to be its own, so that if the hall ever fell, someone would be left who kept its rites.

"Now they will fight for you," said the Maker.

"They are not mine. They are their own. I have only taken away what made them different."

"That," said the Maker, "is what conversion is."

That same year, a dwarvish rider came up from the south-west with a long-handled mace and a red sash and laid before the hall steps, with perfect courtesy, a hammer wrapped in black cloth, which was the dwarvish way of declaring that a god had been insulted. Vurna Emberforge did not care to share the highlands with an Order that built stone temples without a forge in them. It was a holy war.

And in the middle of it came the war that was not supposed to be possible.

It was spring of the Year 67 and the rider who came to the hall was no stranger: a young Northshire man in white and grey, an Aurenvale sash tied about his arm, who stood in the courtyard with the look of someone sent to say something he had not been consulted about.

"Northshire has declared a holy war. On Nobmuck."

Edry stared. "Why?"

"The goblins are idolaters. They worship Brogmar Bloodtide. They keep skulls. Our priests say the Lord of Order has spoken, and that the heathen in the west must be brought low. It is the first thing Northshire has done in the name of the Covenant." He swallowed. "We thought you would be pleased."

"Nobmuck is our ally," said Edry. "We swore. Then we are bound to fight Northshire." She had said it aloud and it was still impossible. In the doorway Marric said very softly, "They are our own faith. We took their lamps."

She did not sleep. She sat with her back to the great hearth and Marda's horn across her knees and asked aloud, as one asks a stranger across a room, what do you want me to say? The dawn came grey through the east window. The decree was the plainest she had ever received. Honour the alliance.

In the Year 67 the Watcher's pride and its own words met in a single morning, and the Maker, watching, said only, "You keep signing the same kind of paper."

"I know."

"Do you mean to stop?"

"I mean to keep my word," said the Watcher. "I do not know what else a word is for."

On the steps in the cold, Edry raised the horn, and it came out flat and cracked over the roofs of the hall and the temple and Brookham, and she said in her level borrowed voice: Aurenvale marches against Northshire. Nobody cheered. In the courtyard Marric took off his helm and stood bareheaded in the cold, and one by one the soldiers did the same, until the whole line stood in the stone yard with their heads bare, like men at a graveside.

The war with Northshire was fought in the Years 67 and 68, and it was the worst thing Aurenvale did in the whole of the Orphan Age.

The army of Northshire was small and brave: thirty men in white tabards singing the Hymn of Aurelan, which they had learned that year, in different keys. Aurenvale met them on the south road with levelled spears. It was hard for Marric, who had been a Northshire boy himself. There were men in that line who had been ploughboys beside him in the Year 41. Before the first battle he rode up and down and called their names across the field, one by one, until a voice from the other side answered: Marric, don't.

He did.

At the third battle, at a ford in the Year 68, a boy in a Northshire tabard dropped his spear in the water and stood with open hands, and a soldier of Aurenvale who was twenty, and had been told all his life that the Northshire folk were cousins, hesitated for a heartbeat. In that heartbeat a sergeant behind him struck the boy down. The soldier sat in the cold water and wept. His name is not in the record.

In the autumn of the Year 67, after a night in which twenty-one wounded were laid on the long benches, Anneth began a painting no decree had asked for. She could not paint the Northshire war. She painted the dwarves: the slope below Dwalhalm in the rain of the Year 57, thirty dead dwarves lying in rows with their helms laid aside, and above them one figure in a grey cloak holding a lamp. She called it Lament for the Fallen of Dwalhalm. It was the only work of art in the history of Aurenvale made in honour of an enemy's dead. "They are not our dead," said Marric, before it in the firelight. "No," said Anneth. "That is why someone should."

They slew Gloomleg in the Year 69. It was a great black spider that had spun a grey web across the birch-wood below Eddale where the children gathered nuts, and when it took the child of a Brookham miller the village came to the hall in the dark with lanterns and stood before Edry's door.

The army had twelve men. They went out in the autumn with torches and fire-arrows and a long chain, and it was the last great hunt of the age. The last blow was struck by a thin, quiet soldier with a hare-lip, whose name the record did not keep, who climbed into the birch with an axe and cut the web's anchor-cable and brought the whole black weight down into the fire. Gloomleg burned an hour. People said the sound it made was like a kettle.

They lost four: the miller himself, who had gone after the thing alone, two soldiers they could least spare, and a boy of sixteen named for Edmund. And that was the year Edry's eldest, the boy of seven who had stood at the foot of Edmund's bed, came home from the war with the Northshire men. He was nineteen. He had been at the ford. He said nothing then or later, but the night he came home he took the brass horn down from its peg, held it a while, and put it back, and his mother watched him and thought: I will not ask.

He was dead by the first snow, of a fever that came up with the army, and they buried him beside Edmund.

That winter Edry made a lullaby to quiet an infant who would not stop crying, a tune that went up and paused, as if listening, and came down slowly, with words of a hall in the snow and a fire that was kept. Anneth carved the words round the temple door and called it the Hymn to Aurelan. Of everything made in the Orphan Age, it was the only thing that outlived the world.

Peace came in the Years 71 and 72. First Northshire: the two armies met at the ford in the rain and nobody could think of anything to say, and Marric walked across the water alone and shook the hand of the white-tabarded captain, who was weeping.

Then Dwalhalm, in the Year 72, the same spring that dwarf wanderers went up into the far north near Dobwhistle and founded a hold called Torzul, a place no one in the highlands knew yet, which would one day trouble Aurenvale very much. The dwarves of Dwalhalm came to the hall under a flag of truce and stood before the mural of their own dead. Balok the general stood a long while with his arms crossed. Then he said something in Dwarvish, and the interpreter said: "He says it is a good likeness of the slope. He says it is not accurate about the weather." "It was raining," said Anneth. "He says it was drizzling." That was the last joke between the two peoples, for by then most of those who might have laughed at it were dead or dying, and the Orphan Kingdom had begun to run out of grown people.

Chapter Nine: The Empty Hall

"We did not die of a monster. We died of a long, quiet subtraction. Nobody could say which year was the last one that mattered."

(From the journal of Anneth of Eddale, found in the hall in the Year 75)

The giants of the world woke up in the Year 73, and Aurenvale was not among them.

The Watcher saw it from above in one sweep, as one sees the edge of a storm across a plain. In the east the elves of Luriel passed into the Age of Engines, a thing of smoke and iron and mill-chimneys, and the elves, who had never in their long history hurried, found themselves building railways. In the south-west the forges of Dwalhalm were loud with new hammers. Everywhere there was a thin continuous hum of making, and everywhere the red star, a little larger than last year, burned low and steady in the evening sky. In Aurenvale, still Medieval among the engines, the shepherds had begun to speak of something large and many-legged on the north ridge, and to call it Shrikka.

There was a good harvest in the Year 73, and the last art of the Orphan Years: a tall chair of white birch in the great hall that Anneth called The Radiant Throne. Nobody was to sit in it. It was, she said, to be left empty for whoever came. She carved it with wheat and fruit and little hammers, and on its back the figure of Aurelan with a hand lifted, and round the base, very small, a row of names. She cut the last name in January of the Year 74. It was Marric's.

He was fifty-four, and had been general seventeen years. He went out on the north road in the first week of the thaw to look into a rumour of something in the pines, and came back with a cough, which he said was nothing. It went to his chest. He died in the long room above the hall with a grey-bearded dwarf from Dwalhalm sitting at the foot of the bed, who had walked all that way and did not say a word in three days.

The war with Northshire had taken some, and the war with Dwalhalm more, and Gloomleg four, and the fevers the rest. The people of Aurenvale found themselves in the autumn of the Year 74 reduced, by something that was not a battle and had no day, to a handful. The children were many. The grown were few. And in the high cold afternoons the Watcher watched a woman with a white streak in her hair walk out on the hall steps with a brass horn in her hand and a small boy on her hip, and look at the sky.

It had begun to think about the unthinkable.

"I could do it," said the Watcher.

It was the first week of the Year 75, a still bright winter night with a thin moon, and the powers were alone above the world. The Maker sat on a stool he had conjured for the occasion. The Watcher stood with its hands behind its back and did not look at him. It looked at the hall.

"Could do what?"

"Put my hand down. I could take every child in that valley and set them behind a wall of ice. I could bring every beast of the Spine down on Zathixal in a night. I could burn Bolthul's clover, or call the dead of Dwalhalm up out of their slope. I have not done any of it, because I always thought it beneath me. It was a thing a lesser power might do. It was what a god would be, if a god were what people say."

"Yes," said the Maker. "And should you?"

"If I do, there is no game. There is a god and its puppets. You said, fight wisely. You said, make them prosperous. You did not say, keep them safe at any price. If I cut every thread then all they ever did was a thing I let them do, and what they built was nothing and what they were was a play. And if I do not--"

"Hm," said the Maker. He considered, not at all alarmed. He appeared deeply interested, like a man who has waited long to see whether a thing would be done and does not much mind which way. "So you will do something in between."

"I am going to bind myself," said the Watcher, and turned and looked him full in the face, which it had not done in years. "I will say what I will do and what I will not. I will rule by the word, one decree each three years, as before. I will not raise a hand against any other people. I will not curse. I will not make anyone believe what they do not. I will not declare a war for another. But I will give my own people a thing. Rarely. Once in a decade at most. A Sign, to them and no one else, never against anyone. And whatever else happens, wars will be won by their soldiers, not by me."

"A Covenant."

"Yes. With myself, and with you, if you will take it. I would like it witnessed."

The Maker stood and looked at it for a long moment with his head to one side, and for the first time there was something in his face other than amusement. It was not quite respect. It was what a man feels when he sees a child do something right and foolish at the same moment and cannot tell which it is.

"It is a very handsome promise," he said. "Is it honour, or is it pride?"

"I do not know. I would like to say honour. But I cannot tell the difference from inside."

"That," said the Maker, "is the most honest thing you have said in seventy-five years. Swear it, then. I will witness. And at the end, when the Stone comes, or does not, you will tell me which it was."

It was sworn on the third night of the Year 75, in no place, to no sound. The Watcher said the words aloud though there was no air, and the Maker listened with his chin on his hand. There were three, as there had once been three people in a hall. Only by decree. One Sign in a decade, to my own and no other. Wars are won by soldiers, not gods. They were not long. They did not seem, once said, to change the world in the slightest.

And yet, in the place where the accounts were kept, a line was drawn.

In the great hall at Eddale, at that same hour, the fire had burned down.

The six children of Aurenvale lay asleep on the long benches in the old grey cloaks. There was Jomund, ten, the eldest and the loudest, with the hands of a Brookham soldier who had fallen at the Northshire ford when the boy was three. There was Annric, nine, thin and quiet with a stubborn jaw. There was Robert, seven, who talked in his sleep. There was a small dark girl named Elry, for the one who went after the lamb, and a taller pale one named Elora, and a round sunny child called Tombert, who had no mother and slept with his thumb in his mouth. They had gathered through the long autumn out of houses emptied one by one, as birds gather in a barn.

Edry was not asleep. She sat in the high chair at the head of the table with the horn in her lap, and she was dying, though she was only fifty-nine. Anneth, who had gone before her in the first snow, quietly in her sleep, had left one sheet of paper on the table by the empty throne, the last page of her journal. We did not die of a monster. We died of a long, quiet subtraction.

"It is not fair," Edry said to the fire, because there was no one left to hear it.

The stillness at the back of the hall did not move. But the old herald looking up thought for a moment she heard it, faint, the way you hear a thing you have listened for all your life: an attention turned toward her, huge and gentle, like a hand held a little above a flame.

"I do not know what you are," said Edry. "Marda did not know either. But I have carried your word thirty-five years, and I spoke it every time. I do not ask for anything. I only want you to know that it was done."

She got up. It took some time. She went to the door in her grey cloak with the horn in her hand and went out on the steps, under a sky black and enormous with stars and the red star low over Brookham, larger than she had ever seen it.

Then she put Marda's horn to her mouth and blew. It was not a decree. It was only a note, long and clear and true, the best sound she had made in thirty years, and it went out over the sleeping roofs of hall and temple and Brookham, over the frozen fields and the cairn on the north road and the grass where Roston had been. For once it did not crack at all. She lowered it, and in the great dark behind her the fire in the hall, which had been nearly out, took a sudden golden breath and burned up.

It was Annric who found her in the morning, a thin brown boy of nine in a man's cloak, who came out on the steps at dawn and stood quite still. She sat on the top step against the doorpost as if she had sat down a moment to rest, her face turned south, the horn in her lap and frost on her hair. Her eyes were open, and in them was a look he had never seen on a grown person: a vast quiet, like someone who has finished a long task and set it down.

The others had come to the door behind him. Jomund, stocky and furious, clutching a blanket. Robert, wide-eyed. Elry with her hand over her mouth. Elora and Tombert side by side.

"Is she asleep?" said Tombert.

"No," said Annric.

"Is she dead?"

"Yes."

"Who is going to do the horn?"

It was a child's question and the only one that mattered. Annric stood a long moment on the cold step. Then he stooped and lifted the brass horn from his aunt's lap and found it heavy, green at the seams, and warm, as if it had been held. He did not know how to blow it. He had only ever heard it from a distance. He stood with it in both hands, a boy of nine in the winter dawn, and in the silence the five others waited.

He lifted it to his lips.

And in the hall behind them a light came on.

It was not firelight. It was a pale clear even light, like morning that has come through a wall, and it fell across the benches and the table and the lintel and the empty white throne. The children turned. In the middle of the great hall, where the flagstones were worn, the air had thickened, as it does above a road in summer, and in the thickness, one by one, as though stepping through a curtain, there were people.

There were six. They were grown, tall and lean and weathered, in hard leather and bright mail, with swords and spears in their hands, and every face wore the same faint dazed resolute bewilderment of soldiers wakened in a strange camp. A broad dark woman with a scar through one brow looked down at her own hands as though she had never seen them. A long thin man with a spear turned in a slow circle, staring at the rafters. A young woman put a hand to the mail at her breast and drew a quick breath. A big fair man with a lopsided grin shook out his shoulders and said in plain Eddish, to nobody: "Well. This is not the barracks."

Then the broad dark woman took a step forward and went down on one knee on the flagstones before a boy of nine, and said low, as one gives the first oath of a new beginning: "I am Maeora. I do not know how I came here. But I know whose hall this is." She looked up. "Tell us what you need."

Over the white hills, in the thin light of the winter morning, the red star set, and the sun came up on the Year 75.

In the place above the world, the Watcher, who had raised no hand against anyone and had done only what it swore, turned and looked at the Maker.

"Well," said the Maker softly. "That is the First Host. You will want to name them."

"Maeora," said the Watcher, "and Elric, and Annwin, and Edwin, and Edora, and Corry."

"And what are they for?"

"They are soldiers. That is all I will say. The rest is theirs."

And below, in the hall of Eddale, Jomund, who was the eldest and had decided it, gently took the old brass horn from Annric's hands, set it on the table, and lit the lamp.

Chapter Ten: Six From Nowhere

"Do not ask them where they were born. They will tell you the truth, which is that they do not know, and then you will both be sad."

(Advice to children, Eddale nursery, attributed to Edora)

They came in the first week of the seventy-fifth year, if the record is to be believed, and the record is all there is. The children told it afterward two ways, in the hall and on the road, and the roll sets down both and chooses neither.

The six children of Eddale were at the well. They had been at the well since before light, because the rope had frayed in the night and the eldest of them, a boy of ten named Jomund who had appointed himself the keeper of everything, would not let the little ones draw water until he had spliced it. He was bent over the splice with his tongue between his teeth when Elry, who was six and had the sharpest eyes in the hall, said in a flat small voice, "There are people on the road."

There were no people on the road. There had been no people on the road for the better part of a year, and the children had stopped looking at it the way one stops looking at a door behind which something has died. Jomund did not look up. Then Elry said, "Six," and Jomund looked.

They stood in the grey light at the head of the road where it came down out of the birchwood, three men and three women, or two men and... the children would argue about it for years, and the argument is not worth preserving. They were grown. They were armoured in the plain boiled leather and iron of the old Medieval kind, which Aurenvale still wore while the elves down the coast had begun to wear something that hummed. They carried spears. They were not hungry, and they were not frightened, and none of them seemed to have walked anywhere. There was no dust on their boots. That, Jomund said later, was the thing he noticed first, and then could not stop noticing: there was dust on the road, grey and thick from the dry spring, and there was none on them.

A tall woman stepped forward. She had a long plain face and a scar through one eyebrow, and she looked at the six children with an expression Jomund had never seen on a grown face, because he had barely seen a grown face. It was the look of someone who has been handed a thing too large to hold and has decided to hold it anyway.

"I am Maeora," she said. "Where is the hall?"

Jomund pointed. His mouth had stopped working. Behind her the others had begun to look about them, at the birchwood and the hills and the great timbered hall with its half-fallen roof, and he saw them each in turn go very still, the way a man goes still when he is trying to remember a name. The man beside her was thick and quiet and called Elric. A younger woman, Edora, put a hand over her mouth. A burly soldier named Edwin said, to no one, "I know this place. I have never seen this place." And the last of them, a wiry young man with a crooked grin who gave his name as Corry, laughed, once, and then stopped, because nobody else had.

They were all, of course, from nowhere. It took the hall some days to grasp it, and some years to stop minding. They had no mothers. They had no village to remember, no first kiss, no childhood fever, no war that had made them. When Edora was asked, gently, by one of the children, what her father had been like, she opened her mouth to answer and found, to her visible horror, an empty room where an answer should have been. Yet she knew how to string a bow and read the weather and pray to Aurelan, Lord of Order. She knew the Eddish tongue, and the words of the Oath, and the name of the hall. She knew, in the way one knows one's own hands, that she was a soldier of Aurenvale. She simply knew nothing at all about why.

"It is a gift," Maeora said on the second night, to the fire, "and I intend to treat it as one. I have been given six hands and a spear. I will not sit weeping because the hand did not come with a history."

But she did not sleep that night, and neither did anyone else.

On the fourth evening Annwin, who had said least of all of them, drew Maeora aside. He was a heavy, grave man of perhaps thirty, with a soldier's economy of movement and an oddly gentle voice.

"There is something I should say," he said. "I have the name of the first general. It was in my head when I came. I do not know why."

"Annwin was the general who died on the Weaver."

"Yes."

"And you are Annwin."

"I am," he said, "and I do not know whether that is a blessing or a warning."

Maeora considered this. "It is a name," she said. "Names are given to be worn. Wear it better."

Above the world, in the place that is not a place, the Watcher looked down at what it had done, and was not sure.

It had sworn the Covenant not three days before, and it was still, in some small corner of its attention, waiting for someone to tell it that the oath had been a mistake. It had kept its Sign: six souls, grown, armed, set down on the road to Eddale with nothing in their heads but what a people needs in order to carry on. It had agonised over the details. Should they have names? Should they have memories? It had decided against memories, because a false past is a lie and the Covenant, whatever else it was, was to be built on the truth. It had given them skill and habit and faith and nothing else. They would have to make their own histories now, the way everyone else had to.

It did not, in the end, feel like triumph. It felt like leaving a child at a door.

"You seem troubled," said the Maker.

The Maker had come as it always came, without arriving: a presence on the other side of the Watcher's attention, patient, a little amused. It was not in the habit of speaking first. It had set the terms and then, to the Watcher's mild alarm, kept its word and left the Watcher alone. The Watcher had grown used to long silences, and to the odd, dry visits in which the Maker asked a question and listened to the answer with what felt like genuine interest.

"They are lost," said the Watcher. "They know they are lost. Every one of them is looking at the ground for a past they cannot find."

"You gave them what you could give."

"I gave them hands. A people needs more than hands." The Watcher paused. "I wonder if I have given them the wrong thing."

"You told me you would only help in ways that did not cost anyone else," the Maker said. "That is what the Covenant is. I do not see you breaking it."

"No. But I see what it costs me." The Watcher was quiet. "There were seventy-five years in which I did anything I liked, and look what I made of them. A village decree that emptied my hall. Wars I stumbled into. An army fed to a spider." It did not say what it was thinking, which was: and then I swore not to do any of it again, and the swearing feels like a wall I built around my own hands.

"Do you want me to say the Covenant was wise?" the Maker asked.

"No."

"Good. I wasn't going to."

The Watcher looked at the small bright lamp of Eddale in the central highlands and said nothing for a long while.

The Watcher's first decree after the Covenant, spoken in the year seventy-six through the mouth of the boy Jomund, who was eleven and had been put forward as herald in a robe three sizes too big, was an alliance with the goblins of Nobmuck. Their ambassador, short and bandy-legged and wrapped in a cloak of dyed rushes, looked about the nearly empty hall with open scepticism and said, "You are very few." "We are," said Maeora, "but we are not weak." It was a piece of nerve, and it worked.

Jomund, who was now eleven, kept the staff of the herald under his bed that night, as he would for the rest of his life. He had been told that the herald was the one who spoke the Watcher's decrees. He had not been told how the Watcher spoke, and he did not ask, because he already knew. Every three years, in the hour before dawn, he woke with the sentence already in his head, whole, plain, and not his own. He spoke it in the hall at sunrise, and the hall obeyed. He was a quiet, watchful, orderly boy who would become a quiet, watchful, orderly man, and he never once, in all the years of his heraldry, confessed to anyone how much he feared getting a word wrong.

Years passed. They were not years of triumph, but they were years in which no one died, and Aurenvale was learning how rare that was.

Year eighty-two: Torzul attacked.

The dwarves of Torzul had been founded ten years earlier, far in the north near the gnomes of Dobwhistle, a hold of wandering craftsmen who worshipped Gorim of the gems and kept their own counsel. No one in Eddale had ever met one. They came down in the spring with axes and a great clanking wagon-train, and Maeora, who had hoped the First Host's first battle would be a more dignified affair, found herself defending the northern pastures against stout bearded strangers who did not seem to know why they were there either. She was the best of the Host with a spear. She took a cut across the thigh and a bruised rib. Corry, who was twenty paces off, laughed throughout, the wild brief laugh of a man for whom a battle is the first thing that has made sense in seven years of nothing.

It ended in a season. The dwarves went home. Nobody was quite certain what the war had been for, and the hall, which had expected a longer and more terrible thing, held a quiet, puzzled peace.

Year eighty-three was less simple. The ruined Kingdom of Northshire, the human cousins who had embraced Aurelan only seventeen years before and been bled by Aurenvale's wars for the privilege, finally fell, its last hearth cold. It went with no battle and no sound. A merchant from Brookham came back with the news in the autumn and told it standing in the hall with his hat in his hands. A people that had built a temple, and sung the Hymn, and been, for a while, kin, were gone. In the same year Dwalhalm attacked Nobmuck, and Aurenvale, bound to its goblin ally by the pact of seventy-six, marched against dwarves for the second time in a generation.

It was an old war, a stale war, a war of burned barns and trampled barley on the southwest border, fought by an army of five soldiers and whatever the hall could spare. The dwarves of Dwalhalm had come to the end of their strength; they fought like a thing that had already been told it was dying. The peace came in eighty-five, and no one cheered.

Elric, the carpenter, died that autumn, without ceremony, of a fever that took three days. He was the first of the First Host to go.

It was a terrible, ordinary thing. He had been splitting kindling in the yard. He came in at noon with a headache and sat by the fire. By the second night his hands were hot and wandering. Edora sat with him, and Maeora, and the herald Jomund, who by now was nineteen and had a man's height and a boy's frightened face. Elric spoke very little in the dark of the third night. Once he said, with some effort, "I made the beam," and Maeora said, "You did. It is the best beam in the hall." He smiled. He died toward dawn.

They buried him on the hill above Eddale among the earlier graves, the old graves, the ones that went back to Tomry and Marda and the six who had begun it. Edora planted a birch sapling there. The children stood in a row with their hands folded. Little Tombert, who was eleven now and had never seen a death, asked in the clear loud voice of the very young where Elric had gone, and nobody could tell him, because nobody knew.

The six of the First Host had become five. Of the first six, eighty years ago, none were left, and none of the First Host knew the names of the men who had stood in their places. But something had begun to happen in the hall that Maeora saw and did not name. The children were looking at her differently now. They were looking at all five of the grown ones the way one looks at the last of something. They were learning to look carefully, and to love what they were looking at, because it would not last.

Year eighty-four. The year before the Birth-Wave.

Chapter Eleven: The Birth-Wave

"We were twenty-three by the end of that season. I counted. I counted every morning for a month, because I did not believe the first count."

(Annric, later keeper of the hall rolls)

It began with a cry in the night.

The hall at Eddale had not heard the cry of a newborn in many years. The last had been, as best anyone could reckon, forty-odd winters gone, in the dark of the orphan years, a thing half remembered as a dream. So when it came, thin and furious, from the women's chamber on a night of heavy rain in the eighty-fifth year, the five of the First Host were on their feet before they knew they had heard it.

"It is not possible," said Edora, who had delivered nothing in her life, and who was in the room before anyone else.

It was Marda's grand-daughter's grand-daughter, if the old lines could be believed, a young woman of Brookham who had come up to the hall for the midwinter feast and been delayed by weather. Nobody had known she was with child. She was not a woman who talked about such things. The baby was a girl, red, loud and perfect, and she was the first.

There was a second by the end of the week. A third came before the month was out, in a hut on the Brookham road, and the midwife who attended it said afterward that she had felt the very air of the room go bright and strange, as if the ordinary hand of nature had been laid over by a larger one. By the first day of summer there were eight.

Eight children, born in a single season, in a hall where there had been none for years. It was the Watcher's second Sign. No herald announced it, and no decree named it. It simply occurred, in the way that spring occurs, and the people of Aurenvale received it with a bewilderment that slowly curdled into joy.

The Watcher, who had given the Sign, did not claim it. It watched the eight cradles with an expression it would not have known how to describe, and a feeling that did not have a name in the language of the powers.

"Eight," said the Maker, who had a way of arriving at the moments that mattered.

"Eight."

"You are pleased."

"I am relieved," said the Watcher. "That is a smaller thing."

"Is it?"

The Watcher did not answer. It did not like to look at the red star, which hung over the continent now as large as a thumbnail, and over every realm in a different colour, and over Eddale in the colour of a coal.

Annwin to march on Shrikka.

The great spider Shrikka had come to the highlands in the seventy-third year, a creature of the hills and the old nest-ruins to the north of Eddale, grey as a rotted tree, bigger than any beast the First Host had seen. She had taken sheep. She had taken, in the winter of eighty-five, two goatherds from Brookham, whose bodies were found hanging in the birchwood wrapped in silk. The hall could not rest with her there.

The march was Annwin's, who had the name of the first general and who had gone grim at the decree. The five of the First Host who remained, with seven soldiers newly come of age, went north into the hills in the spring. The march had no art to it. They tracked the silk-lines through the birchwood to the nest on the shoulder of a ridge, a vast grey shroud in the branches of dead trees, and for three days they waited in the cold for the beast to show herself.

She came on the fourth night. The record does not say how, and the survivors, when they came back, did not speak of it. They came down the road in the grey of the fifth morning, eleven where there had been twelve, and the missing one was Annwin.

They did not carry his body. Edwin, who had been the first to reach him and the last to leave, said only, in a very flat voice, that there had been nothing left to carry.

In the great hall that night Maeora stood at the hearth and said the rites for the second Annwin, in the same words, to the same silence, as had been said for the first. She did not weep. She stood there with her scarred face turned to the fire, and when she had finished she said, "Two."

It was in the spring of the next year, the eighty-seventh, that the record at last gave Aurenvale something it could keep.

Edwin made general, and under him the hall marched on Shrikka again, with the eleven and the first of the young ones come of age, a ragged and very young army with fire-pots and long ash-wood spears. They fought her at the ruined nest on a bright morning in the foothills. They did not fight her well. She took four. She came for Edwin himself, the great body rearing grey and hairy against the dawn, and the old soldier, in a moment that none who saw it ever forgot, stood in her shadow and did not run.

It was a young soldier who killed her, and no one remembers the name. The record says only that the soldier was new to the line and struck at the right moment. The spider went down. The hills went quiet. The marchers came home with six dead and the great grey hide on a litter and a feeling that the world was larger than they had been told.

Jomund, standing in the hall door to receive them, wrote it in the roll the same evening: The Hold of Dwalhalm has fallen into ruin. Shrikka is dead. The two events had occurred in the same season and he set them down in the same breath. The dwarves of the southwest, who had fought the hall since the fifty-seventh year, were gone; the great spider of the highlands was gone; and the herald of Eddale, who was twenty-two years old and had known nothing but the thinning of his people, wrote with an unsteady hand: We have been spared.

He did not know whether it was a fact or a prayer. Nobody ever does.

Chapter Twelve: The Siege That Was Not

"A war is a promise made to people who are not there to hold you to it."

(Corry, general of Aurenvale, in his last years)

The first war that Aurenvale chose, it chose out of boredom, though no one in the hall would ever have said so.

The Watcher, if it was honest, would have called it ambition. The Covenant had left it three tools: peace, alliance and war, a village now and then, a gift to the arts. It had used peace. It had used alliance. In the eighty-eighth year it stood above a hall of thirty and a country finally quiet, and it considered what a people is for.

The hall declares war on the Hold of Torzul.

It was the first war that Aurenvale had not stumbled into. For seventy years it had been dragged into wars by alliances, by holy wars, by border quarrels, by the plain bad luck of being in the middle of the continent. Now it chose. The Watcher, who had thought about the decision for a season, would tell itself in later years that it had been reasoning: Torzul was weak, Torzul had attacked first, in the eighty-second year, and Torzul was a dwarf-hold on the far northern cold, a long march away, with no great friends. A war on such a realm might be won, and a won war would be a thing the Covenant did not forbid.

What it did not consider carefully enough was the march.

The hall was a little larger, and a little prouder, in the year that Aurenvale entered the Gunpowder Age. The craftsmen of Brookham had learned the trick of the black powder from a Luriel merchant, and the old iron-and-leather soldiers of the Medieval line were being taught, with some muttering, to carry the new long guns. Edwin hated the guns. They were slow to load and they stank and they made a noise that frightened the horses. But he liked a fight that ended at a distance, and so he practised on the meadow behind the hall with a face like a man eating a lemon.

They marched for weeks. They reached the hold in the autumn, with sore feet and wet powder, and found it a gaunt grey place of stone and slate, with a wall too high to climb, a gate too thick to burn, and a people who had expected them. The dwarves of Torzul did not come out to fight. They sat behind their stone and watched the soldiers of Aurenvale burn three outlying farms, which the soldiers did with a certain sullen thoroughness, being unable to think of anything else to do.

They marched home in November. It took them two months to do it. Four men died of the cold on the way, and the two hundred and fifty miles of road in the mountain autumn gave them a quiet that no battle could have done. When they came back to Eddale at the dark of the year the hall was full of children, and no one asked how the war had gone.

The Bolthul war, which came next, was not chosen. It came in the eighty-ninth year, as the first had come in the ninth: the orc horde of the south, the Cult of Krukka and its drums, declared a holy war on Aurenvale, and the soldiers of Aurenvale learned that a war that comes to you is very different from a war you carry.

In the ninety-first year it did. The decree was peace, and the herald, standing in the great hall with the staff in his hands, spoke it in a voice that cracked on the last word. Bolthul accepted. The war had cost sixteen soldiers and the burned granary at Brookham, and it was, in the end, a thing that happened to a hall rather than a thing the hall did.

In the same year, two events were written in the same hand.

The first was this: Corry was made general.

It was a plain thing, and nobody had expected it. Edwin was alive. Edwin had held the spear for four years with honour, and no one had any complaint against him. But the Watcher, deciding in the ninety-first year that the hall needed a different kind of leader for the long game it had begun to see, sent the thought to the herald in the hour before dawn, and Jomund spoke it at sunrise: Corry is general of the armies of Aurenvale.

Edwin took it without bitterness. He handed over the spear on the hearth with the faint, rueful grin of a man laying down a weight. "You will be better at the long war," he said. "I was always a man for the short one."

"We do not have a long war."

"You will." He clapped Corry on the shoulder. "I have seen how you look at maps."

The second was the founding of Stanfield.

It was a decree: Found a village. The word fell on the hall like a stone in a pond. Jomund, who had cause to remember the last time the Watcher had spoken it, in the twenty-eighth year, could not keep his hands from trembling on the staff. In the roll, which he had read many times, were the names of the families who had gone out to Brookgate and Roston and not come back. The orphan years were not a distant story to him. They were the reason he himself was alive.

But the hall, which had grown, looked at the herald and at the decree, and at the green meadows to the east of Eddale where a stream ran cold from the hills. They chose the place with care. They sent out twelve, not two hundred. They kept a rider in the saddle between the hall and the new hamlet, and every week, without fail, the rider went and returned. The village was called Stanfield, for the stone that stood in the centre of its green. It took root slowly, with the peace and the rider at its back, and the Watcher, which had spoken the word with something like terror, watched the new hearths light with a small and careful hope.

It was a different village, the Watcher told itself. It was a different time. I know the names of the people.

It did not tell itself what it was, in the end, setting in motion. Nobody does.

That autumn the monsters came again, as they always did, a thunderbird over the high meadow and a troll in the far west. The year after, in the ninety-third, the hall made peace with Torzul. The war had lasted five years and had cost twenty-one souls, and no wall had been breached or field lost. In the same year, almost as a kind of reward, a young soldier struck down the basilisk called the Pale Eye in the high meadow, and the herald wrote it in the roll, and for a season the hall felt that fortune had turned.

It had not. The ninety-fourth year came, and the Watcher spoke the decree again: war on Torzul.

The second march on Torzul was better planned and no better rewarded. Corry took twenty-two soldiers and three hundred pounds of powder. They came to the hold in the early autumn of the ninety-fourth year, and Corry, who had learned from the first campaign, did not waste a week sitting outside the gate. He put his guns on a ridge to the east of the wall and shelled it. The dwarves of Torzul, who had expected a siege, were not accustomed to being shot at from a hilltop, and the first week of that war was the closest Aurenvale came to victory.

For six years it went on, in the thin, dispiriting way of a war that has no end because it has no beginning. There were skirmishes in the passes. There were burned barns and burned bridges. There was the long road, north and back again, and the soldiers who fell off it by the cold or the wolves or the plain exhaustion of carrying powder over a mountain. In the ninety-seventh year the hall allied with Bolthul, the orcs who had been the enemy in the eighty-ninth, and the orcs who had been the enemy now rode north with the army of Aurenvale, painted and drumming, to throw themselves at the grey wall. They did not take it either.

In the year one hundred the Watcher made peace with Torzul.

It was the third decree of its kind. It was spoken in the hall at dawn in the ordinary way, and the ordinary silence followed. There was no triumph in it. In twelve years of war the Hold of Torzul had lost three farms, a cattle-yard and a good deal of its patience. Aurenvale had lost fifty-one soldiers and the better part of its youth. The great grey wall in the north had not been climbed.

In the same year the hall made its monument. The Watcher, which in decades past had patronised art for its own sake, with banners and thrones and harvests, chose a subject that year with an odd, rueful clarity. The Siege of Torzul, it was called: a great painted panel in the hall, thirty feet long, in which the artist had put the grey wall and the thin line of smoke and a small dark column of soldiers toiling up the ridge. The critics of Luriel, who saw it in the following decade, thought it a work of unusual honesty. It did not show a victory. It showed a siege that had not happened, and the long bitterness of a people who had decided that carrying a thing a long way was the same as doing it.

The Second Host came in the same year.

It was the Watcher's third Sign, and the second time it had used the gift of soldiers, and it did so with a certain austerity. Six more grown men and women appeared on the road to Eddale on a grey morning in the hundredth year. They had the same bearing as the first: the same missing dust on the boots, the same empty room where a history should have been. This time, however, the hall was ready. It had a burial ground and a nursery and a school. It had the great grey painting and the thin red star. It met them at the gate with bread and salt and the oldest of its people to say the words of welcome.

Maeora, who was dead by then, would have said the words. She had died in the winter of ninety-eight, of a cough that took her slowly, in a bed beside the hearth with Edora at her side and the young ones of the Birth-Wave coming and going in the dark. Her last words, the record says, were: "I was given a spear. I think I held it well." Corry, who loved her like the mother he had never had, read the rites. It was the first time that anyone had heard him weep.

It was Edora who spoke at the gate. She was seventy by the reckoning of the roll, bent, white-haired, with a hand trembling on the head of her stick, and she looked at the six newcomers with a long, kind, unsurprised look.

"You will not remember," she said. "Do not try. It is a kindness. I will tell you what I was told. You are a soldier of Aurenvale. You have a spear. You have a place at the hearth. The rest you will make."

One of them, a young man, said in a voice that shook, "Who are you?"

"I am the last of the first of the First," Edora said. "Come in out of the cold."

That same year, in the high meadow, a farmer of Brookham named Marry, a big, slow, quiet man with a plough-hand's shoulders and no taste for fighting, was out seeing to his sheep when the Pale Eye came down out of the hills.

It was the second Pale Eye, or the same one returned, as the old women would say. It was a great grey basilisk with a single milk-white eye that dealt death at a glance. Marry had never held a spear in his life. He had a pitchfork and a crooked sheep-dog and three hundred ewes. The soldiers of Aurenvale were three days' march away. The beast came down the meadow in a long quiet gliding curve, and the dog ran, and Marry, who could not run, stood where he was and looked at the ground.

The record says that the Pale Eye was killed that afternoon by a farmer with a pitchfork. It does not say how. The farmers of Brookham say, to this day, that the basilisk's gaze is deadly only to those who meet it; that Marry, looking at the grass, never met it; that the thing came near and tilted its blind head to find him and he drove the fork up under its jaw with the whole strength of his back. They call him Marry the Strong. He went home, washed his hands, ate his supper and said nothing for three days.

In the hall, Corry heard the story from a runner. He laughed, and then wiped his eyes, and said to the herald, "There. That is the whole of what we have been doing for twelve years. And a farmer does it with a hay-fork before breakfast."

"Do not say it to the Watcher," Jomund said.

"Why not?" said Corry. "It already knows."

The roll for the hundredth year is the shortest in the book. It records that Aurenvale was twenty-five souls, in three villages. It records that Eddale, Brookham and Stanfield stood, each with a hearth and a rider. It records the Siege of Torzul, and the Second Host, and the death of the Pale Eye by the hand of Marry. It records that the harvest was good. Beneath it, in a smaller hand, the herald had written one line: Nothing gained. Nothing lost that was not already gone.

It was perhaps the most honest sentence in the whole of the chronicle, and the Watcher read it without bitterness, and let it stand.

Chapter Thirteen: Turning on the Oldest Friend

"A friend is whoever stood at your side the last time. Be careful whom you decide that was."

(Saurian proverb, Zathixal)

The elves of Serthas, far in the east, rose against Luriel in the hundred and third year, and the world held its breath. It was not much to look at: a city of Harmony and Grace, tired of being governed from the sea, with a new faith in Sylvaen, Spirit of Spring, and a new banner. But a realm of the founding peoples had cracked, and in the cracking made a new thing, and the world did not end. Realms were not unbreakable. They could split like seed-pods, and the pieces could live.

The Watcher watched it happen and thought, for once, about itself. It had kept the Covenant for twenty-eight years. It had given three Signs and spoken thirty-odd decrees. It had seen its people brought to three souls and raised again to twenty-five, and it asked itself a question it did not like. What am I for?

It was a question the Maker had not put to it. The Maker had given the terms, and the terms were plain. Make them prosperous. Make them the only realm left standing. The Watcher had been aiming at the second clause for a hundred years, with the faint, shamed awareness that the clause could not be done. There were seven peoples in the world and now eight, and the Stone grew a little larger each year, a vermilion bruise on the evening sky. The Watcher had counted. If it did nothing, the Stone would fall. If it fought, it would lose soldiers, but it might at least reduce the number of its rivals, one by one.

It looked at the board and chose a name. It was an old name. It was the name of the first friend it had ever made.

The hall declares war on the Dominion of Zathixal.

Jomund, who was now thirty-eight, took the decree in the hour before dawn, and did not at first believe he had heard it.

He lay in the dark with the sentence in his head, whole, plain and not his own, and for a very long time he did not move. He had been herald for twenty-eight years. He had spoken war on Torzul and war on Bolthul and a dozen peaces. He had spoken the founding of Stanfield. He had never been asked to say this.

The saurians of Zathixal were the first allies of Aurenvale. In the first year of the world, before the great hall had its roof, the Watcher had spoken an alliance with the lizard-folk, and it had held. When Bolthul had come against Eddale in the thirty-ninth year, the saurians had marched to its defence. When Aurenvale was three souls at the nadir of its history, the saurians had not attacked. They had not gloated. They had simply been, in the dark of those years, a steady light on the north-eastern horizon, and the old people of the hall, who remembered or half-remembered them, said it as a prayer: Veritas keep the lizards. They were a people of law, who valued honour and kept their bargains, and who had never, in a hundred years, done Aurenvale any harm.

He rose. He dressed in the herald's grey. He went down into the hall and stood at the hearth at sunrise with the staff in both hands, and the hall filled around him with its soldiers and its children and its old, and he said the words.

There was a silence. It went on for a long time.

Then Corry, from the back of the hall, said, "Say it again."

"The hall declares war on the Dominion of Zathixal."

"I heard you," said Corry. "I wanted to hear whether you would say it the same way twice."

"I will say it the same way every time," said the herald. His voice shook. "That is the whole of my office."

It was Corry's war, as it would be, in one way or another, for the rest of his life.

They marched. The saurians met them in the pine-forests in the first week of autumn, tall, lithe, scaled in russet and green, with long curved swords and the cold unblinking dignity of their kind. They fought without battle-cries. They did not need them. They fought as a people who had been taught, from the egg, that the law was the only thing worth dying for. The army of Aurenvale burned two hamlets. The army of Zathixal burned a farm in Brookham. In the dark of the year, in a village by the northern pass, a young soldier named Alold, a veteran by the age of nineteen, found a saurian child in the ashes of a house and stood over it for a long time with his sword in his hand, and then, without anyone seeing, put the sword down and carried the child to the road.

He did not tell anyone. Nobody knew until he died.

In the hundred and sixth year, Bolthul joined Aurenvale's side in the war.

It was a sour partnership. The orcs who had once marched against Eddale in the name of Krukka, and who in the ninety-seventh year had become its ally, now rode north with drums and ancestor-skulls to fight against the saurians. They were an unlovely army. They looted. They burned what Corry had asked them not to burn. They sang at night in a tongue that no one in the hall of Eddale could follow, a low, rhythmic, guttural chant that went on until dawn.

In the hundred and ninth year the Watcher made an alliance with the elves of Luriel, who had been its enemy in the twelfth year and the twenty-eighth, who had been its ally in the forty-ninth, and who were now, in the midst of the long fracturing of the east, the strongest realm in the world. It was a sensible alliance. It was also, though no one in the hall said so, a little too convenient, and the old soldiers shook their heads at it in the evenings over the fire.

The year of Roric was the hundred and tenth.

He was twenty-three years old, a soldier of the Second Host: one of the six who had appeared on the road in the hundredth year with a spear in his hand and nothing in his head. He was tall and fair and quick, and he had a way of grinning into the sun that made the young recruits want to follow him. He had made soldiers laugh, and had, by every account, been a fool about a girl named Edry in Brookham.

Shrikka came back in the spring.

The grey spider that a young soldier had killed in the eighty-seventh year was a story, a legend, a name. Another Shrikka had taken the old nest. Or the same had returned; the old women of Brookham, who had their own opinions, said that the spider's name was a crown that monsters passed on, and that when one fell another picked it up. Whatever the truth, a great grey shape came down out of the northern hills in the third month of the year, and by the fifth it had taken a shepherd, two children and the herald's old dog.

The hall marched, as it always did, and Corry, who was seventy by any reckoning and leaning on a stick, would not be held back. He went with the soldiers as far as the foothills and no farther, and sat on a rock at the edge of the birchwood, watching the long grey line of the spider's silk glitter in the morning light. He had been general for nineteen years. He had never killed a giant. He did not suppose he would.

Roric did.

He was not the first to see her. He was not the strongest of the company. He was twenty-three, and he had been given five hard victories in the preceding months, a bandit chief, a troll, two goblins and a raider, and he came up the ridge with them as a man comes up a hill he has climbed before. When she reared out of the nest, grey and vast and silent, with the eight black eyes catching the morning, the soldiers broke, as soldiers do. Roric did not. He stood in her shadow with a long gun in his hands and fired, and the shot took her in the throat, and he drew his sword and went in under her legs.

He killed her. The record says so, and the record is all there is. The spider went down in a great heap of grey legs and silk, and the hills went silent. Roric stood over her with his sword in his hands and a long red gash down his side, and he looked up at the ridge, where Corry sat on his rock, and he grinned.

Then he sat down, quite slowly, in the grass.

They carried him home on a litter. It took two days. He did not speak much. Once, on the second night, he asked Corry whether the spider had been a she or a he, and Corry, who did not know, said, "She."

"I thought so," Roric said. "She had a face like my aunt."

"You have no aunt."

"No," said Roric. "That is true. It was a good face, though."

He died on the third morning, as the litter came down the road into Eddale. The herald was waiting at the gate with the staff in his hands, and Edora, who was nearly eighty now, sat on the bench beside the well where she had sat for so many years. Roric was very quiet, and then he was not there.

They buried him on the hill. They set a stone on his grave. It was a plain stone, grey and weathered, and the herald cut the letters himself, in the plain grey Eddish of the hall: Roric. He killed Shrikka and did not come home. The stone is not in the roll, which says only that in the hundred and tenth year the hero Roric slew Shrikka and fell the same year after five victories. But the old women of Brookham keep it, and in a hundred and ten years, in the whole record of the world, there was no one who ever said it better.

The Watcher did not give a Sign in the hundred and tenth year.

It had thought about it. It had stood above the grave on the hill with a feeling it did not trust, and it had thought of the thing it might do: a word to the soldiers, a gift to the hall, a miracle of the kind that had put six strangers on the road, and eight children in their cradles, and six more strangers on the road a second time. It was permitted. The decade had closed. The Covenant said one Sign a decade, at most, and the Watcher had spent its third in the hundredth year.

It did not. It looked at the roll. It looked at the great gap in the line where Roric's name sat, between the dead and the unborn. It looked at forty-nine souls in the hall, at seventeen soldiers, at Edora's white head bowed over the well, at the stone on the hill. It thought that a Sign would be easy, and it was beginning, after a hundred and ten years, to understand that easy was exactly the thing it had sworn against.

"You are not giving one," said the Maker.

"No."

"The hall would have welcomed it."

"They would have taken a gift. They would have called it grace." The Watcher paused. "I would have called it grace. I am no longer sure what the word means."

"Is that a failure?"

"I do not know." The Watcher looked down at the small bright lamp of Eddale in the central highlands, and the long grey dark around it, and the red star over all of it, vast now, and the colour of a coal. "I think a people can only be as strong as the thing it has lost. I think I have been trying for a hundred years to give them something to lose that is not themselves."

"And?"

"And I think the answer is that I cannot." It was very quiet. "I think there is nothing I can give them that is not simply more of me."

At its peak, Aurenvale held one hundred and thirty-five souls.

That was the number the clerks of the hall wrote in the great ledger in the spring of the Year 140, and then underlined, and then, because it seemed insufficient, wrote again in the margin in red. It was the highest count in the realm's history, nearly three times the number it had held thirty years before. She was not a priest, she said, but she had been taught by priests, in a place far to the south, in a village long since fallen into ruin. She was a follower of Caspian. Caspian, she said, was not an old god. He was an old name for a new idea. She carried it in a book, bound in grey leather, that nobody in Stanfield could read the first time.

"What does it say?" asked the innkeeper.

"It says," said the stranger, "that there is a god of ambition and glory whom men call the Rising Crown. He is not the Lord of Order, who gives to those who serve. He is the god of those who stand up. He asks nothing of you but that you have the courage to ask for more."

"We don't have crowns," said the innkeeper, in some confusion. "This isn't a place with crowns."

"No," said the stranger. "That is the point. It is a crown of the spirit. A crown on every head that dares to lift it."

The innkeeper looked at her for a while. Then he poured her a drink, and said that she could speak in the barn on Thursday if she liked, but that Isbert would be there, and Isbert did not hold with preachers.

On Thursday Isbert came, and sat at the back with his arms folded, and listened with the squint. When she had finished, he said only: "I've nothing against your god."

"I'm glad."

"But I'll tell you something. I've served the Lord of Order for twenty years. He never lied to me once." Isbert's voice was quite gentle. "He never asked me anything either."

"Maybe," said the woman softly, "that is what you have been missing."

Chapter Seventeen: The Rising Crown

"We did not rise against the sky. We rose against the road that led down from it."

(Said to be the last sentence written by a priest of Stanfield)

In the Year 142 the Watcher declared its thirteenth war on Zathixal, and the Maker, who had been counting, said nothing at all.

Two other things happened that year that the hall of Eddale did not think were connected. The dwarves of Torzul, who had been a quiet northern hold for seventy years, sent an envoy to the Dominion of Zathixal and joined its side. And in the high, clean country of the east the elves of Luriel entered the Future Age, a realm of glass and silent lights and engines that hummed, and the saurians of the Dominion, who had five lamps and a legal code, looked at the east and then at the south and drew from this a sensible inference, which was that the world was becoming a place with no room in it for anyone who was small.

In Stanfield, in the spring of the next year, the levy came: twenty-two men. It was the largest ever demanded of the village. Nobody went. Isbert did not call for it. It was just that on the morning the soldiers were to assemble in the square, the square was empty, and the herald's clerk, who had come out from Eddale with a clipboard, found twenty-two doors shut, and behind each of them a quiet household sitting around a table.

He wrote that down too.

The Watcher saw the rebellion only when it was already a fact. In the Year 144, on the first morning of the first month, the people of Stanfield assembled in their square. Isbert stood on a cart. He had put on his old grey coat, and in his lapel, for the first time in fifteen years, the blue ribbon from the hall.

"I am not a king," he said. "There are no kings. I am a soldier of the realm and I have served it, and so has every one of you. We have served the road from Eddale for fifty years. A murmur ran through the crowd. The kingdom of what, someone asked.

"Of Stanfield," said Isbert. "Under Caspian, the Rising Crown."

The woman in the brown coat, who had lived in the village for three years now and taught half its children to read, lifted the grey book. Over her head a banner went up that someone had sewn in the night: a plain white crown on a dark blue field, the crown and no head under it. A cheer went up, uncertain at first and then enormous, the cheer of people who have been quiet for a very long time.

There is no chronicle of Aurenvale that tells this part well. The hall of Eddale kept no proper account of it, because the hall of Eddale did not believe it. In the capital, in the first hour, the news was brought to the herald, who read it twice and said nothing. It was brought to General Wilold, who was past sixty, and who looked at the courier for a long time and then asked, in a flat voice, "Isbert?"

"Yes, General."

"The Isbert that took the ravine?"

"Yes."

"Against the saurians, General?"

"No," said Wilold. "Against Stanfield."

The fighting that followed lasted a few months, and it was the worst thing that had ever happened to Aurenvale.

It was not the numbers, though they were bad enough. It was the faces. A soldier of Eddale who had been taught at the same temple school as a soldier of Stanfield met him on a road between hedges, in the dawn, and each of them recognised the other. Each of them raised a rifle, and each of them held it for a very long time. In the first week there were forty-one such incidents in the army's own reports. By the third week there were no more, because by then, on each side, the men who could not do it had been replaced by the ones who could.

Isbert was a very great soldier. He had been a hero in the Year 129, and he had not stopped being one. In the first months of the rising he won eight victories, each of them against an Aurenvale company that had been sent to bring him in, each of them a thing nobody in Eddale had thought possible. He fought with the squint. He fought, the survivors said, like a man carrying something. He never once raised his voice. He killed no one who had thrown down a weapon, and the soldiers of Eddale, who had been trained to despise a traitor, found themselves unable to despise him.

"He's fighting like a man who wants to be stopped," said a sergeant of the Fifth Host one evening, to nobody in particular, in the smoke of a burned barn. "Like he's waiting for somebody to say stop."

"Nobody's going to say stop," said another.

"No," said the sergeant. "That's the trouble."

It was in the high summer of that year that Isbert fell.

Nobody could say, afterward, precisely how. The reports in the hall of Eddale were confused and mutually contradictory. One said it was a volley from a hedge. One said it was a single shot from a young soldier of the Fifth Host, who had been a child at the temple school with Isbert's younger daughter, and who had never been able to say the name afterward. One said it was a fever, and one said it was exhaustion, and one, written in a neat hand by a clerk who had never left Eddale, said: the traitor Isbert was slain in the field. However he fell, the roll of the hall set it down in its dry way, under the Year 144: Isbert, hero of the Kingdom of Stanfield, has fallen after eight victories.

His wife found him at the edge of the road with the ribbon in his hand.

She sat beside him in the dust for a very long time. The soldiers of Eddale, who had come up the lane in a column, stopped at a respectful distance, with their rifles lowered, and not one of them spoke. It was a hot, still day. The hedgerows hummed. When at last she stood, she was not weeping. She folded the ribbon in her palm. She looked at the soldiers, and she said, in a clear, ordinary, level voice, "He didn't want any of this. He wanted them to hear him."

"Ma'am," said a young sergeant, and stopped.

"Tell them," she said. "Tell the herald. Tell whoever is up there. Tell them he asked."

She walked back to Stanfield alone.

The Kingdom of Stanfield lasted three years, if you count by the calendar, and it lasted rather less than that, if you count by anything else.

With Isbert dead there was nobody to lead it, and nobody that the village could agree on. The priests of Caspian, who had been so sure in the square, turned out to be more a matter of voice than of spine. They argued over the ribbon. They argued over the banner. They argued over whether the god of ambition might be satisfied with a smaller crown, and then with no crown, and then with a kind of sullen quiet. The harvest of the Year 144 came in thin because half the farmers were in the militia. The harvest of the Year 145 came in thinner, because the militia was the half that was left. In the Year 145 the Watcher declared another war on Zathixal, and the army marched north again, and left Stanfield alone for a season, because there was nothing in Stanfield that anyone in Eddale could bear to attack.

It was not conquered. This is a thing the Watcher would insist upon in the long reckoning afterward, with a stubbornness that surprised the Maker. Aurenvale did not take Stanfield. It was simply that Stanfield, deprived of its one great soldier and its one great idea, could not keep itself. People left. Some went north. Some went back to Eddale and were received without questions. Some went to Brookham and said nothing. A few went south to the coast and were not heard of again. The school closed. The market closed. The woman in the brown coat, who had been so certain, was seen on the road one morning in the Year 146, walking away from the village with her case of books, and when someone asked where she was going she said, "I don't know. I thought it would be easier."

In the Year 147 the kingdom of Stanfield fell into ruin. The last family left in the autumn. The banner on the square hung in the rain until it rotted off its pole. The soldiers of Eddale, when at last they marched through, found the streets empty, the doors open, and in the window of the house at the end of the lane, where a retired veteran had once sat with a pipe, a grey kettle on a cold stove, set out for a guest.

"Show me," said the Maker.

There was the count: a hundred and thirty-five, then a hundred and twenty, then fewer. There were the soldiers who had gone to Stanfield and the ones who had not come back. "General?" said the adjutant.

Wilold did not answer. He was looking at the light.

"There."

She read it anyway, in her clear dry voice, to the people who had come to hear.

Chapter Eighteen: Nimnook

"A war you cannot win is only a long way of walking home."

(Marching saying of the Eddale garrison, Year 150s)

In the hundred and forty-eighth year the Watcher made its fifteenth declaration of war upon the Dominion of Zathixal, and in the same year it understood, with the slow horror of a careful mind, that it had been making the same mistake every three years since the hundred and third.

The report that taught it was plain. Zathixal had grown. Where the Watcher had once counted three saurian souls behind a ruined temple, it now counted twenty-three, spread across five hamlets (Zathixal, Zathzun, Iskthal, Thexaizzar and Raszzizzar), each in a high fold of the pines where no wagon could climb and no cannon be dragged. Aurenvale's generals had marched into those folds with the best soldiers of three ages. They had burned one hamlet. By the time the smoke rose the war was over, as every one of those wars was over, because a people who can be burned out of one valley simply live in the next, and the Spine does not care whose banner is on the pass.

Not one hamlet kept. Not one border moved.

The Watcher sat above the world in the place that is not a place, and it did what it had not done since the Orphan Years. It laid the whole continent out before it and counted.

There were more realms now than at the beginning. Peoples had a way of splitting; a city grew proud, a priest found a different god, and one realm became two. The Watcher had watched it happen to Luriel and Bolthul and Nobmuck and Dobwhistle, and had thought of it, mostly, as other people's trouble. Now it saw what the splitting meant. Every realm that cracked was a realm that would have to be ended separately. The Maker's terms had not changed. Make them prosperous and the only realm left standing. But the number of realms to stand over was not shrinking. It was growing.

In the corner of every sky the red star hung low. A child in Eddale could have covered it with a fingernail in the first year, and could not have covered it with a thumb now. The humans called it the Falling Star, and the older soldiers, who were fond of omens, called it Aurelan's Eye and touched their collars when they said it. The elves called it the Wanderer. In Bolthul it was Krukka's Drumstone, and the shamans said that one day the god would strike it and the whole sky would ring. The Watcher noticed that no people called it anything kind.

It did not look at the star for long. It looked at one town on a long northern peninsula, under a lone mountain knot, in a country of temperate forest.

The Burrow of Nimnook had risen against Dobwhistle in the hundred and thirty-fifth year, worshipping the Deep Ancestors, and had come back to Gorim Gemheart in the hundred and forty-third. It had one town and fifty-four souls. The road to it ran over ground a column could cross in a season.

One town, thought the Watcher. Not five hamlets scattered across a mountain range. A people with a single heart, and a heart can be reached.

It was the kind of thought the Watcher had been proud of for a hundred and forty-eight years, and it would be wrong.

The herald that decade was named Annric, though the soldiers called him Annric the Younger, because the first Annric had been a child in the days of the First Host and this one was his namesake and a good deal taller. He stood in the great hall at Eddale on the first morning of the hundred and fifty-first year and read the decree in the clipped, even voice that heralds learn so the words will not belong to them.

"The Watcher decrees war upon the Burrow of Nimnook. The army will muster at the Birchgate by the thaw."

A generation ago there would have been a murmur. But the hall had listened to fourteen wars on the saurians, and the room that heard this one was quiet in the way of a room that has stopped being surprised.

It was a bad year to be at war in two places. That same season the Warren of Nobstink, a goblin realm that had risen against Nobmuck twelve years before and served Zhulka, Mother of Ash, sent a holy war up the roads against Aurenvale. The goblins burned outlying farms, drove off cattle, and screamed their mother's name at the walls of Brookham until the garrison rolled out and the expedition scattered. At the end of the year, far to the north, the Hold of Torzul fell into ruin, and its hero Dwagrim, after seven victories, fell with it. Aurenvale had once besieged those dwarves for nothing. Nobody in Eddale marked the passing; the news arrived in the week of the cheese tax.

What the soldiers found in the country of the gnomes was a land arranged so that they would find nothing in it.

The forest was beautiful and wet. The road was a bed of packed leaf-mould that took a wheel and gave it back. There were farms, small and tidy, with fences built to the height of a gnome's elbow and doors you had to kneel to enter. They were empty. The kettles were still warm in three of them. A column that sets fire to a farm and finds the cat watching from a stump feels something it cannot quite name, and the army burned eleven before it understood that every farm had been left with a lamp lit in the window, as an invitation or a joke.

The Burrow itself lay beneath a long round hill at the foot of the mountain knot. There was a town above ground, built of stone as grey as the hill, and a much larger town below it, and the soldiers who tried to reach the second through the first learned things about gnomish carpentry that they did not enjoy.

The Burrow kept a Book of Grudges, which was not a metaphor. It was a great vellum volume bound in copper, kept in a niche beside Gorim's altar, and every injury that any gnome or any gnome's grandmother had suffered at the hand of any other people was written in it, in small neat hands, with the date and the name. It had been carried out of Dobwhistle in a wheelbarrow, under a blanket, by a woman named Pibble who had personally been wronged by a tax collector. Aurenvale was in it three times already. The keeper of the Book, a calm grey-bearded gnome who was the nearest thing the Burrow had to a general, added a fourth entry on the day the first farm burned.

Fizfen, who was young and quick and the best-known fighter in Nimnook, stood beside him as the page was inked.

"They will have a long list of what we did to them," said Fizfen.

"They have nothing," said the keeper. "That is why it is a war. Wars between people with lists end quickly."

"Tok lo," said Fizfen, which was the Burrow's motto and meant, more or less, we endure, and the keeper blotted the page.

The army spent one season in the country of Nimnook. It took one outlying hamlet that held a mill and eleven stubborn gnomes, who retreated into the wheel-pit and could not be dislodged. It lost forty horses to a bog marked on no map and a dozen soldiers to small arrows from places that turned out, afterward, to be hollow trees. In the autumn the Watcher saw that the town below the hill was not going to open, that no winter siege could be afforded, and that nothing in the campaign would ever resolve into a battle in which Aurenvale's guns could be brought to bear.

It decreed peace.

The war cost the Burrow three souls; Nimnook, which had numbered fifty-four, now numbered fifty-one, and a people who write their dead into the margins of a grudge-book are not inclined to forgive. It cost Aurenvale more, in ways the ledgers did not measure.

In the hundred and fifty-third year the old general laid down his cloak.

The record gives no battle for Wilold's ending. It gives only that in the autumn, in the hall of Eddale, he unpinned the grey cloak and held it out to the herald and said in a voice meant for the back row, "Somebody young, please. My knees have declared peace with the Watcher and I do not intend to break it."

The hall found somebody young. Her name was Joda, a lieutenant of the Brookham garrison, twenty-six years old, who that season won six fine victories against raiders and beasts and, in one extraordinary night action, a column of Nobstink goblins who found themselves to their alarm on the wrong side of a river. The hall loved her at once. In her first month she was seen at the Tally Wall, the long plaster wall on which the generals of Aurenvale had their names cut in a column, running her thumb over the grooves.

"There's a lot of room under Wilold," she said.

"There is," said Annric. "There always is. They do not tend to need it."

She fell the same year. There was no great beast. A skirmish at a ford, a bolt out of the reeds, and the grey cloak lay in the mud with a very ordinary stain. The old stone-cutter, Tomold, whose great-grandfather had cut the first names, came to the Tally Wall with his mallet and added JODA beneath WILOLD, and beside it the figure 6, because by then the hall had taken to carving the number.

The Watcher watched him work. It had always known that generals died young. It had not understood until that morning that it had built a machine that required them to.

Elda took the cloak in the hundred and fifty-fourth year, and they called her Swift, because she had been the first soldier in Aurenvale to get from Eddale to the Birchgate in a day and a half. She was practical, quiet, a little deaf from the cannon, and fond of a black bread she baked herself. On her first morning in the war-room she asked what the objective was, and nobody could tell her. She cut a piece of her black bread and offered half to the staff officer, and said she had not thought so.

The second war on the gnomes was almost exactly the first. The farms were empty, the lamps lit, the bog where it had been. The army carried marsh-planks this time and burned fifteen farms instead of eleven, and Nimnook sank to forty-eight, and Fizfen's name began to be spoken in the kitchens of Eddale with the dread and affection people give an enemy they have never seen.

In the hundred and fifty-sixth year a lean man of the high meadows named Corwin, whom the garrison called Tough, struck the last blow on the Pale Eye, the basilisk that had stalked the middle lands since the days of the first generals. He came home with stone-dust in his collar and a limp that never healed, and the herald announced it so cheerfully that the whole hall forgot for an afternoon what the army was for.

The year after, the Watcher decreed an alliance with Luriel. It was a small, strange kindness. The elves had been Aurenvale's enemies in the twelfth year and the twenty-eighth, and friends in the forty-ninth and the hundred and ninth, and their envoys came to Eddale tall and quiet in the pale blue their Circle preferred, and were shown the Tally Wall as a courtesy, the way one might show guests the family portraits.

The envoy was a woman named Lirael. She walked the length of the wall in silence, reading the names and the numbers beside them, and looked for a long moment through the open door at the Wanderer, red and patient over the hills of Eddale. "Among us," she said gently, "a general is a woman who lives long enough to be unremarkable."

Chapter Nineteen: The Golden Age

"The harvest came in so thick that year the mice sat down and wept."

(Folk rhyme of the Eddish country, Year 160s)

The orcs were the first to show what a giant looks like when it comes apart.

The Horde of Bolthul had been the largest realm of the south, a country of clover-meadows and war-drums, and Durgoth was its greatest city, eighty-seven souls strong and never quite at ease beneath the Horde. In the hundred and fifty-eighth year it stopped pretending. A shaman climbed the steps of the Skull-Hall and told the captains that Krukka had been replaced in the night by Ghorak the Skullfather, god of war and conquest, and that anyone who wished to remain loyal to Bolthul could do so from the other side of the river.

Most of the city went with him. Bolthul, which had numbered a hundred and eleven, was left with seven.

The Watcher counted it twice. It had never seen a realm go from a hundred and eleven to seven in a single year without an army marching on it, and it understood, belatedly, that this was the most important thing it had ever seen. That was how the world ended its giants. Not on a battlefield. A giant fell apart.

The same year the Horde of Kragdak sent a war-band against Aurenvale's southern farms. It was beaten at the lemon groves, and the garrison went home smelling of crushed fruit. But the Watcher was not looking at the lemon groves. It was looking at Aurenvale's own streets, where something was wrong.

The taxes had risen, and the soldiers who marched north did not come back in the numbers that went. The harvest had been middling, then poor, then middling again. In the inns of Brookham and along the Birchgate road people had begun to say that the Watcher's wars were an expensive way of getting nowhere. In the spring of the hundred and fifty-ninth year someone painted a crowned figure on the side of a granary in Eddale, with a small red star above it. Nobody in Aurenvale had worn a crown in all its history. Everyone knew what the painting meant. It meant Stanfield, and Isbert, and the Rising Crown, a village that had been Aurenvale's own and had declared itself a kingdom and burned.

The painting was scrubbed off in an hour. But the hall had seen it.

"The people are not rebellious," Annric told the Watcher, in the way a servant brings a small coin to a large table. "Not yet. They are tired. The inns are full of men saying that nothing good can come of it."

"Of what?"

"Of the wars, my lord. Of everything."

The Watcher considered it. It could not strike a rival. It could not curse, or convert, or declare a war for another's sake. Wars were won by soldiers, not gods. But its own people were not another realm, and for its own the Covenant permitted, once in a decade, a Sign. It had skipped the Sign of the hundred and fiftieth year, thinking it had no right to a miracle on behalf of a people whose wars it was losing. Now it saw that the people did not need a victory. They needed a harvest.

So in the hundred and sixtieth year it declared again its war upon Nimnook, because it did not yet know what else to do, and in the same breath it gave the Sign. The people called it the Golden Age.

It began in a single night. The farmers of Aurenvale woke in the first week of the planting to a smell none of them could place until a girl in Brookham put it into words: it was the smell of wheat already ripe. The fields stood tall and gold, three weeks early, and when the reapers came out the stalks bent under the sickles as if glad. The granaries filled, and filled again. By the end of that summer there was no one in Eddale who had not eaten white bread, and the bakers of the market square, who had sold black loaves at a loss for ten years, put out trestle tables and gave bread away until their flour ran dry.

The streets went quiet. That was the strangest part. A full belly does not make a saint of anyone, but it removes a great many reasons to shout. The brawls in the market stopped. The informers had nothing to inform on. The man who had painted the crowned figure was found, in the end, to be a decorator's apprentice, and was given a commission to paint a whole wall in the temple of Aurelan instead, and did it so well that the priests wept.

Not everyone was singing.

In the Warren of Nobstink the goblin painters finished a very large work called Victory over Aurenvale. It showed, in red and black, a goblin captain on the broken gate of a human city, holding up an enormous skull. Nobstink had gone to war on Aurenvale nine years before and been driven from the walls of Brookham, and had decided, as people do, that if the war had no victory in it the painting would have to supply one. They hung it in the Skull-Hall and held a feast beneath it.

In the orc country, the other side of the same year was being written in red. A warrior of Durgoth named Snagnak, tall and silent, with ancestor-skulls braided into his hair, had won ten great battles for the new Horde, each against a larger force than the last. The shamans said Ghorak walked beside him. In the hundred and sixtieth year, in the season that Aurenvale's wheat stood ripe, Snagnak fell in the tenth victory, and the whole of Durgoth went silent for three days and then began to drum. It was merely the way of the age. Heroes came out of nowhere, fought like fire, and were gone, and the world was filling with the light of short comets.

The Golden Age gave the art back to Aurenvale. For a long time the hall had patronised only the arts of war, the Siege of this and the Banner of that. In the hundred and sixty-third year the Watcher decreed something else, and the painters of Eddale, who had become numerous because there was bread enough to raise a class of them, worked three seasons in the square before the temple. What they made was The Radiant Harvest of Aurenvale, and it was nothing like the old murals. No throne, no banner, no army. A field at dusk, full of people bent to the cutting, with a child asleep in a basket at the edge of the frame. The wheat was painted so gold that people walked in from three villages to see it.

In the hundred and sixty-second year a young soldier named Maeric, twenty years old, had struck the last blow on Shrikka, the great spider of the high birch, who had come for Eddale again and again and never yet been killed for good. The hall gave him a whole day of festival. In the hundred and sixty-fifth year a rebel named Ugdush led some of Durgoth's orcs out of their own city and founded the Horde of Bolgor, which would be among the smallest realms in the world. In the hundred and sixty-seventh year a young man named Maery, named for one of the old orphans, struck down Skyrend the thunderbird, and in the same year Corwin, who had ended the Pale Eye, fell in the field after six victories, and the stone-cutter carved his figure by the generals, though he had never worn the cloak.

It was a good decade. Nobody who lived it said otherwise. It was only that on certain evenings, when the harvest suppers were over and the singing had died, some of the old men sitting on their doorsteps looked up.

In the hundred and sixty-ninth year the Age changed.

Aurenvale had been Modern since the hundred and twentieth year, a time of wires and engines, and thought it knew what came next. It did not. The Future Age came in the spring, in a month. The glass came first. The masons of Eddale, who had laid stone for a century and a half, found that a certain sand from the river bank, heated a certain way and drawn into sheets, would stand without a frame. In six weeks the first spire was up, a slender thing of translucent green seven storeys tall, with light running through it at dusk like water through a leaf. By autumn there were nine.

The soldiers got their armour. It was not metal. It was layered grey plating over a lattice that clicked when the soldier moved, and it turned a woman of five feet four into a thing that could carry a cannon on one arm. The guns became lights. In the barracks a corporal fired one at a target for the first time and then sat quite still for a quarter of an hour with the weapon across his knees, saying nothing.

Eddale had been six souls once. Then three. Now it had spires. There were children in the market who would not believe the realm had ever been small. It was exactly what the Watcher had been aiming at for a hundred and sixty-nine years. The Maker had said make them prosperous, and the Watcher had.

That same spring Elda fell. She had been general fifteen years, longer than any since Wilold, and in all that time she had kept every promise she made to the garrison. She fell on a wet hillside in Nimnook's forest, in the ninth month of the war, struck by something small and unseen, in a moment so ordinary that the soldiers beside her did not at first understand. She had five victories. They found half a loaf of black bread in her satchel and, by garrison custom, shared it among the nearest twelve.

The cloak went to Maery.

On the last night of that year the Watcher set out its arithmetic as plainly as if writing for a clerk. The realm was prosperous. The realm was powerful. And it had waged five wars on the gnomes of Nimnook and had not taken a hamlet.

The Maker's terms were not a request for greatness. They were a request for an ending, and the Watcher had spent a hundred and sixty-nine years refusing, with perfect integrity, to do the things endings required. It had refused to curse. It had refused to strike. It had told itself this was a virtue, and in the nights, when the star shone through the high cloud, it could no longer tell whether it was a virtue or a very elegant way of failing.

"You are quiet," said the Maker.

The Watcher did not turn. The Maker's presence had never been a matter of turning; it simply was, as a lamp is when lit. It held something that might have been a cup.

"I was thinking of the year Maery slew Skyrend."

"You are always thinking about heroes."

"They are what I have." The Watcher looked at the star, which was the size now of a plum held at arm's length. "I have been counting, Maker. I do not think I will be the last."

"You do not know that."

"I know the arithmetic. Every year there are more of them, not fewer. Every war I wage ends in the burning of a barn. And the star comes down."

They buried Annmund on the south slope below the temple, where the lemon trees come up to the edge of the hill, and the whole of Eddale stood in the rain to do it.

It was the largest funeral the town had ever held, and it was held in a town that had learned to bury well. The herald read them in the flat voice of a man announcing a harvest, because that was how Annmund had liked to hear things said. When the list was done there was a silence in which you could hear the rain on forty thousand leaves, and then the soldiers, twenty-six of them standing, lowered their spears, and the line of them went down the hill like a wave going out.

Above all that, in the place that is not a place, the Watcher stood with its hands behind it and did not lower anything.

Wars are won by soldiers, not by gods. It had sworn that. It had sworn it in the empty hall with six frightened children for witnesses, and it had held to it, and it had lost a general a decade for the keeping.

"There is a kind of friend you do not recognise until everyone else has gone."

(Elvish proverb, quoted in the Luriel annals)

The winter of 197 was the mildest anyone in Eddale could remember, and the town took it as an omen and could not decide which kind.

And all of it, the mild winter and the quiet halls and the ordinary bread, lived under the star.

Nobody believed it would fall. Everybody believed it would fall. This is the usual condition of a people before the end of the world.

In Eddale a girl called Edry, named for the old herald of the Orphan Years, who was nineteen and had the clearest voice in the choir, was made herald in the Year 199.

It was an accident, in the way such things are accidents. The old herald, Annric the Younger, a stooped, ink-fingered man who had read the Watcher's decrees since the Year 151, took a fall on the temple steps and broke his hip, and the hall, after an hour of embarrassment, sent for Edry, who was the only person in Eddale who could read aloud without her voice going flat. She read the decree of the Year 199 in a clear, high voice that cracked on a single word, and nobody ever mentioned it.

But that was two years off. In the Year 197, the Watcher did not decree. Decrees came every three years, and the next was due in the Year 199, and so the Year 197 and the Year 198 were quiet, the quiet of a house in which something has been decided and not yet said.

Isan used them. She walked the walls at dawn. She drilled her twenty-seven soldiers on the east terrace, in sunlight so bright it made the red star disappear, and she stood at the end of each drill-line afterwards and thanked them, one by one, by name. She had begun to do this in the Year 196 and nobody had asked why. A sergeant, a hard woman called Maera whom Isan had known since the patrols, asked her once, in the barracks, late, over cold tea.

"You're counting again," said Maera.

"Always."

"You think there's something to count toward?"

Isan looked into her cup. "I think," she said slowly, "that someone ought to know their names. In case there's a day when somebody asks."

The sergeant did not answer. After a while she put her hand over Isan's, a thing she had not done in thirty years of friendship, and they sat like that until the lamp burned low.

In the Year 198 the orcs of Durgoth, who had been Bolthul's greatest city and Bolthul's greatest rebels, turned their faces from Ghorak the Skullfather, and were converted back to Krukka, the Chaos-Drum, by priests who came, it is said, with no army, only a drum.

The Watcher went to its high place and looked, and thought about what it was permitted.

It thought now: I could break it.

If I break it now, what was it for? it asked itself. And then, because it was fair: And if I keep it, what is it for?

It had no answer. It had, for the first time in all those years, only the practice of the oath, bare of justification: a habit become a spine.

It kept the Covenant.

The decree of the Year 199 was read in the hall at Eddale by a nineteen-year-old with a cracked voice.

The hall was full. It was always full, now. There were three hundred chairs and four hundred standing, because the whole realm had come, a hundred and ten souls, soldiers and clerks and children on shoulders, as if to be present for a thing was to hold it. Isan stood at the front with her cloak altered, and beside her stood the old herald, leaning on a cane, who had insisted on coming. The Eternal Banner hung over the door. Above all of them, on the lintel, someone had carved, in the first year of the world, the words that were still there: We were six. Write that down, and then write that we were enough.

Edry unrolled the scroll. Her hands shook; she steadied them against the lectern.

"By the Watcher's word," she read, "in the Year One Hundred and Ninety-Nine: Aurenvale makes alliance with the Realm of Luriel."

There was silence.

It was not the silence of surprise. They had a hundred and ninety-nine years of decrees behind them, and they knew the music of them, and in this one there was something they had not heard in any: the sound of a door being closed gently from the inside.

Alliance with Luriel. They had been allied before, in the Year 49 and in 157 and again in 184; they had been at war with the elves, too, in the Year 12 and in the Year 28, when the elves had been the long silver enemy at the edge of the map. A people the Watcher had once fought. The elves had tended their orchards across two centuries of other people's wars, and they had never wanted anything but to be allowed to remain.

The decree said nothing else. It was a short scroll. The Watcher, in its high place, had spent three months writing it, and in the end, there was nothing to add.

The envoy from Luriel came to Eddale in the third week of the Year 199, travelling with six guards and no banner, and she was the oldest elf anyone in the hall had ever seen.

Her name is not in the record. She stood in the hall beneath the lintel and she said, in a thin, exact Eddish, that Luriel accepted the alliance, that Luriel had waited a long time to be asked, and that Luriel would be glad, now, to be of use.

"Of use how?" asked Isan. She had learned to be blunt with elves. They respected it.

"We do not know," said the envoy. "That is why we are glad." She looked up, and the red star was visible through the hall's high window, even in daylight now, a smear of rust in the blue. "My grandmother believed that the Wanderer had come to tell us something. She said it was a patient thing, and that when the day came we would understand it. I am old. I have waited for it for two hundred winters, or so it feels. I would rather wait among friends."

The old herald, with his cane, made a small sound. Isan turned.

"Sir?"

"Nothing." He was wiping his eyes with a sleeve. "I was thinking that I have read seventeen decrees in this hall. I thought every one of them was a battle. I thought we were making war, and we were not, were we? We were only finding who we could stand beside."

"It took us a long time," said the envoy of Luriel, gently.

"Two hundred years," said the herald.

"Yes. Well." The envoy smiled. "Most things do."

That same year, the goblins of Nobstink split.

It was the nature of goblins to split; they bred, they quarrelled, they were forever founding another warren out of spite. A goblin named Wibrik, a small, wiry rebel with a broken tusk and a talent for argument, gathered four of his kin and walked out of Nobstink in the dead of night, and in the morning there was a new warren, the Warren of Snikstink, and it had a flag made of a torn blanket, and Wibrik was very proud of the flag.

Nobstink was furious. It started, almost at once, to gather an army.

On the last night of the Year 199, Isan could not sleep.

She went up to the east terrace, in her altered cloak, and found it already occupied. Sergeant Maera was sitting on the parapet with her boots hanging over the edge and a flask of something clear and sharp.

"Couldn't sleep either," said Maera.

"No."

They sat for a time without speaking. Below them, Eddale glittered: the towers, the glass, the hover-lanes dotted with lanterns, the great hall with its banner, the temple with its steeple, and, beyond, the dark flowering hills where the lemon trees were flowering out of season.

"Do you think it's coming?" said Maera at last.

Isan looked up. The Wanderer was enormous now: not a thumbnail but a coin, the colour of a banked forge. She had been looking at it for ten years. It had grown with her. She had begun to think of it, privately, as a creature, patient and attentive and terribly polite, a guest who had been invited to a great house and was waiting, outside the door, with its hat in its hands.

"Yes," she said. "I think it's coming."

"Then what do we do?"

Isan thought of Annmund. She thought of the fifteen victories and the funeral in the rain. She thought of the six frightened people in the empty hall, long ago, whom she had been told about in school, the first of the line, before the First Host, before the Covenant, before there was anything to be afraid for.

"What we've always done," she said. "We stand in the line, and we know each other's names."

Maera laughed, a short bark, and drank, and passed the flask. The Watcher, above, was watching them both, and thinking that of all the things it had made, this, perhaps, was the one it would have kept: not the towers, not the laser walls. Two women on a parapet, sharing a flask in the dark.

The old year turned. It was the Year 200.

Chapter Twenty-Four: The Two Hundredth Year

"At the last, nobody was thinking about the sky. They were thinking about breakfast, and about each other."

(From the Luriel annals; the final entry, in an unknown hand)

It was a clear morning. This is the first thing, and it is the thing that every account agrees on, and it is the thing that hurts most: it was a beautiful day.

The sun rose over the eastern sea, and the sea was flat and bright, and the light went inland across the lowland like a hand opening. There was a little wind. The grain stood in the fields. In the high meadows of Eddale the birches were in new leaf, and in the flowering groves to the south the lemons had come ripe out of season, so that the air smelled, everywhere, like clean linen and sharp sweetness. It was the kind of day that makes people say, to no one, well, that's all right then.

And above the world, where for two hundred years there had been a rust-red speck that was only a rumour, there hung a huge, silent, slowly rolling shape, so near now that it had a face: pitted, glowing along one edge. It had stopped being a star some years ago. It was a mountain. It was the largest thing anyone had ever seen, and it was nearly here, and it did not hurry.

In Eddale the day began with a drill.

Isan woke at the usual hour and dressed in the usual order, and found, tucked into the sleeve of her cloak, a slip of paper in Maera's blocky hand: Went to see my mother. Back by noon. Don't start without me. She read it twice. She laughed, quietly, and put it in her pocket.

On the east terrace the soldiers were already forming up. She walked the line. She looked each of them in the face. She knew every name. She said them aloud, one by one, which she had never done on a drill morning, and a young recruit, who was perhaps twenty and had a spot of oil on his cheek, flushed to the roots of his hair when she reached him.

"You've a smudge," she said.

"Ma'am."

She took out a handkerchief and wiped it away. It was an absurd, tender, very un-general thing to do, and she did it with perfect composure, and the line, which would have laughed at anyone else, did not laugh.

"Dismissed," she said. "Eat something. All of you. It's an order."

They went. At the end of the terrace, Isan stood alone, and looked up at the Falling Star, which now filled a quarter of the eastern sky, and said, in the plain voice of a person addressing a colleague, "I would have liked to know what you were for."

The sky did not answer.

In the orchards of Luriel the elves rose early and did what elves do, which was nothing hurried.

A young scout, who had stood at the burning of a gnome hamlet seven years earlier and sung a song about wind and ash, sat on the steps with her knees up and listened to the harp. She was among the hundred and twenty-one souls of Luriel: the largest realm in the world, and the one that had not, in all those centuries, wanted to be.

"Do you remember the song," said the old woman beside her.

"Which song?"

"The one you sang at the fire. About ash."

"I was frightened."

"Of course. That's why it was a good song." The old woman patted her hand. "Sing it again."

So she sang it. The harp found the tune and held it. It was a short, sad, simple thing about the wind that carries all things, ash and seed and leaf, to the same far place, and in the courtyard the old elves closed their eyes. High above the roofs, the Wanderer was nearly full in the sky.

South, in the clover-meadows by the sea, where the grass was green to the horizon and the great peaks stood behind, the orcs of Bolthul gathered around their drums.

There were thirty of them, once the greatest horde in the world, down to a village now, with a scar on the earth where Durgoth had torn away. They were big, scarred, quiet people, and they sat in a ring around the ancestor-skulls in the old way, with their hands on their knees. They were at war, as it happened, with a few goblins at the border: Wibrik's Snikstink, five of them and a blanket. It had been a ridiculous war from the first. Nobody had died.

The chieftain, a heavy old orc with one eye, looked up at the star and grunted.

"Krukka's Drumstone," he said. "It is coming to be struck."

"By whom?" asked a young one.

"By Krukka. By the world." The old orc stood. "Bring the drum."

They brought it. It was a great skin-drum as tall as a child, older than the horde. He lifted the mallet. He struck it once, and the sound went out across the clover like a heartbeat.

Fifty-four orcs of Durgoth, who had returned to Krukka only two years ago, heard it in their own camp across the hills, and, because it was a drum and they were orcs, they answered. The drums of Bolthul and Durgoth, split by a war and a god and a century, beat together. In the dust, the grass bent. In the sky, the mountain rolled closer. In Vornak and Kragdak and Bolgor, the tiny rebel horde of three that had once followed a man called Ugdush, the drums were taken up, and the drumming spread, hill to hill, a great slow pounding laid over the whole south of the world, so that for a space of minutes the orcs of Gaia, who had not been of one mind since the founding of the Horde, were of one pulse.

In the gnome-halls under the lone mountain of the north, the Burrow of Dobwhistle was at its midday bread.

There were seventy-eight of them: the best-fed gnomes in the world, with fat cheeks and clever hands, and their long tables were laden with mushroom-loaf and honey and cheese. Now a young gnome, one of the clever ones, who had spent all week on a calculation, stood up in the middle of the meal and said, in a trembling voice, "If it lands where I think it lands, it won't matter how deep we dig."

The old ones looked at him with great kindness. "We know, child," said the eldest. "Sit down. Have some honey." He sat. He had some honey. Far under the same mountain, the sixty-six gnomes of Nimnook, who had been burned so many times by their human neighbours, stood in the fortress they had never surrendered and watched the star through a slit of rock. They had a custom: when something frightening came, they wrote the names of the living on slate, as they wrote the names of the dead. It seemed only fair. All sixty-six were written there that morning, chalked in the grey, in rows, in neat gnomish script. A girl, who had never in her life seen the sun, went out onto the ledge to see it, and stood there a long time, blinking.

"It's warm," she said.

Far west, in the savanna peninsula where the goblin-warrens crowded together, the morning was loud.

It was always loud. Fifty-five goblins of Nobmuck were arguing; sixty-one of Nobstink were arguing differently; forty-two of Grubheap were arguing about whose fault it was; and at the edge of the plain, five goblins in a ring around a torn blanket were arguing most loudly of all. The Nobstink war-band, which had been sent to march on Vornak, had not marched. It had stopped on the road, because somebody had spotted the star.

"It's the Big Rock," a goblin said.

"It's very big."

"Bigger than last week."

"Should we still march?"

There was a long pause, in which the war-band considered the question with great seriousness.

"We could," said one, "march anyway."

That was the whole of the debate. They marched, because a thing begun is a thing begun, and because a goblin who stops to think is a goblin who has to feel it. They marched in great ragged bands with spears and banners, shouting, laughing, calling each other names, while behind them, in the blanket-flag warren, Wibrik, the small wiry rebel with the broken tusk, stood on a rock and proclaimed, to his four followers, in a ringing voice:

"Tomorrow, we shall have a constitution!"

"What's a constitution?" said one of them.

"I don't know!" cried Wibrik, with tremendous joy. "But we shall have one!"

And the five of them cheered, and the Big Rock rolled closer, and across the plain the war-bands of Nobstink rolled on toward Vornak, singing a rude song at the very top of their lungs, and none of them looked up, because they could not have borne it if they had.

In the northern hills, in the five scattered villages of the Dominion of Zathixal, the saurians did what they had always done at the approach of a verdict: they sat, and they were still.

Forty-eight of them. Their numbers had come back from a nadir of three, in the Year 120, through all the fifteen wars Aurenvale had thrown at them; they had grown to twenty-three, and then to this. They had been allies of Aurenvale in the Year 1. They had been enemies for forty-five years of it. They had buried their dead in the cold pines and they had called the star the Judge.

In the temple at Zathixal, in the dim sandstone nave, an old lizard-priest with grey-green scales sat on the floor under the image of Veritas, Lady of Law. The nave was full. Nobody spoke. The priest said, in the hissing tongue, softly, the words of the old sentence they had always said before a ruling:

Yss zeth ikix.

It was their motto and their only prayer.

South of them, in the foothills of the Unmined Stone, the dwarves of Kazgar and the dwarves of Thorgar, rivals for two decades, happened that morning to stand within sight of one another across a valley. An old Kazgar miner with a beard as white as chalk raised a hand, and an old Thorgar smith raised one back. Neither could have said why. In Dwaldun, which had once been a great Hold and was down to six, a handful of old dwarves sat in the vast empty halls of their fathers, not unhappy. It is not nothing to be the last of something.

At noon, the sky was wrong.

It is hard to say how, because the people who saw it did not live to say. The sun was there. It was still bright. But a second light had begun to mix with it, red and gold, rolling in from the east and from above, so that every shadow on the ground grew a second shadow, and then a third. The air thickened. The birds fell quiet. The Falling Star was no longer a thing in the sky. It was the sky.

In Eddale, the people had come out into the streets.

They came out slowly, without panic. They stood on the steps of the temple and the doorway of the library and the long terraces of the hall. Mothers lifted their children. The old herald stood under the banner with his cane. Edry, the new herald, stood beside him, holding the scroll against her chest, as if it could be a shield. Isan was on the east terrace, with her twenty-seven, and Maera had come back from her mother's, breathless, and stood in the line, two places down from her general, and gave her a tiny, wicked wink.

There were a hundred and ten of them.

They were the second-largest realm in the world. They had a hall and a temple and a library, a banner called The Eternal, a hundred and ninety-nine years of decrees behind them, and an alliance with the oldest and kindest of their enemies. They had risen from six frightened people in an empty hall, and fallen to three, and risen again, by all the stubborn grace of human stock; and they had stood in the line, again and again and again, for two hundred years, and known each other's names.

Isan said, "Soldiers."

They straightened.

"I am proud of you," she said, in her ordinary voice, as if reading a number from a list. "That is all. That is the whole of the speech."

A child, somewhere in the crowd, began to sing the Pale Eye song, the bad one, the one that rhymed eye with die, and an old man joined in, and a woman, and soon the whole terrace was singing it, badly, with cracked and tender voices, a verse about a ditch-digger and a mirror. It was an awful song. It was theirs.

Somewhere in the high place, the Watcher stood very still.

The nineteen realms of the Biosphere were, at that last moment, as follows:

Aurenvale, a hundred and ten. Luriel, a hundred and twenty-one. Dobwhistle, seventy-eight. Nimnook, sixty-six. Vornak, sixty-two. Nobstink, sixty-one. Nobmuck, fifty-five. Durgoth, fifty-four. Zathixal, forty-eight. Grubheap, forty-two. Kazgar, thirty-five. Bolthul, thirty. Thorgar, twenty-one. Serthas, eighteen. Caelmir, eighteen. Kragdak, fourteen. Dwaldun, six. Snikstink, five. Bolgor, three.

Eight hundred and forty-seven souls.

None of them were the last. None of them, on that clear day, had to be. It is a thing the chroniclers have never been able to bear: that they were all still there, all of them, every one, arguing and drumming and mending fences, at the moment of the end.

The light came without a sound.

For a heartbeat there was no noise at all, in any realm. The birds, the drums, the harp, the singing; all of it stopped, as if the world had drawn a breath. Then the sky opened, from edge to edge, and the thing that had been the Falling Star came down through it, brighter than any sun that had ever risen over Gaia, a white so total that it was not a colour but an absence.

In Eddale, a hundred and ten people looked up.

Isan did not close her eyes. She had always looked at things directly, and she looked now, at the white, and she thought, with great clarity, of the cloak that was too big, and of Annmund's funeral in the rain, and of the young recruit with the spot of oil on his cheek, and she reached for Maera's hand and found it. Names, she thought. I knew all their names.

In Luriel the old woman and the young scout were still holding hands. In the clover-meadows of the south the drum was struck once more, and its sound was the last sound on Gaia, one deep beat, that went out across the grass, and was not answered. Under the grey mountain of the north the gnome girl at the ledge shaded her eyes, and the warmth became a different thing. On the road to Vornak, a goblin laughed out loud at something, and stopped.

It was over very quickly. That is the one mercy the record allows.

The light rolled outward from the point of impact, and the world was struck in a lattice of craters, one beside another, until no land was left that was not pressed with a ring of fire. The seas boiled. The mountains broke. The flowering groves south of Eddale, where the lemons had come ripe out of season, burned without a sound. The great hall, the glass towers, the temple with its steeple, the Eternal Banner with its bridge: all of it was light, and then it was nothing.

In every realm, in every town, in every barn and on every terrace, all the people of the world died.

Nineteen realms. Eight hundred and forty-seven souls.

And then none.

Epilogue: What the Watcher Kept

"It is not the last who is remembered. It is the one who is still holding the pen."

(Inscription found on no wall, because there were no walls)

There was a silence, and it had no edges.

In the place that is not a place, the Watcher stood where it had stood for two hundred years, looking down at a world that was no longer a world. Gaia lay beneath it like a burned plate. The continent that had been walkable, shore to shore, was a pattern of craters and a skin of steam and ash. Nothing moved. Nothing would. The ocean was still rolling, a long time, over rocks that had been mountains.

The Maker came, as it always came, without a door.

"Well," it said.

The Watcher did not turn. "Well."

"I'm sorry," said the Maker. It was true, and it was said simply, with no ceremony, and for a long moment neither of them said anything else.

"You told me the terms," the Watcher said at last. "I accepted them. I'd like that written down. I did not fail because I did not understand. I failed because I could not do it."

"You could have done it," said the Maker.

"How?"

"I don't know. I never do." It sat down, if it sat, on nothing. "That's the thing about a game with real stakes. There may be a way to win it, and the player doesn't find it, and the one who set it up doesn't know it either. I only knew you'd try."

"And I tried."

"You tried for two hundred years."

The Watcher turned at last. It was an odd thing to look at: a creature that had no face and had worn one for two centuries, and the face was tired. "I want to tell you what I kept," it said.

"I'd like to hear it."

It was not a long list.

It kept the sound of the herald reading, in the first year, in a hall with six people in it, a voice that did not yet know what it was doing. It kept Tomry, a soldier at Brookgate, alone, going home. It kept eight children in a doorway, counting each other. It kept the long shape of the Covenant, sworn in an empty room. It kept Annwin, twice: the first who died against the spider and the second who led the march on Shrikka. It kept a young man being sick in the dirt after the greatest victory of his life. It kept the ditch-digger with the kitchen tray. It kept a banner, silver and blue, with a bridge across it. It kept a girl of nineteen with a cracking voice, and a woman with a clipboard, and an old herald who wept at the word alliance.

It kept, also, the other peoples: the elves with their gardens, the orcs with their drum, the gnomes who wrote names on slate, the saurians who said their one prayer, the goblin in the torn-blanket warren who wanted a constitution and did not know what one was. It had fought nearly every one of them, one way or another, and it had never, at the last, wished them gone.

"I kept them all," it said. "Not because I won. Because I was there."

The Maker was quiet for a long while. "Is that what the Covenant was for?" it asked.

"I don't know."

"Tell me anyway."

The Watcher turned again to the burned plate of the world. It was not an easy question. It had turned it over so many times, in the dim place where it kept its decisions, that it no longer knew which side was which.

"I made a rule," it said slowly. "Rule one: decrees only. Rule two: one Sign a decade, never against another. Rule three: wars are won by soldiers. I told myself it was honour. I told myself that a god who reaches into a war with its hand has made a puppet of the people it claims to love, and I would not do that. I would be a power that bound itself." It paused. "I think, some years, that was true. I think, in the Year 75, in that empty hall, it was true. They were six and I was a god. I wanted to be the sort of god who would not use it."

"And the other years?"

"The other years I think it was vanity." The word came out like something dropped. "I think I wanted to be the god who did not cheat. I think I wanted the record to say so. I think that somewhere in the middle of the century the rule stopped being for them and was for me, and I could not tell, and I kept it anyway, because to break it was to admit that I had been, all along, only a creature who was afraid of being seen to be afraid."

"Both, then."

"Both. I do not know in what proportion." The Watcher looked at its hands. "If I had broken it, they might have lived. Some of them. Or I might have broken it for nothing; the world was nineteen pieces, and no hand could have gathered them. I do not know. I will never know. That is the part I cannot bear."

"No," said the Maker. "I don't suppose you can."

"Was it honour?"

The Maker did not answer at once. It looked down at the world. For a time it was almost as if it were not a power at all, only a person, sitting on a hill at the end of a day, looking at something that had once been lovely.

"I think," it said, "that you'll have to carry that one yourself."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It isn't." The Maker smiled, faintly, with all its old amusement and something older underneath it. "But here's what I saw. For two hundred years I watched a god tie its own hands because it believed, rightly or wrongly, that it was the better thing. I watched it lose. And I watched it grieve, every decade, every general, every funeral. Honour doesn't feel like honour from inside. Neither does vanity. They both feel like keeping your word."

The Watcher said nothing.

"I'll tell you what I do know," said the Maker. "The ones who stood in the line did not know your reasons. They only knew each other's names. And it was enough, for them. It was enough for a woman on a terrace at the end of the world to say I am proud of you, and mean it. You gave them a hall to stand in, and a banner, and each other. That's not nothing."

"It's not the terms."

"No. It isn't the terms." It rose. "The stone fell. You didn't win. Those are facts. I won't tell you otherwise. But the record is not only facts, is it? You of all people know that. It's also the song about the mirror."

The Watcher almost laughed. It was a terrible sound, small and cracked, and it did not last. "That was a very bad song."

"It was a dreadful song," said the Maker. "I loved it."

They stood together for a long time, the two powers, above a world that had stopped.

The ash was beginning to settle. It would take a thousand years. Somewhere below, in a crater at the heart of the central highlands, where Eddale had stood among its hills and its birch-woods and its pocket of jungle, a small piece of the great hall's lintel lay half-buried in glass. The weather had not yet worn the words from it. If anyone had been there to read, they would have found, cut deep in the stone, in the old Eddish, the first line the first people had written, in the first year, in the hope of being believed:

We were six. Write that down, and then write that we were enough.

No one was there. No one would be.

"Do you want to begin again?" asked the Maker, at last. "I could. A new world. A new set of terms. A different map." It paused. "There are islands, in some of them."

The Watcher considered it. It was a real offer. It was, perhaps, the kindest thing the Maker had ever said.

"No islands," it said.

"You're sure?"

"No." The Watcher looked down at the burned world, and then, slowly, at the last dim stain in the sky where the star had been. "I am not sure of anything. I am sure that I would like to remember it. I would like to remember them first. After that, you may ask me anything."

"That seems fair."

"And the next time," said the Watcher, "I would like to hear, once, before the end, that you noticed him. The one who got fifteen."

"I noticed," said the Maker. "I told you. I'm always noticing."

It put out a hand, and the Watcher, after a moment, took it. There was no sound. There was no light. There was only the dark between worlds, and two powers in it who had made something and lost it, and who would, when they were ready, make something again.

In the Year 200 of the world called Gaia, on a clear and lovely morning, a hundred and ten people stood in a line on a terrace in Eddale and sang a bad song, and one of them said she was proud.

That is what the Watcher kept.

It is, in the end, the whole of the record. It is not the terms. It is not the victory. It is a woman with a clipboard, writing down names.

APPENDIXThe Codex of GaiaLore, peoples, Signs and years

The World

Gaia. The name of the world the Watcher chose from among many. One continent in a single ocean, every shore of it reachable on foot. The Watcher refused every world that had an island in it, because in the Biosphere Realm no ship ever carried a soldier, and a people on an island could never be reached by war.

The Spine. The chain of white peaks across the middle and north of the continent. Eddale lies in its southern foothills; the saurians of Zathixal live among its northern teeth.

The Middle Lands. The hills, high meadows, birchwoods and pocket-jungle around Eddale. The most beautiful country in Gaia and the most dangerous. Giant spiders, basilisks, trolls and thunderbirds hunted there for two hundred years.

The Stone. A red star that hung low in every sky from the first year and grew for two centuries. The humans called it the Falling Star, or Aurelan's Eye. It was the Maker's term made visible. It fell at the end of the Year 200.

The Ages. Every people climbed the same ladder: Stone, Bronze, Iron, Medieval, Gunpowder, Industrial, Modern, Future. In two hundred years Gaia went from great halls of timber to glass spires, power armour and hovercraft. Aurenvale reached the Future Age in the Year 169.

The Powers

The Maker. The power that made the Biosphere Realm, hung the Stone in its sky, and set the terms: one people, two hundred years, prosperity and the last realm standing, or the Stone falls.

The Watcher. The power that chose the humans of Eddale and ruled them. It named them Aurenvale, their faith the Covenant of Aurelan, their way the Oathbound way and their tongue Eddish. It ruled by one decree in every three years, spoken through the herald of Eddale.

The Covenant. The law the Watcher laid on itself in the Year 75, when its people had no grown adult left. It would give at most one Sign in a decade, and only to its own people. It would never raise its hand against another realm. Wars would be fought by soldiers and not by gods. The Watcher kept it to the end.

The Signs. Nine in all. The First Host, six grown soldiers from nowhere, in the Year 75: Maeora, Elric, Annwin, Edwin, Edora and Corry. The Birth-Wave of the Year 85: eight children in one season. The Second Host (100), Third Host (120), Fourth Host (130), Fifth Host (140). The Golden Age of the Year 160. The Sixth Host (170) and the Seventh (190). No Sign was given in the Years 110, 150 or 180.

The Founding Peoples

The Kingdom of Aurenvale. Humans of Eddale, in the central highlands. Faith: the Covenant of Aurelan, Lord of Order, whose domain is Order and Duty. Culture: the Oathbound way, of Chivalry and Scholarship. Tongue: Eddish. Villages: Eddale (Year 0), Brookgate and Roston (28, both abandoned), Brookham (49), Stanfield (91, rebelled 144). Six souls at the founding, three at the nadir in the Year 40, a hundred and thirty-five at the peak in the Year 140, a hundred and ten at the end.

The Realm of Luriel. Elves of the east coast. Faith: the Syncretic Circle of Zephrael, the Storm Spirit, of Storm, Wind and Sky. Culture of Harmony and Grace. The greatest realm in Gaia for most of two centuries, and the largest at the end, with a hundred and twenty-one souls. Lost Serthas (103), Caelmir (118) and Ithriel (188) to rebellion. Heroes: Finara, Lirwen, Sylnor, Galwyn, Naedil, Tauriel, Taueth. Aurenvale's enemy in the Years 12 and 28, its ally in 49, 109, 157, 184 and 199.

The Hold of Dwaldun. Dwarves of the western hills. Faith: the Contemplative Order of Khazrum the Stonefather, of Stone and Deep Delving. Culture of Deep Lore and Craftsmanship. A hundred and fifty souls in the Year 160; then Thorgar (171) and Kazgar (178) broke away and only six remained at the end. Heroes: Dwanar, Dwaur, Thrur, Gimdain, Oribur, Gimok.

The Horde of Bolthul. Orcs of the southern clover coast. Faith: the Zealous Cult of Krukka, the Chaos-Drum, of Chaos and Frenzy. Culture of the Blood-Oath and the Ancestor-Skulls. A hundred and twenty-six souls at the height; then Kragdak (117), Vornak (127) and Durgoth (158) broke away. Heroes: Mornak, Lugmash, Gashrot, Morgrub, Karbag, Thrarot, Gashmash, Thramash, Bolbag, Rukmash, Gashbag.

The Burrow of Dobwhistle. Gnomes of the northern forest peninsula. Faith: the Contemplative Order of Gorim Gemheart, of Gems and Hidden Wealth. Culture of Grudge-Keeping and Craftsmanship. The first realm to reach the Future Age, in the Year 134. Lost Nimnook in 135. Heroes: Fenpin, who killed the Weaver; Fizble, Quimdle, Tobkin, Bimble, Quimzap, Wobdle, Pippin, Fenny, Pipwick, Nixzap, Fenble.

The Warren of Nobmuck. Goblins of the far western savanna. Faith: the Zealous Cult of Brogmar Bloodtide, of Bloodlust and the Hunt. Culture of the Ancestor-Skulls and the Blood-Oath. Lost Grubheap (127) and Nobstink (139). Heroes: Gnarsk, Gribbit, Skivzag, Kratrik, Rikkgob.

The Dominion of Zathixal. Saurians of the northern peaks. Faith: the Church of Veritas, Lady of Law, of Law and Justice. Culture of Ambition and Honor. Aurenvale's first ally, in the Year 1. Its target in fifteen wars between 103 and 148, cut from thirty souls to three, and grown back to forty-eight across five hamlets: Zathixal, Zathzun, Iskthal, Thexaizzar and Raszzizzar. Heroes: Rizra, Rizor, Rizeth, Kraith, Talssa, Oxssa.

The Later Realms

The Kingdom of Northshire. Human wanderers who came over the horizon in the Year 40. Converted to the Covenant of Aurelan by the missionaries of Aurenvale in 66. Fell into ruin in 83.

The Hold of Dwalhalm. Dwarf wanderers of the Year 56, of the Order of Vurna Emberforge. Generals Thrarik and Balok. Fell into ruin in 87.

The Hold of Torzul. Dwarf wanderers of the Year 72, in the far north. Twice attacked by Aurenvale, in 88 and 94, and never taken. Heroes Dwarik, Grook and Dwagrim. Fell into ruin in 151.

The Realm of Serthas. Elves who rose against Luriel in 103, of the Circle of Sylvaen, Spirit of Spring. Ally of Zathixal.

The Horde of Kragdak. Orcs of Bolthul's sea-settlement, founded 37, risen 117, of the Cult of Zhulka, Mother of Ash.

The Realm of Caelmir. Elves who rose against Luriel in 118, and were won back to Zephrael by missionaries in 125.

The Horde of Vornak. Orcs who rose against Bolthul in 127, of the Cult of Ghorak the Skullfather, of War and Conquest.

The Warren of Grubheap. Goblins who rose against Nobmuck in 127.

The Burrow of Nimnook. Gnomes who rose against Dobwhistle in 135. Aurenvale's target in twelve wars from 151 to 196. Never broken. Heroes Fizfen and Wobwick.

The Warren of Nobstink. Goblins who rose against Nobmuck in 139, of the Cult of Zhulka. They carved Victory over Aurenvale in the Year 160. Hero Pugsnag.

The Kingdom of Stanfield. Aurenvale's own village, risen in the Year 144 under the hero Isbert and the new god Caspian, the Rising Crown, of Ambition and Glory. Isbert fell that year after eight victories. Stanfield fell into ruin in 147.

The Horde of Durgoth. Bolthul's greatest city, eighty-seven strong, risen in 158. Hero Snagnak.

The Horde of Bolgor. Rebels under Ugdush, broken from Durgoth in 165. Hero Gashlok.

The Hold of Thorgar and the Hold of Kazgar. Dwarves risen against Dwaldun in 171 and 178. Heroes Fungrim, Thrli and Bofok, who killed the Weaver.

The Realm of Ithriel. Elves risen against Luriel in 188, of the Circle of Yssa, Winter Moon. Hero Ilinor. Fell into ruin in 191.

The Warren of Snikstink. Goblins under Wibrik, risen against Nobstink in the Year 199. The last realm ever founded in Gaia.

The Generals of Aurenvale

Annwin (made general in the Year 5, fell against the Weaver in 8). Gereth (28). Edmund (51), an orphan of the Year 30. Marric (57). Annwin of the First Host (75), who bore the first general's name. Edwin of the First Host (87), who killed Ghorr. Corry of the First Host (91), who killed the Pale Eye. Henwin (120). Annbert (127), who killed Viperscale and fell in 128. Wilold (128). Isora the Tough (172 to 176), who killed Old Bramble. Annmund (176 to 189), greatest of them, fallen after fifteen victories. Isan (190 to 200), the last.

The Beasts

Giant spiders. The Weaver, Silkfang, Gloomleg, Shrikka, Webmother. Basilisks. Viperscale, the Pale Eye, Stonegaze, Ghorr. Trolls. Gravelback, Old Bramble, Thudgar, Mossjaw, Stonehide. Thunderbirds. Stormcry, Voltwing, Skyrend. The names returned when the beasts were slain. The people of the Middle Lands said a monster's name is a crown, and the next one picks it up.

The Last Census

At the end of the Year 200, before the Stone: Luriel 121, Aurenvale 110, Dobwhistle 78, Nimnook 66, Vornak 62, Nobstink 61, Nobmuck 55, Durgoth 54, Zathixal 48, Grubheap 42, Kazgar 35, Bolthul 30, Thorgar 21, Serthas 18, Caelmir 18, Kragdak 14, Dwaldun 6, Snikstink 5, Bolgor 3. Nineteen realms. Eight hundred and forty-seven souls. After the Stone: none.
