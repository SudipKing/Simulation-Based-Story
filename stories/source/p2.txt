#B BOOK THREE|The Age of Forged Steel and Despair|Years 273 to 347

#C Chapter Six: The Secret of the Two Fathers

#E There is an old saying among the dwarves: a debt of blood does not die. It only sleeps, as the roots of the mountain sleep, and wakes when the ground grows warm.

Peace, after so many wars, felt like a strange coat that did not quite fit.

In the quiet that followed the orc wars, a thought began to move among the keener minds of the court at Sylvanor, and it was a thought that no one wished to say aloud. It was this: that the king of Metallica, Yeth, and the king of Rakas, Grih, were the sons of two men who had once stood in the same hall, with a hammer between them.

Grim had killed Bishop. Bishop's son was Yeth. Grim's son was Grih. And both boys had been made kings at the age of six, shielded from the story by the same swirl of blood and confusion that had given them their crowns.

"Does Yeth know?" Sylvas asked his counsellors one evening, with the lamp light low on his cheek. "Does Grih? Has it been so long that the memory has crumbled into dust? Or does it sit in them like a coal in a pile of ash, waiting for a breath of wind?"

No one answered. No one knew. In the dwarven halls the old ones who had witnessed the killing were long dead, and the young ones had been raised on a tidy lie: that the two nations of Metallica and Rakas were cousins, split by a squabble over mining rights.

In the Year 273 Yeth and Grih did something that made every elf in Aethelgard hold its breath. They signed a treaty.

It was a thick, heavy thing, written in the blunt script of the dwarves on a slab of polished granite. They called it Forged Steel. It joined Metallica and Rakas into a single alliance, the two dwarven realms standing shoulder to shoulder. Together they counted eight hundred and thirty-three dwarves. Aethelgard, at the same moment, counted eight hundred and twelve elves.

Eight hundred and thirty-three to eight hundred and twelve. The dwarves, for the first time, outnumbered their neighbors.

"A foreshadowing," whispered the elves of Mystic. "Or a coincidence."

Sylvas, who had lived through enough coincidences to distrust them, began to look at the map.

#C Chapter Seven: The War of Forged Steel

#E "The elves strike first. This is how the elves always die: by being the first to be brave."

In the Year 284, King Sylvas, who had survived the war against the humans by standing at the back, who had survived the long cold peace by watching the maps, and who had survived two hundred and some winters by trusting his own sense of when a storm was coming, did a thing that no one expected.

He struck first.

He declared war on Rakas. Not on Metallica, not on the alliance as a whole, but on Rakas alone, the smaller of the two dwarven nations, the one led by the unreadable Grih. It was a gamble. If Yeth honored the alliance, the elves would face both dwarven kingdoms in a war they could not win. If Yeth did not, Sylvas could shatter Rakas and then deal with Metallica on his own terms.

A hundred and thirty-four soldiers marched from Mystic, Istar, and Nile toward the mountains where the empires met. A hundred more, with Sylvas himself in the center of their ranks, rolled out from the capital, the old king bundled in his green cloak and carried in a litter, for his knees no longer served him as they once had. The horns of Aethelgard sounded across the Enchanted Wood. Birds fled from the trees.

The dwarves answered. Seventy-one dwarven soldiers of Storm, a fortress-city of Rakas, poured into Mystic like water down a drain. Sixty-three from the Iron Fort and forty-six from Mith held the front line against the elves. And the alliance, so quickly tested, held: Yeth charged out of The Rock with sixty-six of his own troops, the very core of Metallica, and crashed into the elven line at the gates of Mystic. He mobilized eighty-two from The Stone to support the weary front at the Iron Fort and Mith.

It was war on three fronts, and the elves were outnumbered, outgunned, and outraged.

And yet, as it turned out, the dwarves had not planned for the elves to fight so cleverly.

*

The first surprise came in the Year 286, when the elves marched into Orkason, an orc-city that Grih had claimed years before, and found it empty. The orcs had gone. All of them, the thirteen who had knelt at Grih's gate, had left in the night, whether into the wastes or into some darker place no one knew. The elves took the city without a blow.

In the Year 288, the second surprise came. Sylvas's army crashed into Mith. By then only a single dwarf stood to guard it, an old man with a hammer, who looked at the oncoming army and laughed, and was cut down. Mith was taken. The Iron Fort, stripped of its defenders, was razed. Rakas, with only forty dwarves left in all its halls, and none of them soldiers, was a hollow shell. Meanwhile, the elves of Aethelgard counted six hundred still, a hundred and twelve in arms, and sixty of those already marching on the front, their eyes set on Storm.

"The alliance," murmured Sylvas, gazing at the distant mountains, "has meant very little."

It was true. Yeth had retreated. At the first sign of the elves' resolve, the king of Metallica, who had signed Forged Steel with so much ceremony, pulled his soldiers back from Mystic and dug in. Sylvas, secure in his own judgment, withdrew to Sylvanor to command from afar. And Yeth, too, withdrew, the king of the dwarves and the king of the elves each pulling back from the battle as if from a fire, though the elves stood now at the very threshold of Metallica.

Then came the third surprise, and the cruelest.

Mith was razed, and then Storm fell as well, the elves taking it with the last of their strength. Rakas was destroyed. The alliance was no more. Forged Steel, which had been signed in granite, dissolved like sugar in rain. And then Yeth saw his chance. He looked at the elven army, ragged and exhausted, only twenty elves at the very front, and he did what a king does when his enemy has gone limp. He turned around.

He took Storm. It became a part of Metallica. And with a bellow that echoed down the mountain, he commanded his last eighty soldiers to crush the twenty exhausted elves in front of him.

It might have ended there. It might have ended in a slaughter, the elven front line trampled, the road to Mystic open, Aethelgard cut in half. But the elves of Istar, a little band of soldiers who had been sent to guard the flank, did something that no general would have written down in any manual. They ran. They fled the front line, not out of cowardice but out of brilliance, and swung around the dwarven flank like a hand closing, and fell upon Orkasta, the city Yeth had taken from the orcs, and took it from the rear. Then, with the swiftness of a wind, they pressed on to The Boulder, Yeth's proud new city, and when his scattered troops rushed to defend it, they lost it too.

Istar's soldiers came back to the front in triumph, but they were too few to hold what they had taken. With no one left to live in it, Orkasta was destroyed. And in the maelstrom of the fighting, The Boulder and Storm were both torn from the map, burned so thoroughly that not even ruins remained.

That was the war's turning point, and its tragedy. The elves had won more cities than they could hold, and lost more than they had ever dared to risk.

*

In the Year 292, the war entered its final act, and it began with a funeral.

The great King Sylvas, the Sun-Watcher, who had stood on the high balcony of Sylvanor at every dawn for nearly three centuries, died in his bed, with the first light of morning on his face. His age had finally caught up with him. He had outlived three human kings and a dozen orc ones; he had broken a wall and made two new worlds, and at the end he did not seem frightened, only tired, as a man is tired who has come to the last page of a very long book.

At the same time, on a field in the north, the elven armies, now commanded by his marshals, charged at last toward The Rock itself. The city was almost empty. Only three soldiers stood in the gates. The Stone still had seventy-five who had never left its walls. And in the hours when the elves broke the first line of the Rock's defense, Yeth, the king who had killed no one with his own hand and had kept his hands clean for nearly two centuries, rode out to the front to face them. He was killed there, in the mud, struck down by an elven spear.

The elves, with the capital almost in their grasp and both kings dead, surged forward.

And then, in the last desperate minute, the soldiers of The Stone arrived. Seventy-five dwarves who had stayed behind their own walls came racing along the mountain road, shields locked, voices bellowing a war-song that was older than the nation. They slammed into the side of the elven army and the elves were forced to turn. The Rock was saved.

And then came the strangest thing of all.

In the fields outside The Rock, in the cold light after the battle, an old farmer straightened up from his plowing and leaned on his stone hammer. He was tall for a dwarf, and gray, and tired. He looked out over the battlefield, the bodies of elves and dwarves lying in the stubble, and he said nothing. People who saw him thought he was just another peasant, come to scavenge for armor.

It was Grih.

The king of Rakas, who had lost his entire nation, who had lost Storm and Mith and the Iron Fort, who had lost everything, had simply disappeared into the countryside and taken up a farmer's plow. He had been alive the whole time. He had been working the fields of The Rock for years, in the shadow of the city whose last king had just died.

"Yeth is dead," someone told him.

Grih looked at the hammer in his hand. Then he looked at the hall of the Rock, where the great doors stood open.

"Then I suppose," he said, "that I had better go in."

#C Chapter Eight: The Farmer King

#E "He did not forget his power," the chroniclers would write. "He had only put it down, as a man puts down a heavy thing, to rest."

The queen of the elves was a hundred and eleven years old, the only child of the dead king, and she had been waiting for her father's crown since before the Year 200.

Her name was Dostier.

She took the throne of Aethelgard in the shadow of her father's funeral pyre, and her first act as queen was to stare at the map. The elves held most of the north, now: Mystic, Istar, the ruined fields of Orkason. The dwarves were broken, their kings dead, their cities burned. The war, by any measure, had been won.

But the war was not over. It was not the sort of war that ends when someone wins. It was the sort that ends when someone is tired.

Grih strode into the great hall of The Rock with the stone hammer on his shoulder, in the same mud-caked boots he had worn in the fields. The nobles of Metallica, who had expected a king to come to them, wearing a crown and carrying a banner, stared at him in astonishment. Then someone, an old dwarf with a white beard, knelt. Then another. By the time Grih reached the throne, the entire court was kneeling.

The farmer sat down upon the throne of Bishop and said, "Bring me the maps."

He took command of the army, such as it was. He did not wait. He did not sue for peace. He sent his entire strength, every dwarf who could carry a weapon, against Mystic and Istar, straight at the heart of the elves' conquests, and he did it with the cold fury of a man who had lost everything once already.

It was a terrible mistake. The elves, who had spent the last six years learning to fight dwarves, destroyed his army utterly. The front line shifted to the right entrance of the mountain, and there, in the shadow of the stone, Grih's survivors marched through the ruins of Orkason, putting it to the torch, until the final city of the orcs was gone.

Both sides were exhausted. Both sides had run out of soldiers and of hope. On the plain, in the smoke of the burned cities, Grih and Dostier, a farmer-king and a daughter-queen, who had never met, sent messengers to one another. They met in the ruins of Orkason, with the wind blowing ash across the ground between them.

"I killed your father's army," said Dostier. "You killed my father's. Neither of us has an army left."

"No," said Grih.

"Shall we stop?"

Grih looked at the ruins, and at the dead, and at the hammer in his hand.

"Yes," he said. "I think we shall."

And so the war of the elves and the dwarves ended, in the Year 292, in a peace that no one had planned and that no one believed would last. The elves had a hundred and ninety-one souls, thirty-eight of whom could still fight. The dwarves of Metallica, a hundred and thirty-six, only three of them soldiers. The cities were burning. The fields were ash. The great alliances had been broken, and the great kings were dead.

Nine years later, the sky itself would pass judgment.

#C Chapter Nine: The Age of Despair

#E Snow fell on the world for the first time in the Year 301, and it did not stop falling.

It began as a few white flakes drifting out of a grey sky, and the elves, who had never seen such a thing, held out their hands and laughed. The dwarves, who had heard of snow in their old songs, kept their hoods up and their eyes narrow. Within a month, the laughing had stopped.

The snow came in sheets, then in storms, then in a great white silence that lay over the land like a lid. The sun was a pale coin behind the clouds. The crops died in the frozen fields. The forest of Aethelgard, which had never known a hard winter, locked itself in ice. The rivers, which had flowed since the first year of the world, froze to the bed. Starvation moved through the halls of both peoples like a quiet guest. Hypothermia took the old and then the young.

The Age of Despair had begun.

By the end of the Year 301, only eighty-eight dwarves and seventy-seven elves were left alive in the whole world. They hunkered in their cellars and burned their furniture and ate their seed corn. Neither people had enough strength to fight, or enough hatred to remember why they should.

Even in that dark, there were those who tried.

In the Year 309, Grih, who had been a farmer and a king and was now an old man with a stone hammer and a stubborn heart, founded a new village at the edge of the northern ice. He called it O'Raka, an echo of the lost nation of his father's making. It was a small, fierce, bitter place. It would be a long time before anyone knew whether it would survive.

And in the same year, something worse than the snow came up out of the earth.

It crawled from the mouth of an abandoned mine beneath Ember Al, the city Sylvas had founded in honor of his lost golden Ember. It had been a man once, a mage, perhaps, of some forgotten human order, who had gone into the dark in search of forbidden knowledge and had been changed by what he found. His name was Gran Kal. He was a necromancer, and his skin was gray as candle wax, and his eyes were pits of cold green light.

The soldiers of Anaconda, passing the mine on patrol, saw him. They did not stop to ask his business. Their spears found his heart before he could finish his first spell, and Gran Kal died with a rattle and a look of mild astonishment.

But he had already woken something. A handful of skeletal soldiers, dragged from their ancient graves by the last pulse of his magic, rose up around the mine and fell upon the weakened, defenseless city of Ember Al. The elves who lived there, starved, frostbitten, and unarmed, were cut down without a fight.

Ember Al fell. For the second time in the history of the world, a city called Ember was lost. The people said it was cursed.

#C Chapter Ten: The Thaw

#E And then, in the Year 340, the snow began to melt.

It melted slowly, the way a thought dissolves. Little streams ran out from under the drifts. The ground softened. A green shoot, so small it could hardly be seen, pushed up through the black mud of the Enchanted Wood.

Spring had come at last.

The people who had survived the winter crept from their cellars like ghosts. Elves, thin and silent, looked at the sky and wept. Dwarves, hardier, laughed and stamped the dirt and began to dig. In the north, in the small bitter village of O'Raka, the survivors stood on the walls and watched the ice retreat, and somebody, perhaps the old king himself, said quietly, "We are still here."

The numbers were small. The elves had fallen to fifty-four; the dwarves, thanks to the harshness of the northern hills, had grown to eighty. But the spring brought a boom, as springs do. In the Year 347, the dwarves had risen to a hundred and thirty-seven, while the elves, who bear children as rarely as they bear grudges, had added only three.

But there was one gift the snow had left behind, and it was a great one.

The wasteland was gone. The poisoned soil of the northeast, the black land that had given birth to the orcs, had vanished under the frozen white, and when the snow melted, the toxic mist did not return. Where the orcs had once dwelled, the jungle came creeping back, green and fierce and glad, and the northwest became habitable for all.

It was a bitter consolation, but a consolation all the same. The world had been scoured. In the long, cold hand of the Age of Despair, the old poisons had been washed away, and the survivors, thin and hungry and almost without hope, found themselves standing in a clean place.

O'Raka, the village at the edge of the ice, stood among them. It carried, as its founder had intended, the flame of Rakas.

#B BOOK FOUR|The Fall of Aethelgard|Years 363 to 406

#C Chapter Eleven: The Rebellion of Dua

#E She was the daughter of a king and the lover of a rebel, and her heart was too large for the world she had inherited.

By the Year 363, Anaconda, the old jungle capital of the humans, had passed under the nominal rule of Aethelgard. The elven queen Dostier, in order to seal the peace with her human subjects and secure their loyalty, had given the governorship of the city to a man named Dua, who was a human of the old jungle line, and who had, to the surprise of the whole court, become her lover.

Dua was a clever man, a restless one, handsome in a rough way. His city had twenty-three humans and seven elves living within its walls. Few in number, but humans have always known how to make a few seem like many. He listened to the old stories, the legends of the Jungle Federation and its kings; he heard the humans muttering at night about the days when their people had ruled half the world. And one day, in a moment that the humans of Anaconda would remember for generations, he stood up in the marketplace, and he proclaimed himself king.

He named his new nation Bierth.

Dostier heard the news in her throne room, and her face turned white. Aethelgard declared war to retake it, as it was bound to do. But the war was a pathetic thing. Aethelgard had twenty-four elves with four soldiers. Bierth had thirty humans with six soldiers. On the other side of the mountain, Metallica had grown to two hundred and seventy-four, with fifty soldiers, and the elves watched, nervous, as the dwarves held their swords still and did not move. It would have been so easy for them to take advantage. It would have been so easy to finish off the dying elves for good.

They did not move. Not yet.

The elven queen, who had loved a man and been betrayed by him, did something that no one anticipated. She mounted a horse and rode alone into Bierth, to challenge Dua to single combat in the great plaza of his new kingdom.

Dua did not come out. He sent his soldiers.

In the Year 365, Dostier, queen of the elves, who had held her father's throne for seventy-three years, was killed by a soldier named Oreero. By a bitter irony, Oreero's father, Dadier, was Dua's oldest and best friend.

She died with her hand on her sword and her eyes on the door through which her lover should have come.

*

The twist that followed, the Watchers agreed, was the cruelest of their long game.

The crown of Aethelgard did not pass to a rival, a general, or a distant cousin. It passed to a young man named Ambe, the only child of Dostier and Dua, half elf and half man, born of the love that had broken a kingdom. He took his dead mother's throne. His father was the enemy of his people.

Year after year, border skirmishes bled both sides. By the Year 375, twelve years into the war, Aethelgard was down to twenty-six, with no soldiers left at all. Bierth had twenty-two, with three who could fight. The land between them was a patchwork of ambushes.

In the same year, in the mountains where the elven city of Mystic had once stood, King Grih of Metallica built a new city. He called it Yeth.

No one asked why. Everyone wondered. It was said in the taverns of The Rock that the old king had never forgotten the war, never forgotten the slain Yeth, whose father had been murdered by his father, and that in naming a city for him, he was making a kind of apology that he could never say aloud. It was said, too, that he was merely a sentimental old dwarf. The truth was probably both.

#C Chapter Twelve: The Dragon and the Traitor

#E In the Year 387, the last great power of the old world awoke, and it was not elf, nor dwarf, nor man.

In the far northeast, beyond the places where any map had ever been drawn, a dragon slept.

It had slept for a very long time, under a mountain of cold stone, coiled around the memory of a fire that had been lit before the first sunrise. Nobody knows who woke it, whether the Watchers or the shifting of the earth or some distant dream. But in the Year 387, its eyes opened, and the lids rolled back from two pits of molten gold.

It rose. It stretched its wings, and the mountain cracked. It flew over the sea of forest, an ember falling through the dusk, and the elves of Nile looked up from their fishing nets and saw a thing in the sky that had no name.

The dragon burned Nile. It burned the docks and the lanterns and the fishing nets, and then it turned its great head toward Sylvanor, the capital, the oldest city in the world.

For months the elves fought it. They had so few soldiers that the old and the young took up bows. They shot arrows that skittered from golden scales. They poured oil and lit it. They dug pits. They sang the old green songs, and the trees themselves, as old as the world, reached down with their branches and tried to hold the beast. At last, in a final desperate hour, a band of archers found the one soft place beneath the monster's wing and loosed a hundred arrows at once. The dragon screamed and fell, and lay across the city like a mountain.

Six elves were dead. The dragon was dead. It should have been a victory.

In the middle of the fight, as if the world could not bear to be so kind, an earthquake struck the center of Sylvanor. Houses fell in streets. Old walls cracked. The great hall of the Sun-Watcher, the balcony from which Sylvas had greeted every dawn, groaned, shifted, and began to lean.

King Dua of Bierth, who had been watching the dragon fall from across the border, did not hesitate. He marched on Nile while its people were weeping over their dead, and took it without a blow. Then he gathered his small army and turned them toward Sylvanor.

In Sylvanor, King Ambe, half elf and half man, stood on the cracked balcony and watched his father's banners approach. He was, for a moment, perhaps, very young, and very afraid.

He did not have long to be afraid.

A soldier of his own guard, an elf named Tohil, stepped out of the shadows, and killed him.

No one has ever learned why. Some said Tohil had been bought by Dua's gold. Some said he was mad. Some said he was the kind of man who has no loyalties, who had waited all his life for a chance to burn something, and finally found it. Whatever the reason, Ambe died on the balcony of his mother's palace, and the crown of Aethelgard passed, by a law that no one had thought to question, to the nearest heir.

That heir was Emy, a child of three, the son of Tohil himself.

Tohil looked at the baby on the throne. He looked at the cracked ceiling. He looked at the last of the elves of Sylvanor, a few dozen old men and women, standing in the great hall with the ghosts of their dragon-slain neighbors in their eyes.

And then, in a moment that would haunt every elf who ever heard it told, he killed the king.

He killed his own son. He killed the elves who stood watching, one by one, with a calm, methodical thoroughness that no one could stop. And when the hall was silent, he walked out into the streets and set the city on fire.

Sylvanor burned. After three hundred and eighty-two years, the great capital of the elves, the city of the Sun-Watcher, the city where the first dawn had been greeted, was ash. There was no one left to mourn it, and the wind blew the cinders out over the forest like a flock of dying fireflies.

Aethelgard, the Sovereign Realm of the Enchanted Woods, had been murdered from the inside.

#C Chapter Thirteen: The Slow March

#E "We did not conquer them," the dwarven general Ones would say, years later. "We merely arrived."

In the Year 400, Grih, king of Metallica, did what he had not done in a hundred and eight years of rule. He declared war on a man.

Bierth was all that remained of the jungle's human pride. It held thirty-one humans and seven elves, with Dua at its head and only four soldiers at his back. Against it Grih sent one hundred and ninety dwarven soldiers, marching out of the Rock under banners of black and silver.

It should have been a quick war. It was not. The dwarves were old, and in no hurry, and they had a proverb: the mountain does not run. Sixty of the soldiers marched toward Nile, and they did not march so much as stroll, for it was a very long way, and the sun was warm, and the roads were soft after the long thaw. The elves of Nile, who had a handful of men and women with bows, watched the dwarves come and decided not to die.

There was no fight. The dwarves arrived, stood in the square, looked around, and Nile, by the sheer force of a presence it could not hope to match, surrendered. Tohil, the traitor of Sylvanor, who had been expected by all the world to make a last stand, did nothing at all. He watched, hooded, from a window, and did not lift a hand.

Then the dwarves walked on to Anaconda.

And here the story turned once more, for Anaconda did not surrender. The humans, whom the elves had called weak and short-lived and foolish, took up spears and axes and fought with the fury of men who had nothing left but their name. In the narrow streets of the old capital, twenty-three humans killed seventeen dwarven soldiers. It was a ratio that old dwarven generals would study for years. But it could not last. The numbers were too uneven.

In the heart of the city, in the great hall of the Jungle Federation, a dwarven general named Ones fought King Dua in a grim, close struggle, and killed him.

It was the Year 405. The war had lasted five years, though only a few months of it had been spent fighting.

When it was over, there were two humans left in Anaconda and two elves left in Nile, and both cities, those last pockets of an older world, were under the rule of Grih. Metallica, a two-hundred-and-fifty-seven-year-old empire, was the sole nation in all the world. And its ruler, Grih, was two hundred and fifty-seven years old himself. He had been on the throne for a hundred and twelve years. He had lost Rakas, his father's old kingdom, and everything in it, but he had never stopped.

In the Year 406, the soldiers returned to the Rock. There were fireworks over the black granite towers. There were feasts, and songs, and a thousand dwarves in the streets, drunk on ale and glory.

Grih, the old farmer-king, stood on the highest balcony of the Rock and looked out over the world he had won. It was nothing but dwarves, now. The elves were two. The humans were two. The orcs were long gone.

"Is this world over?" some young dwarf asked, in the happy tumult of the feast.

No one answered.

And somewhere above the clouds, where the Watchers sat, one of them said, quietly, to the other: "Should we close the chapter here?"

The other thought of a king named Sylvas, who had once conquered the world and stood in an empty hall tasting ash.

"No," said the second Watcher. "Let us spice it up."
