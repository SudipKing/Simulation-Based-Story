#C Chapter Fifteen: The Queen's Secret

#E "Not by sea."
Queen Angelica, Year 100

There were seven in the root-vault that night, besides the queen.

It lay deep under the great tree of Utica, a round chamber walled with living root, and it was where the Unister Mysin kept the things they did not show: the first horn-shed of every monarch's foal, the plans of the guns, and a lead-lined box that gave off a faint red warmth. Angelica stood beside the box. She was two hundred and sixty-seven years old.

Before her stood the captain of the Unicorn Defense; the Eye of the fleet; the Carver; the Warden of Roux, who had sailed eleven days to be there; the Master of Erant, who governed twenty souls on an empty point; and two others. One was a broad, quiet mare with a pale blaze, a commander out of Trecua who was reckoned the best mind for war the Vanguard had produced. Her name was Luxo. The other was a very old stallion with chalk dust permanently in his coat, the chief engineer of the realm, whose name was Pell.

"I am going to tell you a thing," said the queen, "and after I have told it, it will be known to the eight of us, and to the people of Erant, and to nobody else alive. If a ninth learns it from one of you, I will know which, and there will be no statue."

They understood her. Among the Unister Mysin it was the heaviest threat that could be made.

"For a hundred and fifty years," she said, "I have wanted one thing, and I have had every reason not to take it. You know what it is. You want it too. You were made wanting it." She looked at each of them. "I held back when they were burned, because I would not take a ruin. I held back when they grew, because I could not carry an army to them faster than they could kill it on the water. Twelve to a deck, thirty leagues, and every one of them an archer. I have done that sum each year since before any of you were foaled. It has never once come out."

"The fleet is four times what it was, my queen," said the Eye.

"And theirs is six times. No. I said it a hundred and fifty years ago and nobody was listening. *Not by sea.*" She turned to the old engineer. "Pell. Tell them what you told me."

Pell cleared his throat. He was not used to audiences.

"The sea between Erant and the humans' southern cape is eleven leagues across," he said. "I have had it sounded. It took thirty years, a line at a time, from fishing boats. It is not deep. There is a ridge. Some old drowned hill-chain runs most of the way, never more than twenty fathoms down and in places less than six." He drew a breath. "It can be filled. If we had sand and stone enough to raise that ridge to the surface, and broad enough at the top, then what you would have is a road."

Nobody spoke.

"A road," Luxo said at last, slowly. "From the Grove to the Savannah."

"Wide enough for forty abreast," said the queen. "Strong enough to bear every soldier I have at a gallop, and their supply behind them, and to go on bearing it. Their navy can go where it likes. Their archers can stand on every deck they own. I will walk past them."

"My queen," said the Carver carefully, "the quantity of stone..."

"Is beyond anything that has been moved since the world began. Yes." Angelica turned her great head toward the Warden of Roux. "You have been cutting statue-stone out of that headland for a hundred and sixty years. Tell them what you have found there."

"That it does not run out," said the Warden. "The whole eastern shore is stone to below the tide, and sand above it, dune on dune. I could cut a city's worth a year for a century."

"Then that is what you will do," said the queen.

#C Chapter Sixteen: The Hollowing of Roux

#E "Luck grows there. For twenty-five years, she kept it."
A saying of Erant

The first ten years were spent on preparation, and none of it was visible from the sea.

Erant was the heart of the work. Its twenty inhabitants had been there since its founding, and now they were replaced, one or two at a time, so that the number never changed. A factor in a passing grain ship would have seen the same fishing station with the same twenty unicorns. He could not have known that the twenty were now the finest builders in the Unister Mysin. They were Pell's own picked engineers, masons who had raised the walls of Trecua and shipwrights from the yards of Utica, and the best of the realm's Reach was among them. There was not one ordinary villager in the place.

They built a dock, long and deep. They built boats, and the boats looked like fishing boats and went out each dawn as fishing boats do. They did not fish. They worked the eleven leagues of water north of Erant in a slow, unceasing pattern, and when a trader strayed that way, which was rare, since the trade ran east between Utica and Sol-Vael and nothing lay west but empty sea, a pair of them would drift across its bow. Their crews spoke politely of shoals and bad currents, and of a wreck last season. The trader would bear away. In thirty-five years no human keel crossed that water.

And at Roux, behind the timber wall that Thone had once asked about, the eastern headland began to go into the sea.

*

Roux covered more ground than any city in the world but Trecua, and next to nobody lived in it.

It had thirty-two buildings and eighty-five souls. That was all it ever had, because Angelica would not let it grow. A larger town would have had traders in it, and children, and gossip. She kept it at eighty-five, and every one of the eighty-five was a quarryman. The rest of that great enclosure, league after league of it behind the wall, was pit.

They cut the stone in blocks the size of a house-end, with wedges and horn-light, a dozen unicorns lifting together until the sweat ran. They dug the sand out of the dunes behind the shore by the bargeload. All of it went down to a dock on the eastern side, which no human ship was ever permitted to use, and into the bellies of great lightless barges built for that single purpose. The barges sailed after dark. They went south and east around the tail of the Clover Fields, across open water where nobody went, and came into Erant from the west before dawn, eleven days out.

They did this for thirty-five years.

Nobody has ever reckoned the tonnage honestly. It was a hill. It was several hills. They took so much out of the eastern shore of Roux that the shore itself changed: the headland drew back, the dunes vanished, the quarry floors went below the tide and the sea came in over them. Where there had been a league of red-brown cliff there was, by the end, a league of bright shallow water over a floor of cut steps, with fish in it. It is there still. Ships' pilots on that coast call it the Queen's Bite.

And all the while the Humans were a day's ride away.

That was the audacity of it, and it needs to be understood. Sol-Clova stood on the same continent, on the north-east coast, and after the two-hundred-and-forty-eighth year Ostrich-Meadows stood nearer still. Thone himself had sat on that headland of an evening. The humans of the Clover Fields saw the dust. They saw barges, sometimes, far out, at dusk. They were told that the queen was fond of statues, and they had seen the statues, the miles of them at Utica and Trecua, and they believed it.

Some of the secret-keeping was skill and some was discipline. There was a third thing, though, and the eighty-five of Roux came to speak of it in low voices. For thirty-five years nothing went wrong. No barge foundered on the long haul or was sighted by the wrong ship, and nobody's tongue ran away with them in front of a human in a tavern. Out of a ground that dealt good fortune and bad with an even hand, they cut ten thousand cargoes of stone, and every one of them arrived.

"It's in the rock," old Pell said once, standing on the dock at Erant with his horn-light on a block as it swung ashore. "The luck. We're not just building her a road. We're building it out of the stuff."

"Good," said the Master of Erant.

"Is it?" said Pell. "Nobody's ever learned who gets to keep it. I'd like to know whether it stays lucky once it's laid, and who for."

#C Chapter Seventeen: Sand, Then Stone

#E "First a layer of sand for the foundation. Then a stone way, able to bear the whole force of the Unister Mysin if it should move."
From the plans of Pell

The laying began in the two-hundred-and-sixtieth year, and it took twenty-five.

Sand went down first. That was Pell's stroke, and it was what kept the work hidden. The barges came up from Erant by night, a league at a time along the line of the drowned ridge, opened their bottoms, and let go. The sand went down through the dark water onto the old hilltops and stayed there. Night after night for twenty years they raised that ridge, grain upon grain, until it lay like a pale road just under the sea, a few feet below the lowest tide, eleven leagues long and a bowshot wide.

Nothing showed. A human ship might have sailed straight over it and seen only a change in the colour of the water. None came, for the fishing boats of Erant saw to that, and the sea mists that hung in that strait for half the year did the rest.

Then came the stone, and that was the dangerous part, because stone stands above the water and can be seen.

They began at the Erant end and built north. The blocks of Roux went down onto the sand in courses, each locked to its neighbours by a tongue and a slot, without mortar, for the weight alone would hold them. A roadway rose out of the sea, forty unicorns wide and flat as a table, with a low kerb on either side. Angelica came to see the first league of it. She walked out to the end, alone, with the water moving on either hand, and stood there a long time looking north.

"How near can you take it," she asked Pell, "before they see it from the cape?"

"Three leagues off, on a clear day, my queen. Less in mist."

"Then build to three leagues and stop. Cut everything for the last three and stack it at Erant. When I give the word you will lay those three leagues in a single season, in the mists, and I do not care if every mason in the realm has to stand in the sea to do it."

So for years the bridge stood unfinished, eight leagues of stone road running out from the western tip of the Grove and ending in open water, pointing at the Savannah.

*

The Humans had every chance to notice, and missed each one.

The Dominion in those years was looking the other way. Its eye was on the Clover Fields, where it now had two towns and a king's affection. It was prosperous and it was old. Its council was made up of men and women of a hundred and more who had never seen a war and whose grandparents had never seen one. The watch against the man in red was still kept, for that had become a rite. But the hundred and eighty-four soldiers of Theamin's day had been drilled against one particular fear, an enemy from the sea, and every plan they had was laid on that.

In the two-hundred-and-eightieth year a fisherman of Ostrich-Bane, blown south in a squall, came home saying he had seen a wall standing in the middle of the ocean. He was known to drink. In the two-hundred-and-eighty-second, a grain factor at Utica noted that the price of the pale fruit had risen, and that an uncommon quantity seemed to be going east to Trecua, where the soldiers were. He wrote to the council. The letter was read, and filed.

King Thone was not a fool. He was a hundred and sixteen that year, the serious, sorrowful governor grown into a serious, sorrowful king, and he had counted. The count said that Trecua now held six hundred and nineteen unicorns, more than any city in the world, while Utica held only two hundred and fifty, and that this was odd. He raised it with the unicorn merchants on his quay.

"Trecua is where the young go," they told him. "It is a city of the restless. Our queen prefers them at a distance from her statues."

It was true, as the answer about the quarry had been true. He let it rest.

*

In the mists of the spring of the two-hundred-and-eighty-fifth year, Pell laid the last three leagues.

Every mason in the realm did stand in the sea. The blocks were waiting, cut and numbered. They went down in a hundred and ten days and nights, the work lit only by the masons' own horns, in fog so thick that the far end of each day's course could not be seen from the near. On the hundred-and-tenth night the final block was lowered into its slot within a long bowshot of the cape, at a place where a spit of shingle ran out under the cliffs, three leagues south of the nearest human farm.

Pell walked across. He was the first living thing to go on foot from one continent of the world to another. He stood on the shingle of the Savannah, a very old stallion with chalk in his coat, and looked up at the cliff, and found that he had no wish to go any farther.

"It's done," he told the queen, eleven days later in Utica. "It will bear all of them."

Angelica gave the bridge no name, though the humans gave it several afterwards. In the tongue of her own people it came to be called the Sovereign Bridge, since a sovereign had willed it and no one else in the realm had been asked.

She did not march that year, or the next. She had waited a hundred and eighty-five years and she spent two more making certain. The fruit went east by the shipload. The Vanguard of Trecua drilled in column on the roads of the Grove, forty abreast, until they could hold that front at a gallop. The carvers of Utica were set to a commission they did not understand. And in all that time no human went near the shingle spit under the southern cliffs, because there was nothing there.

#C Chapter Eighteen: The Tenth Likeness

#E "Two hundred and eighty-seven years of peace. I should like it recorded that she sent the statue first."
The last entry of *The Second Book of Crowns*

In the summer of the two-hundred-and-eighty-seventh year, the *Swan* came up the river-mouth to Aegis-Sol.

She was the third ship of that name. Like the first she glowed faintly on the water, and she carried her sails struck, as the *Swan* did when she brought a funeral gift. The harbour-master saw that and was puzzled, and sent a runner up to the hall to ask whether anyone of the royal house was ill.

Nobody was. The king came down himself.

A line went out and took its turn around the bollard, as lines had done at that quay for a hundred and ninety-four years. A dozen unicorns stood at the rail. There was a shape on the deck under a sailcloth, and four of them lifted it in their horn-light, and swung it out, and set it down on the stones in front of the king.

The cloth was drawn off.

It was a likeness in shining wood, one-quarter the size of life, of a man in bronze armour with a grave, lined face. It was very good work. Thone looked at his own image for some moments.

"There is some mistake," said the harbour-master. "These are given for the dead."

"There is no mistake," said the unicorn at the rail. She was broad, with a pale blaze, and she spoke the trade-tongue well. "I am Luxo, General of the Vanguard of Trecua. I am sent by Angelica, first and only queen of the Unister Mysin, to Thone, ninth king of the Sun-Gilded Dominion. She bids me say that she has traded with your house for a hundred and eighty years and found it honest. She thought it right that you should have this while you could still see it." She inclined her head, very slightly. "We are at war. I am sorry for it. You have been good neighbours."

The line slid off the bollard. The *Swan* went out with the tide.

*

Thone stood on the quay until she was out of sight. Then he went up to the hall, and sat down under the shining bough that Angelica had sent his grandfather, and called for the tally.

It was all he had, and he was his house's truest heir in that he knew how to read it.

The Unister Mysin numbered one thousand and thirty-six. Of these, five hundred and fifty-three were soldiers: eight at Erant, three hundred and seventy-four at Trecua, a hundred and forty-five at Utica, and twenty-six at Roux.

The Sun-Gilded Dominion numbered eight hundred and sixty-eight. Of these, three hundred and ninety-seven were soldiers: a hundred and sixty-six at Aegis-Sol, eighty-six at Sol-Vael, ninety-two at Ostrich-Bane, and fifty-three across the strait at Sol-Clova. At Ostrich-Meadows, which he had founded himself, there were none.

"Three hundred and ninety-seven against five hundred and fifty-three," said his marshal. "And each of theirs is worth..."

"I know what each of theirs is worth." The king was looking at the map. "Where will she land? She has, what, thirty transports? Twelve to a deck. She cannot put four hundred ashore in one wave. Put the fleet off Sol-Vael and..."

A rider came in then from the south, on a horse that died in the courtyard. He had come from Ostrich-Bane in three days. He was a shepherd, and he had gone down to the shingle spit under the southern cliffs after a strayed ewe.

He told them what was there.

It is recorded that King Thone did not say anything for a long while. Then he laid his hand flat on the map, over the empty blue between the cape and the western point of the Grove, where a factor had once written *fishing station*.

"Stone," he said. "She told me herself. *We cut stone.*" He closed his eyes. "I sat on that headland and watched the dust go up, and thought about fish."

*

Above the mist, the Player had the air of one who has been waiting a very long time for a turn.

"Two hundred and eighty-seven years," it said. "You asked for one hundred. Then you asked me to take my hand away and see what they would do. I took it away. I have not touched her since. Everything she did, the dock, the ridge, the sand, the lie about the statues, she did herself." It sounded almost proud. "Finally it has become interesting. Would you not say so, Watcher?"

The Watcher looked at an old king with his hand on a map.

"Who will win?" asked the Player.

"Yours," said the Watcher. "She outnumbers him by a hundred and fifty-six, and she has turned his navy into a spectator. He has one chance. The bridge is eleven leagues long and forty wide and there are eight soldiers at the far end of it. If he can get there before Trecua does, a few hundred archers could hold that mouth against anything."

"Trecua is at the other end of the Grove."

"Yes. So it is a race." The Watcher was silent for a moment. "He has seen it. Look. He is already writing the order."

#B BOOK FIVE|The Sovereign War|Years 288 to 297

#C Chapter Nineteen: The Ninety-Two

#E "They were ordered to take the far end of the bridge and hold it until relieved. They did the first."
*Annals of Ostrich-Bane*

The order reached Ostrich-Bane in the first days of the two-hundred-and-eighty-eighth year. *Cross now. Do not wait for Sol-Vael. Take the far end and hold it.*

The garrison of the south was ninety-two strong. They were the descendants of the people who had cleared the plain of the great birds and been burned out by Burcien and come back, and they had a name in the Dominion for being hard to discourage. Their captain was a rangy woman of sixty named Sefa Harrow. She read the order, and then she walked her people three leagues south to the shingle spit and showed them the bridge.

It ran straight out from the foot of the cliff, pale and level, wider than the main street of their town, until it was lost in the sea-haze. There was no rail on it and no shelter anywhere along its length. The swell broke in a white line along each kerb.

"Eleven leagues," said Sefa Harrow. "Two days, if we don't sleep much. The king thinks there's next to nobody at the other end. He's usually right." She looked along the rank. "I'll not order it. Anyone who'd sooner hold the cliff can hold the cliff."

Nobody stayed on the cliff.

*

They were seen on the first afternoon.

The fishing boats of Erant had never been fishing boats. They came up out of the haze on both sides of the roadway, six of them, low and quick, and for the first time in thirty-five years they ran out what they had been carrying under their nets. Each mounted a single gun in the bow.

A bridge without a rail, across open water, is a hard place to be shot at. There was nothing to get behind. Sefa Harrow strung her people out in a long loose file so that no one ball could take more than two or three, and she kept them moving, at a jog, hour after hour, while the boats stood off at two hundred paces and fired, wore round, and fired again. The archers of Ostrich-Bane shot back as they ran. They killed gunners. They set one boat alight with fire-arrows and cheered, hoarsely, as she burned to the water. It did not stop the others.

They ran through that night and the day after it. By the time the sun went down on the second evening they had passed the midpoint of the bridge, a place marked by nothing but a slight rise in the roadway where the drowned ridge stood highest. There Sefa Harrow called a halt and counted her people. Forty answered.

Fifty-two had died on the first half of the road, most of them carried off the kerb by shot into the sea.

"We could go back," someone said.

"It's as far back as on," said Sefa Harrow. That was true, and they went on.

The boats could not follow them to the end. Near Erant the roadway ran in among reefs, and the guns fell silent, and on the fourth morning forty men and women of Ostrich-Bane came off the southern end of the Sovereign Bridge onto purple earth, under trees that went up into the cloud. They were the first army of Humans ever to stand on the Celestial Grove.

The eight of Erant were waiting for them.

They were the soldiers of the place, the eight the tally had counted, and they had been posted there to guard a secret and not to fight a battle. They knew that the Vanguard of Trecua was coming and that it was days away. They could have fallen back among the great trunks and waited. A human commander would have done so.

They were unicorns. They put their heads down and charged forty archers in the open.

It was the first time the two peoples ever met in battle, and what the Dominion learned from it shaped the war. The eight did not stand a chance, and they nearly won. Arrows that would have dropped a lion stood in their hides like quills and did not slow them. They went through the human line as a plough goes through stubble, turned, and came back. One of them killed five soldiers before she fell. They were brought down the way Burcien had once been brought down, by a great many arrows from three sides and the patience to keep loosing, and when the last of the eight lay still, Sefa Harrow had twenty-six left on their feet.

Twenty-six people held the mouth of the bridge, and there was nothing else between Angelica and a closed road. They dragged the dock timbers of Erant across the roadway for a barricade, and they waited to see who would arrive first.

#C Chapter Twenty: The Shields

#E "We turned our horns to the dark between the trees and asked. It was the only time I ever saw the queen ask for anything."
Ysolt the Fourth, Carver of Utica

The eighty-six of Sol-Vael came over the bridge on the ninth day.

They had run the same gauntlet and lost fewer to it, for by then the boats of Erant were short of powder. They came off the roadway into the arms of Sefa Harrow's twenty-six, and for one evening the Humans on that shore believed that the thing might be done. There were a hundred of them, and they held a front forty paces wide, with the sea on both flanks and a breastwork of ship's timbers before them. Every one of them had a bow, and the arrows of two cities were stacked behind the line.

"A hundred, in a gap that size," said the captain of Sol-Vael, a grizzled man named Dorn Achter. "I'd hold that against a thousand of anything."

"You've not seen them come on," said Sefa Harrow.

The Vanguard of Trecua arrived the next morning.

It had come the whole length of the Grove at the pace Luxo had drilled into it, forty abreast, and the purple earth shook under it for an hour before it was seen. Then it came out from among the great trunks, rank on rank, with the pale light of the boughs on three hundred and seventy-four horns. It halted a long bowshot from the barricade. Luxo walked out alone in front of it and looked at the breastwork, and the sea on either side, and the hundred bows.

The archers held their fire. She was not within fair range, and they could count as well as their king.

"She's not what I expected," said Dorn Achter.

"What did you expect?"

"That they'd just charge. Like the eight."

"So did I," said Sefa Harrow.

*

Luxo did not charge, and that made her the most dangerous creature the Unister Mysin ever bred.

She had the love of blood in her like the rest. But she had been in the root-vault thirty-eight years before and had heard the queen do the sum, and she had done one of her own since. A soldier killed at the head of a column could not be led into the next battle. She had never herself struck a blow in anger. Among her people this was thought eccentric, and by some shameful, and she had ignored that. She meant to bring her Vanguard to the far side of that bridge with as many alive as could be managed.

She looked at the gap. It was forty paces of front, where her numbers counted for nothing, against a people who had killed eight of her kind with arrows alone.

She sent to Utica.

What followed was a thing the Unister Mysin had done perhaps three times in their history. On the queen's word, in all four cities at once, every unicorn who was not a soldier came out into the avenues of statues: carvers and shipwrights, fruit-shakers, foals and the very old. They turned away from the likenesses, and faced the dark between the trunks where the watching thing lived, and lowered their horns.

They prayed. Angelica stood at their head under the great tree, with her horn to the ground like the rest. Nobody alive had seen her bow.

*

The prayer was felt above the mist. It came up like heat.

"They are asking me," said the Player. It sounded startled. "By name, very nearly. They have brushed against me every still night for three hundred years, and now they are asking."

"We agreed to take our hands away," said the Watcher.

"From *choosing* for them. I have chosen nothing. She built the bridge. She declared the war. Now a whole people asks me a thing, of its own will, and you would have me not answer?" The Player was quiet. "Yours never asked. Not once. After Sol-Vael I think they were afraid to find out that someone was listening."

The Watcher looked at a hundred archers behind a pile of timber.

"No," it said, heavily. "They never asked. Answer, then. It is within the rules. I only wish it were not."

*

On the morning of the twelfth day, the hundred behind the barricade saw the Vanguard change.

It began at the front rank and went back through the column like wind over grass. Around each soldier the air thickened and took light. It was a pale, curved gleam, like a pane of the boughs' own shining, that hung before her chest and moved as she moved. Three hundred and seventy-four shields stood in the air under the great trees, and the soldiers behind them stamped and tossed their heads as though they had drunk something strong.

"Loose," said Dorn Achter, and a hundred arrows went out, and most of them rang off the shields and fell.

Then Luxo sent them in.

The fight for the mouth of the bridge, and then for the bridge itself, lasted the better part of two years, and no part of the Sovereign War was harder. The Humans did not break. When the barricade went they fell back onto the roadway, where forty paces of front was all the enemy could bring against them, and they held it league by league, behind breastworks of their own dead and of blocks prised up from the kerb. Aegis-Sol fed them arrows and reliefs along the road behind. They learned to shoot low, under the shields, at the legs. They learned that a unicorn brought down in the front rank blocked the two behind her. In those two years they killed sixty-eight of the Vanguard, which was more than anyone had thought could be done.

But the shields did not fade, and the Vanguard did not tire as people tire, and it had the pale fruit behind it by the cartload. By the two-hundred-and-ninetieth year the last human breastwork stood on the shingle spit under the southern cliffs of the Savannah, at the end of the road Pell had walked. On a grey morning in the spring it was ridden down.

Three hundred and six soldiers of Trecua came off the Sovereign Bridge onto the golden grass.

Sefa Harrow was not among those who fell back up the cliff path. She had been killed in the last hour of the last stand, eleven leagues from where she started and on the soil of her own country.

#C Chapter Twenty-One: The Fall of the South

#E "He had been a governor and a mourner and a counter of grain. On the last day he was a king."
A survivor of Sol-Vael

Ostrich-Bane stood nearest the bridge, and it was the first to fall.

Its soldiers were gone; they lay along eleven leagues of roadway and under the sea on either side. What remained in the town was its people, who under the Dominion's old law all kept a bow behind the door. They fought for it. The annals of the Unister Mysin, which are not generous to enemies, call it *a fierce struggle*, and note that the town had to be taken house by house and that some of the houses had to be taken twice. It lasted most of a season. At the end of it Ostrich-Bane, which had been burned by Burcien and built again, flew a violet banner, and the Vanguard turned north along the coast road toward the white town on the cliff.

King Thone was waiting for them at Sol-Vael.

His marshal had begged him to stay in the capital. He had a hundred and sixty-six soldiers at Aegis-Sol behind the best walls in the realm, and the king was a hundred and twenty-five years old. But Thone had done the sum, as his house always did. If Sol-Vael went, Aegis-Sol stood alone, and a city alone could only wait to die. Whatever chance remained lay on the cliff where Valerius had once set a cairn. So he left the capital its garrison, and took every volunteer who could draw a bow, and went down to his great-grandfather's town in his great-grandfather's bronze.

With him went his cousin Bruhis, the daughter of Treiala, who had been passed over for the crown seventy-three years before and had spent the time since commanding the militia of the capital. She was the same age as the king. She was a hard, practical woman, and she did not think they would win.

"You should be behind a wall," she told him, on the road.

"So should you."

"I am not the last of Theamin's blood."

"No," said Thone. "You are what comes after it. Stay near me. If I fall, I want the crown picked up before it stops rolling."

*

They held Sol-Vael for a year.

It was the second great siege of that town, and those who knew the history felt it keenly. Their ancestors had held these same streets for three years against one man in red, from the cellars and the sea-caves, with bows. They did it again now, against three hundred. The white stone houses stood wall to wall, and the lanes between them were too narrow for a unicorn to turn in. Every roof had archers. For month after month the Vanguard ground its way into the town a lane at a time and was shot at from above.

Luxo did not waste her soldiers. She took a street, fortified it, and took the next. She was always behind the line, where a commander should be, watching.

Thone was always in front of his. He did not have to be, and nobody had asked it of him. But the man who had sat down in the clover when the tablet came, and had been crowned looking like someone under a load, was seen in that last year to have put the load down. He went from roof to roof in the old bronze. He knew every archer's name. He shot well, for a man of his years, and he was heard to laugh, which nobody in the capital had ever heard.

He was killed in the autumn of the two-hundred-and-ninety-first year, in the market square where Burcien had once sat on the steps of the cross. A lane had given way, and the line was breaking, and he went down into the square on foot with a dozen of his guard to hold the gap while the roofs were cleared. They held it. It took a charge of nine soldiers of the Vanguard to end the thing, and when it was ended the ninth king of the Sun-Gilded Dominion lay under the market cross of Sol-Vael in the armour of the first.

Bruhis took the crown off his head with her own hands. There was no ceremony. She put it on over her helmet, in the middle of a street fight, and shouted for the left of the line to fall back to the granary. So she became the tenth monarch of the Dominion.

*

She fought on for two more years. Nobody could say what she fought for by the end, except that stopping seemed worse.

The Vanguard killed that town. No other word serves. It was not done in a single sack, hot and quick. Lane by lane, over two years, the people of Sol-Vael were killed where they stood, and they did stand. In the spring of the two-hundred-and-ninety-third year Queen Bruhis sat in the cellar of the last house on the cliff and had her people counted.

There were five, and she was one of them.

She thought about that number for some time. She had read Thesia's book. *Five, and then three. Remember those numbers.* The same town had once before been burned down to five, on the same cliff, and she no longer thought it could be chance.

"Someone is laughing at us," she said.

Then she got up, took off the crown, and went out under a white cloth to find the general of the Vanguard.

They met in the market square, by the cross.

Bruhis was a hundred and twenty-seven years old and had not slept properly in two years. She stood very straight. The general of the Vanguard came to her without a guard and without haste, a broad mare with a pale blaze, and stopped at a courteous distance.

"You are the queen," Luxo said, in the trade-tongue.

"I am what is left of one. I have come to give you this town."

"I have this town."

"You have its streets. You have not got its surrender, and you want it. Aegis-Sol is watching what happens here. If Sol-Vael is seen to yield and its queen is seen to live, the capital may think that yielding is a thing that can be survived. If we die in a cellar, they will fight you for every stone as we did, and you have lost sixty-eight already." Bruhis held the general's eye. "I can count too. I offer you Sol-Vael, formally, under my seal, with whatever that is worth to you on the road north. I ask one thing. I should like to see what comes after this, even as a prisoner. I ask for my life."

Luxo considered it. She took her time, as she did with everything.

"And the other four?"

"I ask for theirs as well. I can only bargain for my own."

"That is honest." The general inclined her head. "Very well. In the name of Angelica, Queen of the Unister Mysin: the town for your life. It is promised."

Bruhis had the seal brought. She set it to a tablet by the cross, in front of four witnesses of her own people and forty of the Vanguard, and Sol-Vael passed from the Sun-Gilded Dominion to the Unister Mysin. The cliff with the cairn on it went too, and the lamp that Angelica had once looked at across thirty leagues of water. The violet banner went up on what was left of the watchtower. The citadel gates were opened, and the last four defenders came out of the cellar with their hands empty.

When it was done, and the tablet was in the hands of her adjutant, Luxo walked up to the Queen of the Dominion and killed her.

It was done quickly, with the horn, a single thrust. Bruhis had time to look surprised and that was all.

The soldiers of the Vanguard were silent. They had served under Luxo for six years of war and had never seen her strike anything.

"You promised," said one of the four prisoners. He was an old man, and past fear. "You gave your queen's word."

"I gave my queen's word that the town would buy her life," said Luxo. She was cleaning her horn on the grass by the cross. "It did. She had it, from the moment she asked until the moment the gates were open. I did not say how long." She looked at the old man without any anger. "Understand me. Your people have had ten crowned heads in three hundred years, and each time one falls another gets up. My queen did not build a road across the sea to leave one of them in a prison where it might be rescued. As long as one of that blood is breathing, your people have a place to gather. I am ending that."

"Then why make the bargain at all?"

"Because she was right. The capital is watching. I wanted the gates of this town opened to me, in daylight, under seal." The general straightened. "And I wanted to see whether she would ask for the other four first. She did. It was well done. I will see that she has a likeness."

The four were spared and sent to the quarry at Roux, and one of them lived to tell it.

Whether Angelica had ordered the killing or only made it possible, nobody ever learned. When the report reached Utica she read it without expression and gave instructions for a statue of the human queen, one-quarter size, to be placed in the hall with the others.

"That is ten," she said. "There is one more."
