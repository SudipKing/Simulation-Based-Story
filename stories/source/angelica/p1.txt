#C Prologue: The Watcher and the Player

#E Three lands, one ocean, and two who leaned over the water to see what would come of it.

Before there was a year to number, there was the Great Ocean, and it had no shore.

It lay grey and patient under a sky that had not yet decided on a colour, and mist stood on it the way breath stands on a cold morning. Nothing swam in it. Nothing sailed on it. It was waiting, although it did not know for what, and it would have gone on waiting for ever if two powers had not come to stand above it.

They had no bodies that a mortal eye could have fixed upon. Later ages, which knew of them only by rumour and by one terrible season of madness, gave them names that were closer to titles. The first was called the Watcher, because it preferred to set a thing in motion and then keep still. The second was called the Player, because it could not keep still at all.

"Three," said the Player, and three lands rose dripping out of the mist.

The first came up in the north, broad and rugged, with jagged peaks along its spine and gold already showing in its grass. The second surfaced far to the south of it, long from east to west like a blade laid flat upon the water. The third rose in the west, low and round and green, and it was the quietest of the three.

"One for me, one for you," said the Player. "You choose first."

The Watcher looked a long time at the northern land. It saw sun on tall grass, flat-topped acacias standing alone like sentries, rivers that ran full in one season and thin in the next, and hills with good pale stone in them.

"Savanna," it said. "A country that gives a great deal and then, some years, gives nothing. I want to see what a people makes of that. I will put Humans there. They adapt, they build quickly, and they are never content with the hill they are standing on."

It gave them a first king, and it gave the king a name: Valerius. It gave him bronze armour, because it liked the way bronze took the sunrise, and it named his city Aegis-Sol, the Shield of the Sun, and his realm the Sun-Gilded Dominion. And because the savanna would be hard on them, the Watcher gave the people of the Dominion one gift more, which they afterwards called the Long Sun: left alone by blade, beast and fever, a man or woman of that country might see a hundred and fifty years, and some saw more.

"Generous," said the Player.

"Long lives make long memories," said the Watcher. "I want them to remember what happens to them."

Then the Player bent over the southern land, and where its attention fell the earth changed colour. The soil turned the soft purple of the sky an hour after sunset. Trees rose out of it and did not stop rising; they went up past the height at which trees are sensible and on into the cloud, and their boughs gave off a pale, steady shining, so that it was never fully dark beneath them. A single kind of fruit hung in those boughs, pale and round and faintly luminous, like small moons that had been caught in the branches. In time it would be called the Celestial Avocado, and whole armies would march on it.

"The Celestial Grove," said the Player. "And for the Grove, Unicorns."

The Watcher considered this. "Gentle creatures, in most tellings."

"Not in mine," said the Player. "Mine are tough, and defiant, and they love blood the way yours love building. They will raise statues of themselves and expect the world to come and look." The Player set a queen among them, taller by half than any of her kind, with a well of mana in her so deep that the air around her mended small wounds without being asked. "Her name is Angelica. Her realm is the Unister Mysin, and her city is Utica."

"And the third land?"

The two of them looked west together. Clover covered that country from shore to shore, and something moved in the clover that was neither wind nor animal. Things went right there, or wrong there, a little more often than they should have.

"Luck grows in that soil," said the Player. "I can feel it. What I cannot tell is who gets to keep it. Leave it empty for now."

So the Clover Fields were left empty, and the two powers settled above the mist to see what their peoples would do.

"When do they fight?" asked the Player. "I will hold them back until you say. No war is declared until the year you name."

The Watcher thought about bronze, and about how long it takes to build a thing worth losing.

"Give them a century," it said. "Year One Hundred."

It was a reasonable answer, and it was wrong by one hundred and eighty-seven years.

#C Chapter One: The King in Bronze

#E "He was crowned at sunrise, and for twenty-five years he behaved as though the sunrise had been arranged for him."
Thesia of Aegis-Sol, *The First Book of Crowns*

The first thing the people of the Savannah ever saw was their king with the dawn behind him.

They woke on the slope of a limestone hill above a river valley, a few dozen of them, with no memory of any earlier night. Below them the grass ran gold to the horizon. Above them, on the crest of the hill, stood a man in bronze, and the sun came up directly behind his shoulders so that for a moment he seemed to be made of it. Nobody elected Valerius. Nobody needed to. When he walked down the hill and pointed at the pale rock and said that this was where the wall would stand, they went and found something to dig with.

That was Year 0, and in that same year a girl was born beside the first cook-fire, before there was a roof in the whole country to be born under. Her mother named her Thesia. She was the first child of Aegis-Sol, and she was to outlive nearly everything described in this book.

The Savannah taught its lessons early. The rivers that ran brimming in the wet months shrank to brown threads in the dry ones. The grain that came up thick the first year came up thin the second, and in the third year a wind out of the interior laid a whole field flat in an afternoon. The people learned to store, and to count what they stored. They cut the limestone into blocks and set a timber palisade on a footing of stone. They learned which acacias gave shade and which gave thorns, and which of the animals that grazed the plain would run from a shout and which would not.

Valerius was everywhere in those years. He hauled stone with the masons and stood watch with the watchmen, and when the quarry in the heart of the hill was first opened he went down the shaft before anyone else, to show that it could be done. Men said of him that he had never once looked at a thing without wondering what was behind it. He walked the borders of his little realm himself, farther each season, and each season he came back with a new piece of the map in his head: a salt pan, a stand of fever trees, a line of cliffs in the west where the land stopped and the ocean began.

From those cliffs, on clear evenings, his scouts reported a smudge of colour on the southern horizon. They called it purple and then argued over the word, since nothing in their country was that colour except the sky at dusk. Valerius went to see for himself. He stood on the cliff edge for an hour in his bronze, looking at a stain of violet light where there should have been only sea.

"There is land there," he said at last. "And something on it that shines at night."

His people had no ships. They had barely learned to make a door that hung straight. But the king had the cliff marked with a cairn, and he said that a city would stand there one day and that it would look south. He did not live to see it, and neither did most of those who heard him say it. It stood all the same.

*

In those first years the Dominion grew slowly and steadily. There were births in Aegis-Sol nearly every season, and under the Long Sun very few deaths. By the twenty-fifth year thirty-nine people lived within the palisade, which does not sound like a kingdom, and was one. They had a granary, a quarry, a law about the sharing of water, and a king whose bronze they could see from the fields. They also had an heir. Valerius had a son named Theamin, born in the thirteenth year, a quiet, watchful boy who took after his mother and liked to sit on the wall and count things.

Thesia, who was thirteen years older than the prince, was often set to mind him. She wrote long afterwards that he once counted every acacia visible from the north gate, and then counted them again the next morning to see whether any had moved.

"They don't move," she told him.

"They haven't yet," said Theamin.

She recorded this in the first year of her own reign, when she was a hundred and sixty years old. She did not live to see it come true, and it is as well that she did not.

#C Chapter Two: The Queen Beneath the Boughs

#E "Something watches from beyond the trunks. When the wind stills, it brushes back."
A saying of Utica

Far to the south, in a country where the ground was the colour of dusk, fourteen unicorns stood in a ring around a tree too wide to see past.

They were not the unicorns of the gentle tellings. A grown mare of the Unister Mysin stood taller at the shoulder than a man stands with his arm raised. Their hides turned a spear-thrust that would have killed a savanna bull, and their horns were weapons first and ornaments a long way second. They had no hands. They had the Reach instead, the horn-light, a pale glow that gathered at the tip of the horn and went out from it like a second limb. With the Reach a unicorn could lift a beam, turn a chisel, knot a rope or thread a needle, and a strong one could do all four at once. It tired them as lifting tires an arm. It was the reason they could build, and they built constantly.

At the centre of the ring stood Angelica.

She was seventeen years old when the world began, which made her the only being in it who had an age before there were years. She did not find this strange. She was taller than the tallest of her people by half, and where she stood the air felt different, close and warm, like the air over a spring. A stallion who had gashed his flank on violet stone the day before stood near her without knowing why he had drifted that way, and by evening the gash was a pale seam. That was the aura. She did not send it out. It simply came off her, the way heat comes off a stone that has lain in the sun.

They crowned her under that tree. There was no crown as the Humans would have understood it; the unicorns had no use for a thing that sat where the horn already was. Instead each of the fourteen went up to her in turn, lowered the horn until it touched hers, and let a thread of horn-light pass across. When the last had done so her own horn burned so bright that the fruit in the boughs high above showed like a field of small moons.

It is told that she looked up then, and that she stood very still.

Every unicorn in the Grove knew the feeling she had at that moment, because every one of them had had it. Out past the trunks, where the shining of the boughs gave way to the dark between the trees, something was paying attention. It never showed itself. Now and then, when the wind dropped, a traveller alone on the purple earth would feel it pass close, like a great flank brushing lightly along their own. Most of them bolted. Angelica, on the day she was crowned, leaned into it.

She never said what she felt. But from that day she spoke of the watching thing as one speaks of a patron, and when her people prayed, which they did rarely and only in great need, it was toward the dark between the trunks that they turned their horns.

*

She called the place Utica, and the first thing she built there was a statue of herself.

This has been held against her by chroniclers of the other shore, who saw vanity in it. It was vanity, and a unicorn would have been puzzled by the charge. Among the Unister Mysin the worst fate that could befall anyone was to go unlooked-at. A deed nobody saw had not properly happened. A life nobody remembered had been a kind of rumour. So they made likenesses. They carved them from the glowing wood of fallen boughs and from the violet stone of the hills, and they set them along the ways into Utica with their horns levelled at whoever approached, and each new foal was walked down that avenue and told the names.

Angelica's statue stood twice her own considerable height at the end of the avenue, under the great tree. When it was finished she walked around it once and said that it was too small.

"Too small for what, my queen?" asked the mare who had led the carving. Her name was Ysolt, and she was the first Carver of Utica.

"For the distance," said Angelica. "I want it seen from farther off."

"From the edge of the city?"

"From the edge of the world," said the queen. She had not meant it as a plan when she said it. It became one over the centuries that followed.

They were few, in those years, and they stayed few for a long while. A unicorn mare carried a foal for the better part of two years and seldom bore a second within ten. By the twenty-fifth year, when the Humans across the water numbered thirty-nine, the Unister Mysin numbered fourteen and a handful of foals. It did not trouble them. They lived on the pale fruit of the high boughs, which the strongest of them shook down with the Reach, and the fruit made them stronger still. They sparred in the avenues until the purple earth was churned and spattered, and came away laughing, and stood near the queen until the wounds closed. The love of blood that the Player had put in them was real, and for the present it was spent on one another, and nothing came of it worse than scars.

Angelica watched them fight and did not join in. She had found, quite early, that she had something they lacked. They wanted the next blow. She could wait for the last one.

#C Chapter Three: The Ostrich

#E "Tell it plainly. He walked up to a bird and the bird killed him. There is nothing in that to dress."
King Theamin, to his chroniclers

In the twenty-fifth year of the world, in the dry season, King Valerius rode out to look at the eastern grass.

He took a small guard and then, as he often did, left it behind. A river had changed its course out there and he wanted to see the new channel. His guards were told to wait with the horses under a stand of fever trees. They let him go. Twenty-five years had taught them that the king walked where he liked and came back with something useful.

He had gone perhaps four hundred paces when he saw the ostrich.

The Savannah was full of animals in those days, and the people had sorted most of them into the dangerous and the harmless. Lions were dangerous. So were the river-horses, and the horned bulls in the rut. The great flightless birds that stalked the plain on legs like stripped saplings had been placed, without much thought, among the harmless. They ran from hunters. They ran from children. One of them had once run from a dropped cooking pot.

This one did not run. It stood alone in the tall grass with its head high and one eye on the king, and it was as tall as he was, armour and all.

Nobody knows what Valerius meant to do. His captain of guards, a woman named Maren who watched the whole thing from the fever trees, said afterwards that he held out his hand the way you would to a strange dog. He may have thought it could be tamed and ridden. He may only have wanted, as he had always wanted, to see what was behind it. He walked up to it slowly with his palm open, the bronze on his chest flashing in the sun, and when he was a single pace away the bird struck.

An ostrich kicks forward and down, and there is a claw on the great toe as long as a man's finger. The kick took him below the breastplate. He went over backwards into the grass without a sound, and the bird stood over him for one moment more, and then it turned and ran. It was a hundred yards off before Maren had her spear out of its sling and a mile off before she reached him.

He was dead when she knelt down. The first king of the first city, who had gone first down the quarry shaft and stood first on the western cliffs, had been killed by a bird in a field, four hundred paces from his own guard, on a day with no enemy in it at all.

They carried him home on his shield.

*

Above the mist, the Player laughed, and then stopped.

"I did not do that," it said.

"Nor I," said the Watcher. It was quiet for a time. "I gave him curiosity so that he would cross oceans. I had not thought what else it might walk him toward."

"Shall I put him back?"

"No. We agreed. What they do, they do. What befalls them, befalls." The Watcher looked down at the city on the limestone hill, where thirty-eight people were learning that a king could die. "Besides, this may be the most useful thing he ever taught them."

*

The crown went to Theamin, who was twelve.

There was no question of anyone else. The people had been made knowing they had a king, and the king had one son, and the son stood at the pyre with his jaw set and did not cry until it was dark. Maren, who had failed to reach his father in time and never forgave herself, stood behind his chair for the rest of her life. Thesia, twenty-five years old and already the person in Aegis-Sol whom others asked when they wished to know what had been decided last year, was set at his right hand to teach him the reckoning of grain.

The boy king gave one order of his own in that first season. He had the eastern grass burned back a full mile from the fields, so that nothing could stand in it unseen. Then he went back to sitting on the wall and counting.

For as long as the Dominion lasted its people never quite trusted the wild country again. Mothers in Aegis-Sol told their children that the Savannah had killed the greatest man who ever lived on a day when it was not even angry. Soldiers carried their spears unslung beyond the wall. The name of the bird became a curse and later, as will be told, part of the name of a city. Something had gone out of them that Valerius had put there, a readiness to walk up to the unknown with an open hand, and in its place Theamin planted a habit of caution.

It is hard to say whether that was loss or gain. Caution kept them alive through the fire that was coming, and it also kept them from looking closely at a little city called Erant.

#C Chapter Four: The Boy King and the White Ship

#E "My father wanted to know what lay behind everything. I have only ever wanted to know how much of it there is."
King Theamin

Theamin grew into his crown slowly and wore it for a hundred and thirty-five years.

He was nothing like his father. Valerius had been all forward motion; his son moved the way a river in the dry season moves, hardly at all, and always downhill toward the place he meant to reach. He counted the people each spring. He counted the stores each autumn. He learned from Thesia that a granary is a kind of argument with the future, and from Maren that a wall is only as long as the number of people you have to stand on it. He did not found anything for twelve years.

Then, in the thirty-seventh year, when he was twenty-four and the palisade of Aegis-Sol could no longer hold the houses inside it, he led a column of masons and settlers west to the cliffs where his father's cairn stood, and broke ground beside it.

"What shall it be called?" Thesia asked him.

He looked at the cairn for a while. "Sol-Vael," he said. "The sun's, and Valerius's. He said a city would stand here and look south. Let it have his name while it looks."

Sol-Vael went up on the cliff top above a shelving beach, a town of white stone and thatch with a watchtower on the seaward side. It grew grain on the plateau behind it and kept a lamp burning in the tower at night, though in those years there was nobody at sea to see it. Its people were proud of the view and a little uneasy about it. On clear evenings the violet stain still showed on the southern horizon, and nobody could say what it was.

They found out in the sixty-sixth year.

*

By then things had moved in the Grove as well.

The Unister Mysin had grown from fourteen to nearly fifty. In the sixty-fourth year a company of them went east under the boughs for nine days and raised a second city where the purple hills came down to a deep bay. They called it Trecua, and they built it as they built everything, heavy and deliberate, with walls of violet stone and beams of shining wood, and an avenue of statues before the first house had a roof. Trecua was meant from the start to be the hard edge of the realm, the place where the young and the restless went to learn fighting from those who had made an art of it. It was to become the greatest city in the world, and the Sovereign War was fought with its soldiers.

In the sixty-fifth year, at Utica, the unicorns built a dock.

It was Angelica's idea, and her people did not at first see the point. They were a land folk. But the queen had stood on her northern shore on clear nights and seen what the scouts of Valerius had seen from the other side: a small light on the far horizon, low down, where no star should be. It was the watch-lamp of Sol-Vael. She looked at it across thirty leagues of water, night after night, and it galled her as nothing in her life had.

Somebody was over there. They had lit a lamp, and they had not come to look at her.

So the dock was built, and fishing boats were set upon the water to teach her people the ways of hulls and wind. They learned fast. The Reach, which could hold a chisel steady, could hold a tiller too, and a rope, and a sail's edge in a gale. Within the year Ysolt the Carver and a master named Corvane had laid the keel of something larger: a deep-bellied transport, broad enough for a dozen unicorns to stand abreast on her deck, with a hull of shining wood that glowed faintly on the water at night. In her bow they mounted a thing the world had not seen before. It was a tube of cast violet iron as long as a stallion, and it was fed with a black powder that Corvane had made, at some cost in eyebrows, from the salts of the purple hills.

The first time they fired it, the report rolled the length of the bay and every bird in the high boughs went up at once. Angelica was standing beside the piece. She did not flinch, and when the smoke cleared she was seen to be smiling.

They named the ship the *Swan*, for her white glow and her long neck of a prow. In the sixty-sixth year she sailed north.

*

The watchman in the tower at Sol-Vael that morning was a boy of fifteen named Dace, and he lived to be very old, and he told the story on every one of the days in between.

He said that the mist came up thick off the sea at dawn, as it often did, and that he was half asleep when he saw a paleness in it. He took it for the sun and then saw that it was in the wrong place. The paleness grew, and took a shape, and came out of the mist as a ship larger than any building in Sol-Vael. No human hand had ever built a ship. He did not have a word for what he was looking at, and called it afterwards a house that walked on the water.

It glowed. Its sails were the colour of bone. And along its rail, perfectly still, stood a row of creatures that he took at first for horses, until he saw the horns, and saw how high above the rail their heads came, and saw that every one of them was looking at him.

He rang the bell. The town came out onto the cliff with what weapons it had. And the white ship slid along the coast below them, close in, at the pace of a man walking, and nobody on her deck moved or called out, and no boat was lowered. Dace said the worst of it was the quiet. He could hear the water along her side. He could hear, he swore, the creatures breathing.

She passed the length of the cliffs and went on up the coast toward Aegis-Sol. Two days later the fishermen's children saw her come back, standing farther out, and by the third evening she was a pale fleck going south into the violet.

King Theamin came down to Sol-Vael himself and heard the tale from forty mouths. He was fifty-three that year, which in the Dominion was a young man's age, and he listened with his usual stillness. He asked how many creatures had stood at the rail. He asked how long the ship was, measured against the watchtower. He asked about the iron tube in the bow, which several had noticed and none had understood.

"They came to count us," he said finally. "It is what I would have done."

"Will they come back?" Thesia asked.

"Oh yes," said the king. "You do not build a thing like that for one voyage."

*

He was right. The *Swan* came back the next year, and the year after. She never landed. She hung off the cliffs of Sol-Vael and the river-mouth below Aegis-Sol, sometimes for an hour and sometimes for a day, and her crew stood at the rail and looked, and then she went home.

On her deck, on the first of those voyages, had stood a young mare named Quilla, who had sharp eyes and a mind like a ledger. When the ship returned to Utica, Quilla went before the queen under the great tree and recited what she had seen: one town on the cliffs with a single tower, one larger town on a hill by a river with a wooden wall, fields, smoke, and a people who went on two legs and stood about as high as a unicorn's chest.

"Small," Angelica said.

"Small, my queen. But there were a great many of them. I counted more on that one cliff than we have in Utica."

"Were they afraid?"

Quilla thought about it. She had been chosen for the voyage because she thought before she answered. "They were afraid," she said. "But they came out to the edge all the same, every one, even the foals. They stood and looked back at us until we were out of sight."

The queen was quiet for some time.

"Then they are worth looking at," she said. "Go again."

In the sixty-eighth year, two years after the white ship first passed, the Humans founded their third city. It stood in the far south of the Savannah, at the root of the long cape that reaches down toward the Grove, on grassland that had been given over until then to the herds and the great birds. The settlers who went there were the boldest the Dominion had, and they cleared the plain with spear and fire, and they gave their town a name that Theamin thought in poor taste and allowed anyway. They called it Ostrich-Bane.

There were two hundred and twenty-six Humans in the world that year, in three cities. There were forty-eight Unicorns, in two. The Watcher had asked for a century, and the Player kept its word and stayed the hand of both peoples. So the *Swan* looked and did not land, and the Humans stood on their cliffs and looked back, and nothing else passed between them for a long time.

What came next did not come over the sea. It came up out of the quarry of Aegis-Sol, and it wore purple and gold.
