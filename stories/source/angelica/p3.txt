#B BOOK THREE|The Long Peace|Years 100 to 249

#C Chapter Nine: The Year the War Did Not Come

#E "I asked for a century, and at the end of it one side had two soldiers."
The Watcher

The Watcher looked down for a long time before it answered.

It saw Aegis-Sol on its limestone hill with a new wall of rough fieldstone where the palisade had burned, and children on the wall, and two men in the whole realm whose trade was arms. It saw Utica under the shining boughs, and twenty-one great horned soldiers sparring in an avenue of statues until the purple earth was dark. It saw a white ship tied to a human quay with nobody speaking.

"No," it said.

"No?"

"I named the year when I thought a century would make them equals. It has not. Yours would go through mine in a season, and I would learn nothing from it except that twenty-one is more than two." The Watcher considered the white ship. "And there is something else happening that I did not plan. They are looking at one another. I should like to know what comes of that."

"So I go on holding them?"

"No. Let go. Take your hand away entirely, and let us force nothing. If they fight, they fight. If they trade, they trade. If they marry their fishing fleets and raise a temple to the pair of us, I will be very surprised, and I will watch that too."

The Player was silent, in the manner of one who has been handed more freedom than it asked for and is not sure it trusts it.

"You understand," it said, "that mine are bloodthirsty. I made them so. Without my hand on them..."

"Then we will see how much of what you made is nature and how much is choice," said the Watcher. "Yours has a queen who can wait. I have noticed that about her."

So in the hundredth year the hand was lifted, and the war that had been decreed was put off, and the two peoples were left to themselves. Neither of them felt any change.

*

Under the great tree at Utica, Angelica woke one morning with the feeling that somebody had let go of her mane.

She did not know what it meant. She stood for a time testing it, the way one tests a healed leg. The old wish to go north was still in her, as strong as ever. Nothing held it back now. She found that she was not going to act on it, and it was some days before she understood why.

It was the ledger that stopped her. Quilla's count stood at two hundred and seventeen humans to a hundred and fifty unicorns, and humans bred like the clover. She had watched them burned down to a third and grow back past their old height inside fourteen years. Twenty-one of the Unicorn Defense could take Aegis-Sol; she did not doubt it. They could not hold it, and garrison it, and hold the Grove behind them as well, with every transport she owned carrying a dozen at a time across thirty leagues of water that the humans were learning to sail.

She wanted to end a thing and leave nothing of it standing, and with the numbers she had she could only begin it.

"Not by sea," she said aloud, to nobody.

Ysolt the Carver, who was old now and had come to bring her the plans for a new statue, asked what was not to go by sea.

"Anything that matters," said the queen, and would not say more. It was the first seed of the bridge, and it was a hundred and fifty years before she acted on it.

*

In the hundred-and-second year, Roux launched ships of its own, and the Clover Fields became the third corner of the sea-roads. In that same year the Humans, with the hand gone from them as well, did a thing that Theamin would not have dared a decade before.

They went to look.

The boat was called the *Maren*, for the captain who had gone over the wall with an axe, and she was the largest the yards of Aegis-Sol had built: forty feet of tarred acacia with a single square sail and a crew of nine. Her master was a daughter of Dace the watchman, a lean, unhurried woman named Orla, and her orders from the king were four words long. *Look. Count. Come home.*

She was eight days crossing. On the ninth morning her crew saw a light under the cloud ahead that was not the dawn, and by noon they were looking up at trees.

Nothing in the watch-tales of Sol-Vael had prepared them for it. The trunks went up out of the purple shore like the pillars of the sky and vanished into the overcast, and the boughs that spread from them shed a soft pale radiance that fell on the water for a mile out. Fruit hung up there like lamps. And along the shore, among roots thicker than the *Maren* was long, stood a city of violet stone, and an avenue of immense horned figures with their heads lowered toward the sea.

"Statues," said Orla, after a while. "They're statues. Look, that one's not finished."

"That one moved," said her mate.

It had. Between the stone unicorns there were living ones, coming down to the waterfront in ones and twos, and then in dozens, to stand and stare at the little tarred boat sitting off their harbour with its sail aback. Orla held her there. She had been told to count, and she counted: the houses, the ships at the dock, the great white transport she had seen from the cliffs as a child. She counted the soldiers, who were easy to tell apart from the rest by the scars.

She stayed until she saw a longboat being run out. Then she put the helm over and went home.

*

When Quilla brought word of it to the queen, the court was in an uproar. A human vessel had come into the waters of Utica, looked, and left unchallenged. The young soldiers of the Defense wanted the *Swan* sent after it with her gun run out.

Angelica astonished them. She laughed.

It was a rare sound. She stood under the tree with her head thrown back and laughed until the fruit shivered in the boughs.

"They came to look at me," she said. "A hundred years, and at last someone has come to look." She turned to Ysolt. "The new statue. Face it north."

That was the true beginning of the peace. No treaty was signed, for the two peoples had not yet exchanged a single word. But each had now sailed into the other's harbour and stared and gone home again, and neither had been shot at. Trust has been built on less.

#C Chapter Ten: Six Harbours

#E "We sold them stone and they sold us light. I have never been certain which of us had the better of it."
Queen Thesia

The first word spoken between a Human and a Unicorn was *fish*.

It happened at Roux in the hundred-and-ninth year. A boat out of Ostrich-Bane, blown west by a three-day gale, came limping into the clover harbour with her mast sprung and her water gone. Her crew expected to die. Instead a scarred old mare came down the quay and looked at them for a time. This was Hesper, who had once climbed a tree to watch a wizard killed by rabbits. Then she lifted a basket of dried bream off a rack with her horn-light and set it on their deck.

"Fish," she said, slowly and distinctly, in her own tongue.

The master of the boat, who was no fool, picked one up, held it over his head, and said the human word. Hesper repeated it. They went on from there. By the time the mast was fished and the casks were full the two crews had forty words in common, most of them to do with boats, and when the humans sailed they left behind a bolt of woven cloth, having nothing else to give. Hesper had it hung in the garrison hall. No unicorn had ever seen weaving. They spent a month trying to work out how it was done.

After that it could not be stopped. There was no hand above to stop it and no will below.

*

The trade that grew between the two peoples over the next forty years was the making of both of them.

The Humans had things the Unicorns had never thought of: cloth, rope laid in three strands, fired clay, bronze fittings, the wheel on an axle, and above all grain, which could be stored for years and which the great horned folk, raised on a single fruit, came to like a great deal. The Unicorns had things the Humans could not have made in a thousand years: timber from fallen boughs that gave its own light and never rotted, violet iron harder than any bronze, the black powder and the guns it fed, and the pale fruit itself, which humans found bitter and strengthening and paid for in gold.

Sol-Vael, rebuilt at last in white stone on its cliff, put out a dock in the hundred-and-thirtieth year and became the richest port of the three, because it was the nearest to Utica. That made six harbours on the Great Ocean: Aegis-Sol, Sol-Vael and Ostrich-Bane on the Savannah, Utica and Trecua in the Grove, and Roux in the Clover Fields. By the hundred-and-fiftieth year ships went between every one of them and every other. There were human factors living in a walled yard at Utica, craning their necks. There were unicorn merchants stabled, with great courtesy and reinforced floors, in a hall built for them on the quay at Aegis-Sol.

Theamin went aboard the *Swan* himself in the hundred-and-twelfth year, when she next tied up at his dock, and stood on her deck among creatures twice his height. He never said what passed, except that her captain had bowed first.

He and Angelica never met. They exchanged gifts each year for half a century. He sent her a bronze breastplate of his father's pattern, sized for a unicorn, which was hung on her statue. She sent him a bough of shining wood, the length of a ship's mast, which he set up in the hall of Aegis-Sol so that it was never dark there. They wrote, through interpreters. Her letters were short. One of them, which Thesia copied into her book, said only: *You count well. So do I.*

*

Both rulers went on counting through all those years.

Anyone who looks at the tally of the hundred-and-fiftieth year can see it. There were three hundred and ninety-nine Humans in the Dominion then, the most there had ever been, and of those a hundred and eighty-four were soldiers by trade. The realm that had kept two men under arms at the century's turn now kept nearly half its people so. Theamin had seen what stood at the rail of the *Swan*. He liked the unicorns. He trusted their queen about as far as he could have thrown her statue. Every bolt of cloth he sold her went to pay an archer.

In the Unister Mysin there were three hundred and seventy-four Unicorns, of whom a hundred and thirty belonged to the Defense.

Each side said that the soldiers were for the protection of the sea-lanes. Each knew what the other's were for. The peace of those years was real. People lived their whole lives in it and were happy. But some part of it rested on two rulers who had each added up the other's strength and found that a war could not be won quickly.

Angelica read the tally under the great tree and was silent for a long time.

"A hundred and eighty-four," she said. "They have more soldiers than I have."

"Each of ours is worth four of theirs, my queen," said the captain of the Defense.

"On land. With her feet under her." The queen looked north, to where the new statue faced. "How many of ours is a unicorn worth when she is standing on a deck twelve abreast, and the deck is burning, and it is thirty leagues to shore?"

Nobody answered.

"Just so," said Angelica. "Go on trading."

#C Chapter Eleven: The Old Crowns

#E "The Long Sun was a gift. Nobody warned us what it would do to a throne."
*The Second Book of Crowns*

King Theamin died in his sleep in the hundred-and-sixtieth year. He was a hundred and forty-seven years old.

He had taken a kingdom of thirty-eight frightened people from a father killed by a bird. He had seen it burned to a third and brought it back to four hundred, with three cities, three harbours and peace on every horizon. He had never started a war and never lost one. On the last evening of his life he walked the wall of Aegis-Sol as he had done since he was twelve, and counted the acacias visible from the north gate, and went to bed, and did not get up.

The whole Dominion mourned him, and so, in their fashion, did the unicorns. The *Swan*, the old *Swan* that had lasted ninety-four years on a hull that could not rot, came into the river-mouth with her sails struck and lay at the royal quay for a day and a night. When she sailed she left a carved likeness of the king in shining wood, one-quarter the size of life. Among her people, a likeness was the only honour that counted.

His children were alive. He had three daughters, Theara, Gotrin and Tresila. But the council did not at once look to them. For a hundred and thirty-five years one voice had stood at the king's right hand, and the realm was not yet ready to do without it.

So the crown went to Thesia.

She was the first child of Aegis-Sol, born beside the first fire in the year the world began. She had minded Theamin on the wall, taught him the reckoning of grain, and carried a slate through the month of Vexius and the years of Burcien. She was a hundred and sixty years old, exactly as old as the world. She accepted the crown without ceremony. She said that she would hold it until the realm had finished grieving, and that was what she did.

She reigned four years. In that time she finished the *First Book of Crowns*, from which so much of this history is drawn, and in its last pages she set down her list of gold: the hem of Vexius, the hood of Burcien. Beneath them she wrote a line that later readers found chilling. *There will be a third. They come in a pattern, and I have not yet seen what completes it.*

She died in the hundred-and-sixty-fourth year, at a hundred and sixty-four. No Human ever lived longer. The world was then exactly as old as she was, and it has been said that in her the Long Sun reached its full noon.

*

After Thesia the Long Sun became a trouble to the throne.

The gift had a consequence nobody had reckoned. A people who lived a century and a half had heirs who were already old. Theara, the eldest daughter of Theamin, came to the throne at a hundred and twenty, an honest, clear-headed woman who was loved for saying plainly what she thought. She should have had thirty good years in her.

She had two years, and the first of them was the worst the Dominion had known since the fire.

In the hundred-and-sixty-fifth year, an idea came into Sol-Vael.

Nobody knew who had it first. It moved through the taverns of the harbour like a draught under a door. The whole history of their people was strange when one came to add it up. A king had been killed by a bird. A sorcerer had come out of the ground and a second out of the sea, each when the realm was at its fullest. A fire had stopped at exactly five survivors, and later at three. The horned people could have ended them a dozen times and had only stood and watched. And the thought that came into Sol-Vael that year was that none of this was chance, and that it was not fate or the will of any honest god either. Somebody was *playing*. The world was a board. Its people were pieces, moved for the entertainment of beings they would never see, and their griefs were a kind of score.

They could not prove it. That was the cruelty of the thing. They could never know. But in a town built on a cliff that had burned twice, among people whose grandparents had lived in sea-caves for three years, it found dry ground and it caught.

There was a week in the autumn of that year when Sol-Vael lost its mind. People stopped working. They stood in the streets shouting at the sky. Some laughed and could not stop. And some, who had decided that a piece could at least refuse to be moved, went up to the cliffs where Valerius's cairn stood.

Twenty-one people of Sol-Vael took their own lives before the soldiers came down from the capital and put the town under guard.

The chronicles do not dwell on what the soldiers found, and neither will this one. It is enough to say that the men who carried the dead up from the foot of the cliffs were changed by it, and that Queen Theara, who rode to Sol-Vael herself against the advice of her council, stood at the edge for a long time and then gave an order that the thing was never to be spoken of as shameful. They were to be buried with the others. They were to be counted.

"Is it true?" one of her captains asked her on the road home. He was a young man, and shaking. "What they said. That we are someone's game."

Theara was the honest queen. She did not tell him no.

"I don't know," she said. "Nobody can. But I will tell you what I told the ones who lived. If there is someone watching, then let them see us bury our dead and plant the barley. And if there is no one, then the barley still wants planting."

*

Above the mist, neither of the two powers spoke for a long while.

"They saw us," said the Player at last. It sounded shaken, if such a thing can be. "Only for a moment, out of the corner of the eye. How?"

"The mages, I think," said the Watcher slowly. "Whatever sends them does not come from our board. Each one that crosses leaves the door a little ajar. And yours brush up against the edge of you in that Grove every windless night and think nothing of it. Mine have never been touched. To them it was unbearable."

"Twenty-one."

"Yes."

"I am not sure," said the Player, "that I like this game as well as I did."

The Watcher made no reply. But it is recorded that for many years afterwards neither of them lowered a hand toward the world at all.

*

Theara died the following year, at a hundred and twenty-two. The grief of Sol-Vael had gone into her and not come out. Her sister Gotrin, who was the same age to within a season, took the throne in the hundred-and-sixty-sixth year.

The Player, watching the old queen crowned, was heard to say that it honoured the blood of Valerius but that the Dominion needed younger leaders, and that a throne passed from one deathbed to the next was no throne at all. It was right. But nothing could be done under the rules they had agreed, and the Long Sun went on handing the crown to the very old.

In that same year, the hundred-and-sixty-sixth, the third of the hooded ones came. Thesia had been in her grave two years. She would have known him at once.

He appeared at noon on the edge of Utica, where the avenue of statues met the purple earth: a figure on two legs, robed, with a hood of gold and a tall staff in his hand. He drew breath. He began to raise the staff.

He was dead before it reached the level of his shoulder.

Five of the Unicorn Defense had been sparring thirty paces away. They did not stop to ask what he was. A hundred and thirty soldiers lived within a horn-call of that spot, in a city where the love of blood was a civic virtue, and the first of them to reach him went through him at a gallop. The staff fell unused. It was all over in the time it takes to say so, and the soldiers stood about afterwards, rather disappointed, wondering what the creature had wanted.

Quilla's successors compared the gold hood, later, with the humans' tales of their red sorcerer, and drew the correct conclusion, and told the queen.

"One of those destroyed two of their cities," said Angelica.

"Yes, my queen."

"And ours did not get his stick up." She was silent for a moment. "I have been careful of them for a hundred years."

Nobody in the court knew what to say to that, and she did not explain it. But those who knew her best thought afterwards that something shifted in her that day. She had always known her people were stronger than the Humans. Until then she had not known by how much.

#C Chapter Twelve: Two New Cities

#E "A town of twenty, with a dock, on a coast with nothing on it. We wrote it down as a fishing station."
A factor of Sol-Vael

Queen Gotrin reigned seventeen years, quietly and well, and died in the hundred-and-eighty-third year at a hundred and thirty-nine. Her son Onbet followed her. He was ninety-three at his crowning, a broad, cheerful man who had spent most of a century as a prince and saw no reason to change his habits. He kept the trade running and the soldiers paid, and he looked west.

The Clover Fields had been in the Dominion's eye for a long time. Human ships had called at Roux for ninety years. Human sailors had walked in the clover and felt the luck move under their feet, and come home with stories of dice that fell sixes all night and of a man who tripped on a tussock and broke his neck. The best harbour on that whole continent lay on its north-eastern coast, straight across the narrowest water from Aegis-Sol itself, and nobody had built there. The unicorns, oddly, had never spread beyond their one red headland.

In the hundred-and-ninety-eighth year, Onbet sent settlers across. They broke ground on that north-eastern shore a few months into the year and raised a sea-wall, a watchtower and a dock, and the Watcher itself is said to have leaned down and given the place its name: Sol-Clova, the Sun in the Clover. It was the first human town outside the Savannah. On a clear day its people could see the smoke of their own capital across the strait.

They watched Roux for some sign of offence. None came. The garrison of Roux sent a basket of dried bream, which by then was a joke a century old, and went back to whatever it was they did on their headland.

*

In that same year the unicorns founded a city too, and the Humans took no particular notice of it.

It was called Erant, and it stood at the far western end of the Celestial Grove, on a point of land that had lain empty since the beginning of the world. It was a curious site. It faced no good fishing. It had no bay. All it had was this: a ship standing off it on a clear day could see, low on the northern horizon, the tip of the long southern cape of the Savannah, at whose root lay Ostrich-Bane. The sea was narrower there than anywhere else between the two continents, hardly a third of the width that lay between Utica and Sol-Vael.

A factor out of Sol-Vael, passing in a grain ship, noted the new settlement in his log. *A town of some twenty unicorns, with a dock. Fishing station.* He was right about the number. Fifty years later it was still twenty.

Angelica had waited ninety-eight years to found Erant, and she waited fifty-two more before she used it.

#C Chapter Thirteen: The Year of Three Crowns

#E "We buried a king in the second month and a queen in the eighth, and by the twelfth we were crowning a woman of a hundred and thirty-four and calling it hope."
*The Second Book of Crowns*

The two-hundred-and-eighteenth year did the royal house lasting harm.

King Onbet died in the second month. He was a hundred and twenty-eight, and he went suddenly, at table, in the middle of a story. His sister Treiala was crowned within the week, a thin, sharp woman who had waited a long time and had plans. She named half a new council. She ordered a survey of the walls of Sol-Clova. She meant to cross the strait and see the place herself, the first monarch of the Dominion to set foot on another continent.

She died in the eighth month, of a fever, with her bags packed.

For four months after that the Sun-Gilded Dominion had no monarch at all. The council sat in the hall under the shining bough and argued over the tree of descent, which had grown tangled. Gotrin's line was not extinct: Treiala left a daughter, Bruhis, who was fifty-two. But there remained alive one child of Theamin himself, the youngest of the three sisters, born when the old king was past seventy. In a realm that had been founded on the word of one man in bronze, the nearer blood carried the day.

So in the twelfth month of that year they crowned Tresila, the last daughter of Theamin, who was a hundred and thirty-four.

That made three crowns in twelve months, and in the harbour taverns of Sol-Vael people stopped making jokes about it. The Long Sun had given the people years in plenty, and the throne got very few of them. Every monarch since Theamin had come to the crown with most of a life already spent. They were wise and they were tired, and none could plan beyond a decade. Across the water the same queen had reigned since before the first of them was born.

*

Angelica heard the news of each death as it came. The humans always sent word, and the *Swan* always went, and another small likeness in shining wood was left on the royal quay.

There was a hall in Utica where the Unister Mysin kept their own copies of those likenesses. Valerius was there in his bronze, carved from the descriptions of traders. Theamin was there, and Thesia, Theara, Gotrin and Onbet. In that year two more were set beside them, and the Carver asked whether a larger hall should be begun.

The queen walked the row slowly. She was two hundred and thirty-five years old and had not changed since the day she was crowned.

"Eight," she said. "Eight of them, and I am still on my first statue." She stopped before the likeness of Theamin. "This one I should not have liked to fight. He would have counted every stone I moved." She walked on to the end of the row, where the wood of Tresila's figure was still pale from the chisel. "These I do not even know. They are gone before I learn their faces. I am trading with a river, Carver. It has a different water in it every time I look."

"They are still the same people, my queen."

"Are they?" said Angelica. "A people is what it remembers. Mine remember everything, because I am here to remind them. What do theirs remember? Ask one of them who Maren was, or what colour the first sorcerer wore." She turned away. "They are forgetting how to be afraid. No, leave the hall as it is. We will not need a larger one."

#C Chapter Fourteen: The Grieving King

#E "He was the first of our kings who had seen another country, and the only one who loved it."
*The Second Book of Crowns*

Tresila confounded everybody by living.

She reigned twenty-eight years, longer than anyone since her father, and they were good years. The trade was at its height. Sol-Clova grew from a watchtower and a wall into a proper town. And she did a wise thing that none of her predecessors had thought of. She sent her heir away.

Her son was named Thone. He had been born in the year his aunt Gotrin was crowned, and by the time his mother came to the throne he was a man of fifty-two with nothing to do but wait, as Onbet had waited. Tresila would not have it. She made him governor of Sol-Clova and sent him over the strait, and there he stayed for twenty-eight years.

He loved it. Thone was a quiet man of a serious temper, more like Theamin than any of the line between, and the Clover Fields suited him. He liked the green of it after the gold of home. He liked the uncertainty, the way a day there could turn on nothing. He walked the country farther than any Human had, and he came to know the garrison of Roux by name. He sat many an evening on their red headland talking, in the trade-tongue, with a soldier named Hesper the Younger about fish and weather and the great steel-furred rabbits, which the unicorns had learned to leave alone.

He noticed, on those visits, that Roux was a strange place. It covered more ground than any town but Trecua. Yet it had only thirty-odd buildings and fewer than a hundred souls, and the whole eastern side of the headland was fenced off behind a timber wall, with a great deal of dust rising from behind it. He asked once what was done there.

"Stone," said Hesper the Younger. "We cut stone. The queen is fond of statues."

It was true, and Thone thought no more about it.

*

His mother died in the two-hundred-and-forty-sixth year, at a hundred and sixty-two.

The news took nine days to reach him. He was out in the clover, half a day inland from Sol-Clova, looking over a stretch of well-watered ground where he thought a farming village might go, when a rider came up from the harbour with a sealed tablet. Those who were with him said he read it twice and then sat down on the ground.

He was eighty years old and he had not seen her in eleven years. He had meant to go home that winter.

It was most of a year before he was crowned. There was the government of Sol-Clova to hand over, and a bad season of storms in the strait, and, as his council whispered afterwards, a king who was in no hurry to walk into the hall where his mother had sat. He reached Aegis-Sol in the two-hundred-and-forty-seventh year. He was crowned under the shining bough, in the bronze of Valerius, the ninth monarch of the Sun-Gilded Dominion, and every councillor present remarked the same thing. The king did not weep. He looked like a man who had been carrying a heavy load for a long time and expected to go on carrying it.

But he was king, and he did what he had been about to do when the rider found him. In the two-hundred-and-forty-eighth year he sent settlers to that stretch of well-watered ground half a day inland from Sol-Clova, and a village went up there among the clover. It was a place of barns and paddocks and long fields, with no wall and no soldiers. The Watcher gave it its name, and the name was an old joke turned gentle: Ostrich-Meadows.

It was the ninth city of the world's great age and the last one the Humans ever built.

There were nine reigns of peace behind them then, two hundred and forty-eight years without a war. A child born in Ostrich-Meadows that spring could look forward to a hundred and fifty years under the Long Sun, in the luckiest country in the world.

Two years later Angelica called her council to the root-vault under the great tree and had the doors closed, and gave the first orders for the work that ended that peace.
