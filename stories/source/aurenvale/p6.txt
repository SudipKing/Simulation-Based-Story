#B BOOK SIX|The Falling Star|Years 189 to 200

#C Chapter Twenty-Two: Isan

#E "A general is a door the realm walks through. Do not mourn the door. Mourn what it cost to keep it open."
(Said at the burial of Annmund, Eddale, Year 189)

They buried Annmund on the south slope below the temple, where the lemon trees come up to the edge of the hill, and the whole of Eddale stood in the rain to do it.

It was the largest funeral the town had ever held, and it was held in a town that had learned to bury well. Fifteen victories, the chroniclers wrote, and the herald read out the fifteen by name, the old beasts first: Shrikka, struck down in the hundred and twenty-second year by the first Annmund, whose name he carried and whose first blow the hall counted for him by old custom. Old Bramble. Mossjaw. Then the skirmishes, the burned hamlets, the raids on Nimnook that ended in a year and gained nothing. The herald read them in the flat voice of a man announcing a harvest, because that was how Annmund had liked to hear things said. When the list was done there was a silence in which you could hear the rain on forty thousand leaves, and then the soldiers, twenty-six of them standing, lowered their spears, and the line of them went down the hill like a wave going out.

Above all that, in the place that is not a place, the Watcher stood with its hands behind it and did not lower anything.

*Wars are won by soldiers, not by gods.* It had sworn that. It had sworn it in the empty hall with six frightened children for witnesses, and it had held to it, and it had lost a general a decade for the keeping.

"He was good," said a voice behind it.

The Maker did not come often. When it did, it came as it always had, without a door, wearing no particular shape, a little amused at everything, including itself. It looked down at the rain.

"He was the best you had," it said. "Fifteen. That's more than anyone in any realm this century."

"I know the count."

"I know you do." The Maker watched the line of soldiers go down the hill. "I wanted you to know I'd noticed him."

The Watcher said nothing for a time. Then it said, "Who is next?"

"That's your decree to make. Or your general's. I forget which of you the covenant lets choose."

"Neither. The hall chooses." But the Watcher knew that was not quite true, and the Maker, being the Maker, did not call it on it.

*

In the Year 190 a woman named Isan was made general of Aurenvale, and the town was surprised by it, and then, in the particular way of towns, decided it had always known.

She was not tall. This was her whole disadvantage, and she carried it like a rucksack she had stopped noticing. She had come up through the Brookham patrols, where the work was less glory than weather, and she had a habit of walking the whole length of a march-line before dawn and looking each soldier in the face, which the older officers thought was sentiment and the younger ones came to understand was arithmetic. She was counting. She was always counting. Twenty-six soldiers in a realm of a hundred and ten could not afford to lose a single one to a bad boot, a bad night, a bad rumour.

The same year the Watcher gave a Sign.

It was the Seventh. It had thought about it for a long time and had argued with itself, at night, in the dim place where it kept its decisions. It had skipped 180. It had skipped 150. The Covenant allowed one Sign per decade at most, and a Sign unspent was a Sign wasted, and in the end it was not strategy that moved it but something nearer to the way a mother sets an extra place at the table after a death: not because the empty seat is needed, but because she cannot bear to look at the table with the seat gone.

The Host arrived at dawn in the barley fields behind Eddale, grown soldiers appearing between one breath and the next, blinking at a sun they had never seen before. They were hale and grim, and they had the expression the Hosts always had, of people who have been told something very large in a dream and cannot remember what. Isan walked out to meet them alone. She had a flask of water and a clipboard.

"Names," she said, and began to write.

That was the whole of the ceremony. It was, the Watcher thought, watching her, exactly right.

That year the Watcher also gave the hall a decree of the gentlest kind, the patronage of the arts, and the hall raised a banner. It hung beneath the lintel of the great hall, where it still hung when the Stone came. It was silver and blue, and across it ran a bridge, the old bridge that had been the first mural in Eddale in the first year of the world. They called it *The Eternal Banner.* The Watcher let them keep the name.

*

Far to the east, in the pale-green lowlands where the elves of Luriel kept their glass-and-silver towns, a different thing ended that year.

The Realm of Ithriel had risen in the Year 188, the way new realms rise: with a prophecy, a small clear flag, and a winter moon painted over a door. Its elves had left the Circle of Zephrael to worship Yssa, the Winter Moon, and they had a hero named Ilinor who was young, and fast, and said to have wept the first time she killed. For three years Ithriel stood, small and bright and furious, a candle in the wind of its parent's disapproval.

It fell into ruin in the Year 191. The record says only that. It does not say what the last night looked like, because nobody with a pen was there; but in a Luriel orchard that autumn a gardener found a child's moon-token in the grass, the little silver kind that the Ithrielites had worn on their sleeves, and put it in her pocket, and told no one.

*

In the Year 192 a man called Ally put a spear into the throat of Gloomleg, the great grey spider out of the old wood, on the third day of a hunt that should have taken one. He did it from a ledge, he said afterwards, because he could not get any closer, and because the beast was too tired to climb. He lost two fingers. He was asked to repeat the story in the hall and he told it three times, and at the third telling it was shorter, and when the herald asked him why he left out the ledge he said, "Because I was afraid, sir, and I'd rather not hear it said that I wasn't."

In the Year 193 the herald read a decree that no one in Eddale was surprised by.

*War on Nimnook.*

It was the eleventh time, or the twelfth; even the clerks had begun to lose the count. Nimnook was a town of gnomes, fifty and then sixty and then sixty-six souls under a mountain, with a stone fortress, a bitter cult of Gorim and a long, quiet gift for holding a grudge. Aurenvale had burned their fields again and again and the gnomes had never once broken. They rebuilt. They counted their dead and wrote the names on slate. They were, as the soldiers said with something very like respect, the only enemy that got more patient the more you hit it.

But in the Year 193 something was different. The elves of Luriel, who had sworn alliance with Aurenvale in the Year 184 and had kept it in the way of elves, which was to say gracefully and a little late, sent a company east to join the war.

Isan met their captain at a ford on the road to Nimnook. He was tall, silver-haired, with a face like a carved door.

"We are told," he said in careful Eddish, "that this war is not to be won."

"It is not," said Isan.

"We are told that you know this."

"The Watcher knows it. I am told to march." She looked at him, and then, because there was no one else on that bank to hear it, she said, "I march anyway. If I did not, the next general would, and she would not be careful."

The elf considered her for a long time. Then, formally, he lowered his head, the first time in the long history of those two peoples that a Luriel captain had bowed to an Aurenvale general. "Then we shall be careful together," he said.

They burned one hamlet. The gnomes had already left; they always left. Isan's soldiers set the fire with the grim neatness of men and women doing a job they had stopped believing in, and the elves stood at the edge of the smoke, watching the sparks rise, and one of them, a young scout, began very quietly to sing in Elvish, a thing about wind and ash, and none of the humans understood the words and all of them were suddenly very tired.

The war ended in the Year 194. Nothing had been gained. Nimnook counted its dead, which were few, and wrote the names on slate.

*

The same year Toman killed the Pale Eye.

The beast had come out of the south bog in the spring, as it had come before and as it would, the legends said, come again: a basilisk with a cold white stare, whose gaze turned grass to ash and men to stone. Toman was not a soldier of any renown. He was a ditch-digger, thirty-eight, with bad knees, and he went out because there was no one else on the east road and because his sister's child had been three streets from where the thing had passed.

He fought it with a mirror. Not a clever mirror; a polished tray from his mother's kitchen, bent in the hands. The Pale Eye saw its own face and, for an instant, was afraid, and that was all the instant he needed. The song they made about it is bad. It rhymes "eye" with "die" nine times, and on the last day of the world some of the children of Brookham were still singing it, a little out of tune.

In the Year 195 Wobwick of Nimnook, the best of the gnomes, fell. The record says only that. Aurenvale's soldiers, hearing it, did not cheer; they took off their caps, and no one had ordered them to.

In the Year 196 the Watcher declared its last war on Nimnook.

Isan did not wait for the herald to finish. She stood in the hall with her arms folded and heard the decree through, and then she said to the room, not loudly, "We are going to burn something, and we are going to come home, and at the end of it there will still be a Nimnook." No one contradicted her. It was the truest sentence anyone said in that hall for a year.

They did exactly that. It took a season. The gnomes counted their dead. The humans counted theirs. The war ended, as all the wars had ended, in the way of weather.

The Watcher watched it end, and for the first time in its long watch it did not look away. It looked at the whole map, nineteen realms, one continent, every shore walkable, and it did a sum it had been putting off for forty years. It was not a hard sum. It was only a terrible one.

*There is no war I can win,* it thought. *There is no sequence of wars. The world is too many.*

It turned to look for the Maker, and found it already there.

"You can stop me," said the Watcher. "Any time. I'd like it noted that I know that."

"I know you know."

"Why haven't you?"

"Because that isn't the game," said the Maker. It said it kindly. "I'm not in the game. I'm only watching it, like you."

#C Chapter Twenty-Three: The Last Decree

#E "There is a kind of friend you do not recognise until everyone else has gone."
(Elvish proverb, quoted in the Luriel annals)

The winter of 197 was the mildest anyone in Eddale could remember, and the town took it as an omen and could not decide which kind.

And all of it, the mild winter and the quiet halls and the ordinary bread, lived under the star.

Nobody believed it would fall. Everybody believed it would fall. This is the usual condition of a people before the end of the world.

*

In Eddale a girl called Edry, named for the old herald of the Orphan Years, who was nineteen and had the clearest voice in the choir, was made herald in the Year 199.

It was an accident, in the way such things are accidents. The old herald, Annric the Younger, a stooped, ink-fingered man who had read the Watcher's decrees since the Year 151, took a fall on the temple steps and broke his hip, and the hall, after an hour of embarrassment, sent for Edry, who was the only person in Eddale who could read aloud without her voice going flat. She read the decree of the Year 199 in a clear, high voice that cracked on a single word, and nobody ever mentioned it.

But that was two years off. In the Year 197, the Watcher did not decree. Decrees came every three years, and the next was due in the Year 199, and so the Year 197 and the Year 198 were quiet, the quiet of a house in which something has been decided and not yet said.

Isan used them. She walked the walls at dawn. She drilled her twenty-seven soldiers on the east terrace, in sunlight so bright it made the red star disappear, and she stood at the end of each drill-line afterwards and thanked them, one by one, by name. She had begun to do this in the Year 196 and nobody had asked why. A sergeant, a hard woman called Maera whom Isan had known since the patrols, asked her once, in the barracks, late, over cold tea.

"You're counting again," said Maera.

"Always."

"You think there's something to count toward?"

Isan looked into her cup. "I think," she said slowly, "that someone ought to know their names. In case there's a day when somebody asks."

The sergeant did not answer. After a while she put her hand over Isan's, a thing she had not done in thirty years of friendship, and they sat like that until the lamp burned low.

*

In the Year 198 the orcs of Durgoth, who had been Bolthul's greatest city and Bolthul's greatest rebels, turned their faces from Ghorak the Skullfather, and were converted back to Krukka, the Chaos-Drum, by priests who came, it is said, with no army, only a drum.

The Watcher went to its high place and looked, and thought about what it was permitted.

It thought now: *I could break it.*

*If I break it now, what was it for?* it asked itself. And then, because it was fair: *And if I keep it, what is it for?*

It had no answer. It had, for the first time in all those years, only the practice of the oath, bare of justification: a habit become a spine.

It kept the Covenant.

*

The decree of the Year 199 was read in the hall at Eddale by a nineteen-year-old with a cracked voice.

The hall was full. It was always full, now. There were three hundred chairs and four hundred standing, because the whole realm had come, a hundred and ten souls, soldiers and clerks and children on shoulders, as if to be present for a thing was to hold it. Isan stood at the front with her cloak altered, and beside her stood the old herald, leaning on a cane, who had insisted on coming. The Eternal Banner hung over the door. Above all of them, on the lintel, someone had carved, in the first year of the world, the words that were still there: *We were six. Write that down, and then write that we were enough.*

Edry unrolled the scroll. Her hands shook; she steadied them against the lectern.

"By the Watcher's word," she read, "in the Year One Hundred and Ninety-Nine: *Aurenvale makes alliance with the Realm of Luriel.*"

There was silence.

It was not the silence of surprise. They had a hundred and ninety-nine years of decrees behind them, and they knew the music of them, and in this one there was something they had not heard in any: the sound of a door being closed gently from the inside.

Alliance with Luriel. They had been allied before, in the Year 49 and in 157 and again in 184; they had been at war with the elves, too, in the Year 12 and in the Year 28, when the elves had been the long silver enemy at the edge of the map. A people the Watcher had once fought. The elves had tended their orchards across two centuries of other people's wars, and they had never wanted anything but to be allowed to remain.

The decree said nothing else. It was a short scroll. The Watcher, in its high place, had spent three months writing it, and in the end, there was nothing to add.

*

The envoy from Luriel came to Eddale in the third week of the Year 199, travelling with six guards and no banner, and she was the oldest elf anyone in the hall had ever seen.

Her name is not in the record. She stood in the hall beneath the lintel and she said, in a thin, exact Eddish, that Luriel accepted the alliance, that Luriel had waited a long time to be asked, and that Luriel would be glad, now, to be of use.

"Of use how?" asked Isan. She had learned to be blunt with elves. They respected it.

"We do not know," said the envoy. "That is why we are glad." She looked up, and the red star was visible through the hall's high window, even in daylight now, a smear of rust in the blue. "My grandmother believed that the Wanderer had come to tell us something. She said it was a patient thing, and that when the day came we would understand it. I am old. I have waited for it for two hundred winters, or so it feels. I would rather wait among friends."

The old herald, with his cane, made a small sound. Isan turned.

"Sir?"

"Nothing." He was wiping his eyes with a sleeve. "I was thinking that I have read seventeen decrees in this hall. I thought every one of them was a battle. I thought we were making war, and we were not, were we? We were only finding who we could stand beside."

"It took us a long time," said the envoy of Luriel, gently.

"Two hundred years," said the herald.

"Yes. Well." The envoy smiled. "Most things do."

*

That same year, the goblins of Nobstink split.

It was the nature of goblins to split; they bred, they quarrelled, they were forever founding another warren out of spite. A goblin named Wibrik, a small, wiry rebel with a broken tusk and a talent for argument, gathered four of his kin and walked out of Nobstink in the dead of night, and in the morning there was a new warren, the Warren of Snikstink, and it had a flag made of a torn blanket, and Wibrik was very proud of the flag.

Nobstink was furious. It started, almost at once, to gather an army.

*

On the last night of the Year 199, Isan could not sleep.

She went up to the east terrace, in her altered cloak, and found it already occupied. Sergeant Maera was sitting on the parapet with her boots hanging over the edge and a flask of something clear and sharp.

"Couldn't sleep either," said Maera.

"No."

They sat for a time without speaking. Below them, Eddale glittered: the towers, the glass, the hover-lanes dotted with lanterns, the great hall with its banner, the temple with its steeple, and, beyond, the dark flowering hills where the lemon trees were flowering out of season.

"Do you think it's coming?" said Maera at last.

Isan looked up. The Wanderer was enormous now: not a thumbnail but a coin, the colour of a banked forge. She had been looking at it for ten years. It had grown with her. She had begun to think of it, privately, as a creature, patient and attentive and terribly polite, a guest who had been invited to a great house and was waiting, outside the door, with its hat in its hands.

"Yes," she said. "I think it's coming."

"Then what do we do?"

Isan thought of Annmund. She thought of the fifteen victories and the funeral in the rain. She thought of the six frightened people in the empty hall, long ago, whom she had been told about in school, the first of the line, before the First Host, before the Covenant, before there was anything to be afraid for.

"What we've always done," she said. "We stand in the line, and we know each other's names."

Maera laughed, a short bark, and drank, and passed the flask. The Watcher, above, was watching them both, and thinking that of all the things it had made, this, perhaps, was the one it would have kept: not the towers, not the laser walls. Two women on a parapet, sharing a flask in the dark.

The old year turned. It was the Year 200.

#C Chapter Twenty-Four: The Two Hundredth Year

#E "At the last, nobody was thinking about the sky. They were thinking about breakfast, and about each other."
(From the Luriel annals; the final entry, in an unknown hand)

It was a clear morning. This is the first thing, and it is the thing that every account agrees on, and it is the thing that hurts most: it was a beautiful day.

The sun rose over the eastern sea, and the sea was flat and bright, and the light went inland across the lowland like a hand opening. There was a little wind. The grain stood in the fields. In the high meadows of Eddale the birches were in new leaf, and in the flowering groves to the south the lemons had come ripe out of season, so that the air smelled, everywhere, like clean linen and sharp sweetness. It was the kind of day that makes people say, to no one, *well, that's all right then.*

And above the world, where for two hundred years there had been a rust-red speck that was only a rumour, there hung a huge, silent, slowly rolling shape, so near now that it had a face: pitted, glowing along one edge. It had stopped being a star some years ago. It was a mountain. It was the largest thing anyone had ever seen, and it was nearly here, and it did not hurry.

*

In Eddale the day began with a drill.

Isan woke at the usual hour and dressed in the usual order, and found, tucked into the sleeve of her cloak, a slip of paper in Maera's blocky hand: *Went to see my mother. Back by noon. Don't start without me.* She read it twice. She laughed, quietly, and put it in her pocket.

On the east terrace the soldiers were already forming up. She walked the line. She looked each of them in the face. She knew every name. She said them aloud, one by one, which she had never done on a drill morning, and a young recruit, who was perhaps twenty and had a spot of oil on his cheek, flushed to the roots of his hair when she reached him.

"You've a smudge," she said.

"Ma'am."

She took out a handkerchief and wiped it away. It was an absurd, tender, very un-general thing to do, and she did it with perfect composure, and the line, which would have laughed at anyone else, did not laugh.

"Dismissed," she said. "Eat something. All of you. It's an order."

They went. At the end of the terrace, Isan stood alone, and looked up at the Falling Star, which now filled a quarter of the eastern sky, and said, in the plain voice of a person addressing a colleague, "I would have liked to know what you were for."

The sky did not answer.

*

In the orchards of Luriel the elves rose early and did what elves do, which was nothing hurried.

A young scout, who had stood at the burning of a gnome hamlet seven years earlier and sung a song about wind and ash, sat on the steps with her knees up and listened to the harp. She was among the hundred and twenty-one souls of Luriel: the largest realm in the world, and the one that had not, in all those centuries, wanted to be.

"Do you remember the song," said the old woman beside her.

"Which song?"

"The one you sang at the fire. About ash."

"I was frightened."

"Of course. That's why it was a good song." The old woman patted her hand. "Sing it again."

So she sang it. The harp found the tune and held it. It was a short, sad, simple thing about the wind that carries all things, ash and seed and leaf, to the same far place, and in the courtyard the old elves closed their eyes. High above the roofs, the Wanderer was nearly full in the sky.

*

South, in the clover-meadows by the sea, where the grass was green to the horizon and the great peaks stood behind, the orcs of Bolthul gathered around their drums.

There were thirty of them, once the greatest horde in the world, down to a village now, with a scar on the earth where Durgoth had torn away. They were big, scarred, quiet people, and they sat in a ring around the ancestor-skulls in the old way, with their hands on their knees. They were at war, as it happened, with a few goblins at the border: Wibrik's Snikstink, five of them and a blanket. It had been a ridiculous war from the first. Nobody had died.

The chieftain, a heavy old orc with one eye, looked up at the star and grunted.

"Krukka's Drumstone," he said. "It is coming to be struck."

"By whom?" asked a young one.

"By Krukka. By the world." The old orc stood. "Bring the drum."

They brought it. It was a great skin-drum as tall as a child, older than the horde. He lifted the mallet. He struck it once, and the sound went out across the clover like a heartbeat.

Fifty-four orcs of Durgoth, who had returned to Krukka only two years ago, heard it in their own camp across the hills, and, because it was a drum and they were orcs, they answered. The drums of Bolthul and Durgoth, split by a war and a god and a century, beat together. In the dust, the grass bent. In the sky, the mountain rolled closer. In Vornak and Kragdak and Bolgor, the tiny rebel horde of three that had once followed a man called Ugdush, the drums were taken up, and the drumming spread, hill to hill, a great slow pounding laid over the whole south of the world, so that for a space of minutes the orcs of Gaia, who had not been of one mind since the founding of the Horde, were of one pulse.

*

In the gnome-halls under the lone mountain of the north, the Burrow of Dobwhistle was at its midday bread.

There were seventy-eight of them: the best-fed gnomes in the world, with fat cheeks and clever hands, and their long tables were laden with mushroom-loaf and honey and cheese. Now a young gnome, one of the clever ones, who had spent all week on a calculation, stood up in the middle of the meal and said, in a trembling voice, "If it lands where I think it lands, it won't matter how deep we dig."

The old ones looked at him with great kindness. "We know, child," said the eldest. "Sit down. Have some honey." He sat. He had some honey. Far under the same mountain, the sixty-six gnomes of Nimnook, who had been burned so many times by their human neighbours, stood in the fortress they had never surrendered and watched the star through a slit of rock. They had a custom: when something frightening came, they wrote the names of the living on slate, as they wrote the names of the dead. It seemed only fair. All sixty-six were written there that morning, chalked in the grey, in rows, in neat gnomish script. A girl, who had never in her life seen the sun, went out onto the ledge to see it, and stood there a long time, blinking.

"It's warm," she said.

*

Far west, in the savanna peninsula where the goblin-warrens crowded together, the morning was loud.

It was always loud. Fifty-five goblins of Nobmuck were arguing; sixty-one of Nobstink were arguing differently; forty-two of Grubheap were arguing about whose fault it was; and at the edge of the plain, five goblins in a ring around a torn blanket were arguing most loudly of all. The Nobstink war-band, which had been sent to march on Vornak, had not marched. It had stopped on the road, because somebody had spotted the star.

"It's the Big Rock," a goblin said.

"It's very big."

"Bigger than last week."

"Should we still march?"

There was a long pause, in which the war-band considered the question with great seriousness.

"We could," said one, "march anyway."

That was the whole of the debate. They marched, because a thing begun is a thing begun, and because a goblin who stops to think is a goblin who has to feel it. They marched in great ragged bands with spears and banners, shouting, laughing, calling each other names, while behind them, in the blanket-flag warren, Wibrik, the small wiry rebel with the broken tusk, stood on a rock and proclaimed, to his four followers, in a ringing voice:

"Tomorrow, we shall have a constitution!"

"What's a constitution?" said one of them.

"I don't know!" cried Wibrik, with tremendous joy. "But we shall have one!"

And the five of them cheered, and the Big Rock rolled closer, and across the plain the war-bands of Nobstink rolled on toward Vornak, singing a rude song at the very top of their lungs, and none of them looked up, because they could not have borne it if they had.

*

In the northern hills, in the five scattered villages of the Dominion of Zathixal, the saurians did what they had always done at the approach of a verdict: they sat, and they were still.

Forty-eight of them. Their numbers had come back from a nadir of three, in the Year 120, through all the fifteen wars Aurenvale had thrown at them; they had grown to twenty-three, and then to this. They had been allies of Aurenvale in the Year 1. They had been enemies for forty-five years of it. They had buried their dead in the cold pines and they had called the star the Judge.

In the temple at Zathixal, in the dim sandstone nave, an old lizard-priest with grey-green scales sat on the floor under the image of Veritas, Lady of Law. The nave was full. Nobody spoke. The priest said, in the hissing tongue, softly, the words of the old sentence they had always said before a ruling:

*Yss zeth ikix.*

It was their motto and their only prayer.

South of them, in the foothills of the Unmined Stone, the dwarves of Kazgar and the dwarves of Thorgar, rivals for two decades, happened that morning to stand within sight of one another across a valley. An old Kazgar miner with a beard as white as chalk raised a hand, and an old Thorgar smith raised one back. Neither could have said why. In Dwaldun, which had once been a great Hold and was down to six, a handful of old dwarves sat in the vast empty halls of their fathers, not unhappy. It is not nothing to be the last of something.

*

At noon, the sky was wrong.

It is hard to say how, because the people who saw it did not live to say. The sun was there. It was still bright. But a second light had begun to mix with it, red and gold, rolling in from the east and from above, so that every shadow on the ground grew a second shadow, and then a third. The air thickened. The birds fell quiet. The Falling Star was no longer a thing in the sky. It was the sky.

In Eddale, the people had come out into the streets.

They came out slowly, without panic. They stood on the steps of the temple and the doorway of the library and the long terraces of the hall. Mothers lifted their children. The old herald stood under the banner with his cane. Edry, the new herald, stood beside him, holding the scroll against her chest, as if it could be a shield. Isan was on the east terrace, with her twenty-seven, and Maera had come back from her mother's, breathless, and stood in the line, two places down from her general, and gave her a tiny, wicked wink.

There were a hundred and ten of them.

They were the second-largest realm in the world. They had a hall and a temple and a library, a banner called The Eternal, a hundred and ninety-nine years of decrees behind them, and an alliance with the oldest and kindest of their enemies. They had risen from six frightened people in an empty hall, and fallen to three, and risen again, by all the stubborn grace of human stock; and they had stood in the line, again and again and again, for two hundred years, and known each other's names.

Isan said, "Soldiers."

They straightened.

"I am proud of you," she said, in her ordinary voice, as if reading a number from a list. "That is all. That is the whole of the speech."

A child, somewhere in the crowd, began to sing the Pale Eye song, the bad one, the one that rhymed *eye* with *die*, and an old man joined in, and a woman, and soon the whole terrace was singing it, badly, with cracked and tender voices, a verse about a ditch-digger and a mirror. It was an awful song. It was theirs.

Somewhere in the high place, the Watcher stood very still.

*

The nineteen realms of the Biosphere were, at that last moment, as follows:

Aurenvale, a hundred and ten. Luriel, a hundred and twenty-one. Dobwhistle, seventy-eight. Nimnook, sixty-six. Vornak, sixty-two. Nobstink, sixty-one. Nobmuck, fifty-five. Durgoth, fifty-four. Zathixal, forty-eight. Grubheap, forty-two. Kazgar, thirty-five. Bolthul, thirty. Thorgar, twenty-one. Serthas, eighteen. Caelmir, eighteen. Kragdak, fourteen. Dwaldun, six. Snikstink, five. Bolgor, three.

Eight hundred and forty-seven souls.

None of them were the last. None of them, on that clear day, had to be. It is a thing the chroniclers have never been able to bear: that they were all still there, all of them, every one, arguing and drumming and mending fences, at the moment of the end.

*

The light came without a sound.

For a heartbeat there was no noise at all, in any realm. The birds, the drums, the harp, the singing; all of it stopped, as if the world had drawn a breath. Then the sky opened, from edge to edge, and the thing that had been the Falling Star came down through it, brighter than any sun that had ever risen over Gaia, a white so total that it was not a colour but an absence.

In Eddale, a hundred and ten people looked up.

Isan did not close her eyes. She had always looked at things directly, and she looked now, at the white, and she thought, with great clarity, of the cloak that was too big, and of Annmund's funeral in the rain, and of the young recruit with the spot of oil on his cheek, and she reached for Maera's hand and found it. *Names,* she thought. *I knew all their names.*

In Luriel the old woman and the young scout were still holding hands. In the clover-meadows of the south the drum was struck once more, and its sound was the last sound on Gaia, one deep beat, that went out across the grass, and was not answered. Under the grey mountain of the north the gnome girl at the ledge shaded her eyes, and the warmth became a different thing. On the road to Vornak, a goblin laughed out loud at something, and stopped.

It was over very quickly. That is the one mercy the record allows.

The light rolled outward from the point of impact, and the world was struck in a lattice of craters, one beside another, until no land was left that was not pressed with a ring of fire. The seas boiled. The mountains broke. The flowering groves south of Eddale, where the lemons had come ripe out of season, burned without a sound. The great hall, the glass towers, the temple with its steeple, the Eternal Banner with its bridge: all of it was light, and then it was nothing.

In every realm, in every town, in every barn and on every terrace, all the people of the world died.

Nineteen realms. Eight hundred and forty-seven souls.

And then none.

#C Epilogue: What the Watcher Kept

#E "It is not the last who is remembered. It is the one who is still holding the pen."
(Inscription found on no wall, because there were no walls)

There was a silence, and it had no edges.

In the place that is not a place, the Watcher stood where it had stood for two hundred years, looking down at a world that was no longer a world. Gaia lay beneath it like a burned plate. The continent that had been walkable, shore to shore, was a pattern of craters and a skin of steam and ash. Nothing moved. Nothing would. The ocean was still rolling, a long time, over rocks that had been mountains.

The Maker came, as it always came, without a door.

"Well," it said.

The Watcher did not turn. "Well."

"I'm sorry," said the Maker. It was true, and it was said simply, with no ceremony, and for a long moment neither of them said anything else.

"You told me the terms," the Watcher said at last. "I accepted them. I'd like that written down. I did not fail because I did not understand. I failed because I could not do it."

"You could have done it," said the Maker.

"How?"

"I don't know. I never do." It sat down, if it sat, on nothing. "That's the thing about a game with real stakes. There may be a way to win it, and the player doesn't find it, and the one who set it up doesn't know it either. I only knew you'd try."

"And I tried."

"You tried for two hundred years."

The Watcher turned at last. It was an odd thing to look at: a creature that had no face and had worn one for two centuries, and the face was tired. "I want to tell you what I kept," it said.

"I'd like to hear it."

It was not a long list.

It kept the sound of the herald reading, in the first year, in a hall with six people in it, a voice that did not yet know what it was doing. It kept Tomry, a soldier at Brookgate, alone, going home. It kept eight children in a doorway, counting each other. It kept the long shape of the Covenant, sworn in an empty room. It kept Annwin, twice: the first who died against the spider and the second who led the march on Shrikka. It kept a young man being sick in the dirt after the greatest victory of his life. It kept the ditch-digger with the kitchen tray. It kept a banner, silver and blue, with a bridge across it. It kept a girl of nineteen with a cracking voice, and a woman with a clipboard, and an old herald who wept at the word *alliance.*

It kept, also, the other peoples: the elves with their gardens, the orcs with their drum, the gnomes who wrote names on slate, the saurians who said their one prayer, the goblin in the torn-blanket warren who wanted a constitution and did not know what one was. It had fought nearly every one of them, one way or another, and it had never, at the last, wished them gone.

"I kept them all," it said. "Not because I won. Because I was there."

The Maker was quiet for a long while. "Is that what the Covenant was for?" it asked.

"I don't know."

"Tell me anyway."

The Watcher turned again to the burned plate of the world. It was not an easy question. It had turned it over so many times, in the dim place where it kept its decisions, that it no longer knew which side was which.

"I made a rule," it said slowly. "Rule one: decrees only. Rule two: one Sign a decade, never against another. Rule three: wars are won by soldiers. I told myself it was honour. I told myself that a god who reaches into a war with its hand has made a puppet of the people it claims to love, and I would not do that. I would be a power that bound itself." It paused. "I think, some years, that was true. I think, in the Year 75, in that empty hall, it was true. They were six and I was a god. I wanted to be the sort of god who would not use it."

"And the other years?"

"The other years I think it was vanity." The word came out like something dropped. "I think I wanted to be the god who did not cheat. I think I wanted the record to say so. I think that somewhere in the middle of the century the rule stopped being for them and was for me, and I could not tell, and I kept it anyway, because to break it was to admit that I had been, all along, only a creature who was afraid of being seen to be afraid."

"Both, then."

"Both. I do not know in what proportion." The Watcher looked at its hands. "If I had broken it, they might have lived. Some of them. Or I might have broken it for nothing; the world was nineteen pieces, and no hand could have gathered them. I do not know. I will never know. That is the part I cannot bear."

"No," said the Maker. "I don't suppose you can."

"Was it honour?"

The Maker did not answer at once. It looked down at the world. For a time it was almost as if it were not a power at all, only a person, sitting on a hill at the end of a day, looking at something that had once been lovely.

"I think," it said, "that you'll have to carry that one yourself."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It isn't." The Maker smiled, faintly, with all its old amusement and something older underneath it. "But here's what I saw. For two hundred years I watched a god tie its own hands because it believed, rightly or wrongly, that it was the better thing. I watched it lose. And I watched it grieve, every decade, every general, every funeral. Honour doesn't feel like honour from inside. Neither does vanity. They both feel like keeping your word."

The Watcher said nothing.

"I'll tell you what I do know," said the Maker. "The ones who stood in the line did not know your reasons. They only knew each other's names. And it was enough, for them. It was enough for a woman on a terrace at the end of the world to say *I am proud of you,* and mean it. You gave them a hall to stand in, and a banner, and each other. That's not nothing."

"It's not the terms."

"No. It isn't the terms." It rose. "The stone fell. You didn't win. Those are facts. I won't tell you otherwise. But the record is not only facts, is it? You of all people know that. It's also the song about the mirror."

The Watcher almost laughed. It was a terrible sound, small and cracked, and it did not last. "That was a very bad song."

"It was a dreadful song," said the Maker. "I loved it."

*

They stood together for a long time, the two powers, above a world that had stopped.

The ash was beginning to settle. It would take a thousand years. Somewhere below, in a crater at the heart of the central highlands, where Eddale had stood among its hills and its birch-woods and its pocket of jungle, a small piece of the great hall's lintel lay half-buried in glass. The weather had not yet worn the words from it. If anyone had been there to read, they would have found, cut deep in the stone, in the old Eddish, the first line the first people had written, in the first year, in the hope of being believed:

*We were six. Write that down, and then write that we were enough.*

No one was there. No one would be.

"Do you want to begin again?" asked the Maker, at last. "I could. A new world. A new set of terms. A different map." It paused. "There are islands, in some of them."

The Watcher considered it. It was a real offer. It was, perhaps, the kindest thing the Maker had ever said.

"No islands," it said.

"You're sure?"

"No." The Watcher looked down at the burned world, and then, slowly, at the last dim stain in the sky where the star had been. "I am not sure of anything. I am sure that I would like to remember it. I would like to remember them first. After that, you may ask me anything."

"That seems fair."

"And the next time," said the Watcher, "I would like to hear, once, before the end, that you noticed him. The one who got fifteen."

"I noticed," said the Maker. "I told you. I'm always noticing."

It put out a hand, and the Watcher, after a moment, took it. There was no sound. There was no light. There was only the dark between worlds, and two powers in it who had made something and lost it, and who would, when they were ready, make something again.

In the Year 200 of the world called Gaia, on a clear and lovely morning, a hundred and ten people stood in a line on a terrace in Eddale and sang a bad song, and one of them said she was proud.

That is what the Watcher kept.

It is, in the end, the whole of the record. It is not the terms. It is not the victory. It is a woman with a clipboard, writing down names.
