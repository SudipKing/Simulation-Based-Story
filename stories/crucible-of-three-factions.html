Prologue: The Ring of Sand

Two Watchers bent over a world of sand, and one of them said: not yet.

There was a desert, and it went on until the world ended.

It was the colour of old brass in the morning and of dried blood at dusk, and the peoples who would come to live in it would call it the Ochre, because they had no other word for a thing that was everywhere. Nothing grew there but spinewood, a grey, thorned tree that stood alone on the dunes like a hand held up to say stop. To the north and to the east the Ochre ran out to the Brink, the edge of the world, where the sand slid off into nothing and the wind came back from the fall smelling of cold iron. No one who walked to the Brink ever described what they saw there, and no one who was wise ever asked.

But in the middle of the Ochre, like a jewel pressed into a ring, there was something else.

A wall of mountains stood there in a great closed circle, white with snow from shoulder to crown, and the foothills below them were white too. The peoples would call it the Whitewall. Inside the Whitewall lay a country of long grass, green as the inside of a leaf, and through that country ran a river, bright and unhurried, which the elves would one day name the Vein. Nothing lived in the grass. Nothing was permitted to. The mountains could not be climbed and the passes did not exist. The only door into that green heart was the river itself, which left the Whitewall by a single gorge in the south and west and spilled into a wide lake on the desert's edge, a lake large enough for docks and deep enough for keels. The elves would call that lake the Mirror, and the green country beyond the gorge they would call, for two hundred years, simply the Heart, and they would not go there.

Above the world, in the place that is not a place, two Watchers looked down.

One was the Hand. The Hand had made the map and would place the pieces, and it was the Hand who held the Reins. The other was the Scribe, who had been asked to choose the peoples and to write down what became of them. The Scribe loved grand words, and had a habit of writing a thing down before it had quite happened, and of getting a name or a number wrong and carrying on with great confidence. The Hand corrected the Scribe often, and the Scribe was always gracious about it. They were, in their way, good company.

"Three peoples," said the Hand. "Choose."

The Scribe chose orcs for the north, and dwarves, and elves for the water. The Scribe, being the Scribe, set them down at once in the green country by the river, with capitals and kings and a great deal of destiny.

"No," said the Hand. "No one spawns inside the plains. Pick places around it."

So the three peoples were moved out into the sand, each to its own corner of the Ochre, each with its back to something that could not be crossed. And the Hand laid over all three the thing the chronicles call the Cosmic Hold: a stillness in the blood, a law deeper than law. Under the Hold no people could declare war upon another, and no people could swear alliance with another. They could build and breed and bury their dead. They could split apart and found new nations of their own kind. They could even die by violence, for the Hold bound crowns and not knives. But no banner could march on a banner.

"For how long?" asked the Scribe, who had suggested two hundred years.

"Two hundred is too long," said the Hand. "One hundred and fifty. Then I release the Reins, and whatever they do to each other is up to them."

The Scribe wrote it down. The Scribe did not write down, because neither of them yet knew it, that the Hold would last longer than promised, and for a reason that neither Watcher had put into the world. Nor did the Scribe write that three things would walk into this history which no Watcher had made: a figure in a red robe, a field of pumpkins, and a god.

The year was Seven. There were fifteen living souls in the whole of the world.

The Hand let go of the pieces, and kept hold of the Reins.

Chapter One: Five and Five and Five

"No one knows how we came to be. We simply were. Do not let anyone sell you a better story."

(Saying of the Cinder Forge)

They woke in the sand, and they were already grown.

That is the first mystery of the three peoples, and none of them ever solved it. There was no childhood behind them, no mother, no road they had walked. There were five elves on the western shore, five orcs in the far north, five dwarves in the far south, and in each five there was one who was eighteen years old and whom the other four looked at when a decision had to be made.

Among the elves that one was Caelum Strideer.

She woke with her feet in the Mirror. Behind her and above her the Ochre climbed away in dune after dune, and in front of her lay water, the open coast to the west and the great lake curling off toward the white mountains. She stood for a long time looking at the gorge where the river came out of the Whitewall, at the green light that seemed to leak from it, and something in her chest turned toward it the way a plant turns to a window. She felt the Hold then, though she had no name for it. It was not a wall. It was more like a hand laid flat and gentle on the top of her head. Not there. Not yet.

"Then here," she said aloud, to four people who had been alive for less than an hour. "Here, by the water. We will make it green ourselves."

They called the place Thalassia, and themselves the Azure Imperium, which was a very large name for five people and a spinewood lean-to. Caelum chose the colour of their banner on the first day. It was green: the colour of the place she could see and could not have.

In the north, a thousand dunes away, an orc named Gorkha woke face down with the sun already on his back. By the time he understood what the sun was, it had taken the skin off his shoulders, and it never fully gave it back. The others named him Gorkha the Burned, and he liked it, for it meant that the worst had happened to him first and he had got up anyway. His country was a corner. The Whitewall stood to his west, the Brink ran to his north and to his east, and the only way that was not a wall or a fall was south. He raised a ring of stones around a seep of bitter water and called it Khar Zul, and he called his five the Dune Scourge, because he meant them to be the thing the desert feared and not the other way about. He dyed a rag in the red clay of the seep and tied it to a spinewood pole. That was the crimson banner.

And in the south, at the last point of land before the Brink, a dwarf sat up in the sand, coughed, and began at once to dig. His name was Thane Varis, and he had a beard the colour of raw iron before he had ever seen iron. He found no ore that day or for many years. The Ochre gave the dwarves what it gave everyone: sand, and grey thorned wood, and nothing else. But he dug a hearth pit, and struck a fire in it with spinewood and patience, and the five of them sat around it on the first night of the world with the Brink at their backs and the whole desert in front of them. They called the hearth Khazad Krum and the nation the Cinder Forge. They had nothing to forge. They named themselves for what they intended.

Straight north of Thane Varis, far beyond sight, was Gorkha the Burned. West and north of him was desert. East and behind was the end of everything. Neither king knew the other existed, and the elves knew of neither.

Fifteen souls, three fires, one ring of sand. The Hand watched them, and waited, and let the years begin.

Chapter Two: The Unbroken Chain

"A crown with no heir is a dropped cup. We decided, early, to catch it."

(Thalassian law-saying)

The elves had no bread for a long time.

Nothing they pushed into the sand came up again. So they turned their faces to the water, as Caelum had on the first morning, and by the Year 35 there was a dock at Thalassia, a real one, of spinewood piles driven into the lakebed, and tied to it were the first fishing boats in the world. The Mirror fed them. Twenty-three elves lived on that shore by then, and every one of them smelled of fish and woodsmoke, and they were content.

Queen Caelum Strideer died the following year, in her bed, with the shutters open to the west. She was the first of the founders to go, and she left no child.

It is hard now to understand how near the Azure Imperium came to ending that week. Twenty-three people is not a nation. It is three families and a quarrel. There were already two clans in Thalassia who did not share nets, and with no heir of Caelum's body to stand between them, the natural thing, the thing that would happen again and again in the north, was for each clan to take half the boats and call itself a kingdom.

They did not. On the night of the queen's burning, the elders of both clans stood on the dock and swore the oath that elven law remembers as the Unbroken Chain: that the Imperium is one thing and shall stay one thing, and that when a ruler dies and no heir remains, the throne passes whole and undivided to the head of the strongest clan, and every other clan kneels. No halves. No second crowns. A chain, they said, in which each link is a different metal, and which does not break.

It was the wisest thing any people did in the whole of the Long Hold, and they did it when they were twenty-three.

The first link after Caelum was Yage, who was thirty and ambitious. He had plans the way other elves had fish. He wanted a second dock, a third, a city on the far shore of the Mirror, a road to the gorge for the day the hand lifted from the top of their heads. In the first month of his reign he cut a staff from the oldest spinewood on the shore, stripped the thorns from it himself until his palms bled, and named it the Heart of Azure. He said that it was the Imperium made into a thing you could hold. He carried it everywhere. When he spoke in council he struck the dock with it, and elves who were children then remembered the sound all their long lives.

Yage reigned ten years, and all his plans were still plans when he died. The Year was 46. The Chain held a second time, and the strongest clan in Thalassia was no longer his.

The king it raised was twenty-three years old, soft-spoken and narrow-eyed, and the first thing he did upon taking the throne was to have the fish counted. All of them. Then the boats, the nets, the floats, the coils of rope, and last of all the people. There were thirty-eight elves. He wrote the number on a strip of bark and kept it in his sleeve.

His name was Tua. The chroniclers of his own court, who loved him, describe him in two words, and they never soften either one: he was greedy, and he was a pacifist. He wanted everything, and he could not bear to spend anything to get it, least of all a life. He took the Heart of Azure from Yage's bier, because it was valuable, and did not strike the dock with it once.

No one on the shore that day imagined they were looking at the last king the elves would ever need. He would still be counting two hundred and fifty years later.

Chapter Three: The Stone Spear

"He has killed two. Ask him, and he will tell you their names. He will not tell you anything else."

(Said of King Tua)

King Tua's greed was of a particular kind, and the fate of the world turned on it, so it is worth describing.

He did not love gold; the Ochre had none. He did not love jewels or feasts. What Tua loved was having, and above all he loved the count going up. Thirty-eight elves. Forty. Fifty-one. Every birth in Thalassia was reported to the king before it was reported to the grandparents, and he would take the bark strip from his sleeve and correct it, and for the rest of that day he was a happy man. A death undid him for a week. He regarded war, which he had never seen and which the Hold made impossible, as a kind of deliberate, lunatic waste: the spending of the one coin that mattered. That was the whole of his pacifism. It had nothing to do with gentleness. It was a miser's horror of loss.

So the elves under Tua grew and did not move. In the Year 57 the shipwrights of Thalassia launched a new kind of boat, broad and deep, that could carry a dozen elves and their goods across open water. A transport. Yage would have filled it with settlers and sent it to the far shore before the pitch was dry. Tua looked at it, asked how many fish it could hold, and sent it out with the fishing fleet. It carried nets for a generation.

He let go of exactly three people. In the Year 62, pressed by a council that had run out of room on the home shore, the king gave leave for three settlers to go west along the coast. He chose them himself, saw them off himself, and is said to have stood on the dock until their sail was gone. They founded a village on a spit of hard sand and called it Thirefin. It had sixteen people in it eighty years later. Tua asked after each of them by name.

Then came the Year 77, and the night the king learned what his hands could do.

The clan of Yage had never forgiven the Chain for passing them over. They had forgiven still less the sight of Yage's staff, the Heart of Azure, standing unused in the corner of a counting-house while a miser tallied herring. The Hold forbade war between peoples. It forbade nothing between two cousins and a king in a dark room. In the autumn of that year, two of Yage's kin came into the counting-house after moonset to take the Heart of Azure back, and the throne with it.

Tua was awake. He was always awake; he was counting. There was no weapon in the room but a curiosity he kept on his table: a spearhead of black lake-flint that had come up in a net years before, the only worked stone in the whole Imperium, which he had lashed to a spinewood shaft simply because nobody else had one. He picked it up.

The court never learned exactly what happened. In the morning there were two dead elves on the floor of the counting-house, and the king was sitting between them with the stone spear across his knees and the bark strip in his hand. He had already corrected the number. He had subtracted two.

He never spoke of it, and he never put the spear down again. For the next two hundred and twenty-three years, in council, at prayer, at table, on the dock in the evening, King Tua carried that same stone spear, so that one of his hands would always be full and could never again be surprised into anything. The Rangers who came later, armed with mithril, thought it a quaint old man's habit. The few who knew the story did not think it quaint at all.

After that night he ordered the Heart of Azure carried out of the counting-house and set up in a small hall of woven spinewood at the water's edge. He did not say why. In time, elves began to go and sit near it. They said the staff was warm to the touch, even in winter, and that when the wind came off the Mirror it seemed to lean toward the gorge and the green country beyond. They began to say that something lived in growing things, something that had been in the first spinewood before the first elf, and that it listened. They gave it a name, a short one, the kind a child could say.

They called it Hil.

Neither Watcher had made a god. The Scribe looked for the name in the ledgers and did not find it. The Hand said nothing, and watched the little hall fill up.

Chapter Four: Bread in the Desert

"The elves ate what the water gave them. We ate what we forced the sand to give. Remember which of us learned to force."

(Orc saying of Khar Zul)

In the north and in the south, far from the water and far from each other, two peoples made the same discovery in the same generation, and neither ever knew of the other's.

They made bread.

Gorkha the Burned did it by stubbornness. There was a grass that grew in the lee of the Whitewall where the snowmelt seeped under the sand, a thin, miserable, bearded grass, and Gorkha had his people gather the seed of it and scratch it into every damp hollow within a day's walk of Khar Zul, year after year, keeping only the seed of the plants that lived. By the Year 36 the hollows were gold in season. The orcs ground the grain between stones, wet it, and laid it on the rocks at noon, and the desert baked it for them. There were thirteen orcs by then. Gorkha held up the first flat loaf and told them it was the desert's tribute, and that the Dune Scourge had begun to collect.

Thane Varis Iron Beard did it by method. The dwarves dug. They found that the sand of the far south lay over clay, and that clay holds water, and that a pit dug to the clay and lined with spinewood ash would grow a crop where the open dune would not. They sowed wheat in the pits, and in the deeper, wetter pits, close under the walls of Khazad Krum, they sowed a creeping vine that gave a great orange gourd. Twelve dwarves ate bread and roasted pumpkin by the hearth that year. They were pleased with the pumpkins especially. They were so easy. You cut them from the vine, and split them, and scooped them hollow, and the vine simply grew more.

The founders aged very differently. Gorkha was gone by the Year 56. Orcs burn quick, and he had been burning since his first morning. The crown of Khar Zul passed to Ezo, who was forty-nine years old and who had been born in the Year 7 itself, the first child of the world, the first living thing in that whole country that had a mother. The orcs held her in something like awe. With her the idea took root that would shape and finally shatter the north: that there was a Royalty, a bloodline that ran from the Burned through Ezo, and that a crown belonged in it and nowhere else. Ezo ruled seventeen years, and bore many children, and in the Year 73 the crown passed to her daughter Daza without a single voice raised.

Thane Varis, meanwhile, simply went on. Dwarves are made of slower stuff. He had been eighteen at the first hearth; he was a hundred and three in the Year 92, grey as a file and not noticeably weaker, when he walked out into the western dunes with a rope and a bundle of stakes and laid out the ground plan of a second city. He called it Silta. He gave it to his son Esotar, who was thirty-six, to govern.

"It is only sand," Esotar is supposed to have said, looking at his inheritance.

"It was all only sand," said his father. "Dig."

So the first century of the world closed. In the west a miser counted elves beside a lake. In the north the daughters of Ezo ruled a red-bannered people who had taught the desert to pay. In the south an iron-bearded king, as old as the world, tended his hearth and his wheat and his rows of fat orange gourds. Three peoples, each certain it was alone in the sand, each with its back to the Brink or the sea, and all of them, without knowing it, growing slowly toward one another across the Ochre like roots toward water.

Chapter Five: The House That Could Not Hold

"The elves forged a chain. The orcs had only a rope, and every king who pulled on it found it shorter."

(From the Scribe's ledger)

The Azure Imperium had the Unbroken Chain. The Dune Scourge had only blood, and blood, as the orcs were about to learn, can be claimed by more than one person at a time.

Queen Daza died in the Year 111. The crown went to her brother Gozan, who was thirty-five, with shoulders like a doorframe and lungs like wet paper. He could lift a millstone, and a walk across Khar Zul left him grey and gasping. Perhaps it was that, the knowledge of a strong body that might fail him at any hour, that made him what he was. Gozan trusted no one. He saw knives in every sleeve. He had his bread baked in front of him and moved his bed every night.

One month after he was crowned, he founded a city.

He said it was to honour his mother, and he named it Ezo Zul. In truth it was a bolt-hole, a second ring of stones a few days south of the capital where a king might run if his own court turned on him. He sent a kinsman of the Royalty named Eto to govern it, chosen because he was young and seemed harmless. And then, being Gozan, he began at once to suspect the governor of his own refuge, and to send him orders, and counter-orders, and spies to watch the spies.

For nineteen years he ruled like that. When he died in the Year 130, worn out by his own heart, there was no one in the north who trusted anyone. It took three months for the Royalty to agree on a successor, and in those three months the rope frayed through. The throne went to Gozan's elder sister Gazia. And Ezo Zul, the city built for a paranoid king's flight, torn by years of his meddling, simply declined to be ruled from Khar Zul any longer. Eto, forty-three, proclaimed himself a king. He named his nation Zeteurg.

Under the Hold, Gazia could do nothing. No banner could march on a banner, and Zeteurg now had a banner. She answered in the only way open to her. A month later she founded a new city of her own, bigger than the one she had lost, and named it Gaz Zul after herself, so that no one could mistake whose it was. She set over it the eldest and steadiest of the royal kin she could find, an orc of nearly sixty named Ego.

Ego was Eto's older brother.

Gazia died in the Year 141. Again the Royalty wrangled, two months this time, and the crown came at last to her sister Ates. And again, in the gap, a governor looked at his walls and his granaries and his own royal blood, and asked himself why he should kneel to a sister of Gazia when his younger brother knelt to no one. Ego of Gaz Zul declared himself a king. He called his nation Nepokuh.

So within thirty years of Gozan's coronation the north had three crowns where there had been one: Ates in Khar Zul under the old crimson banner, Eto in Ezo Zul, Ego in Gaz Zul. Two brothers and their cousin, each a true child of the Burned, each ruling a single city and calling it a realm.

Ego enjoyed his crown for three years. When he died in the Year 144, something happened that had never happened among the orcs. He had broken from his clan to take his throne, and so his clan, by the old custom, owed his throne nothing. No one of the Royalty came forward. The strongest household in Gaz Zul was a family of grain-factors with no drop of Gorkha's blood, and its head, a broad, practical orc named Zan Tor, sat down in Ego's chair because someone had to.

A king who was not of the Royalty. In Khar Zul they spat when they heard it. In the hollows where the common orcs cut wheat, they heard it too, and they did not spit. They grew thoughtful.

Chapter Six: The Counting

Year 145: twelve years before the breaking of the Hold, the world was weighed.

In the Year 145 the Scribe, who liked ledgers, drew up the first great census of the world. The Hold had twelve years to run. No people yet knew for certain that the others existed, though all three had begun to suspect it: a line of smoke on the wrong horizon, a strange sail far down the coast, a footprint too broad or too narrow at a desert seep. The Scribe set the numbers side by side, and the Hand looked at them for a long time.

In the north, Queen Ates ruled one hundred and seventy-two orcs from Khar Zul. Forty-four of them were soldiers, spear-carriers in boiled leather who called themselves the Raiders, though under the Hold they had never yet raided anything. King Eto in Ezo Zul and King Zan Tor in Gaz Zul each ruled twenty-two souls, and neither had a single soldier. They were kingdoms the size of a wedding.

In the south, King Thane Varis Iron Beard, one hundred and fifty-six years old, ruled one hundred and seventy-seven dwarves. One hundred and fifty-six lived in Khazad Krum around the first hearth, and fifty-three of those were the Hearthguard, sworn to the fire itself, who drilled on the clay flats each dawn with axes and short bows. Twenty-one more lived in Silta under Esotar, and Silta had no soldiers at all.

And in the west, King Tua took the bark strip from his sleeve, which by now was a scroll, and read the Scribe his number with a pride he did not trouble to hide. Three hundred and sixty-six elves. Three hundred and fifty in Thalassia. Sixteen in Thirefin. And of the three hundred and fifty, two hundred and seven under arms.

This was the strangest figure in the whole ledger, and it had the Scribe scratching for some time. The pacifist king, the miser who would not risk three settlers without watching them out of sight, had made more than half his people into soldiers. They were called the Rangers. They carried longbows of laminated spinewood and wore the green of Caelum's banner, and there were more of them than there were dwarves in the world.

But it was no contradiction, and the Hand understood it. A man who cannot bear to lose a single coin does not leave his door unlocked. Tua had never wanted war. He had wanted, with his whole grasping heart, never to be subtracted from again. So he had built a wall, and built it out of the only material he had in abundance. He had no intention of ever using them. He simply wanted the count protected.

Two hundred and seven Rangers. Fifty-three Hearthguard. Forty-four Raiders. One elf under arms for every living orc in Khar Zul.

"When the Hold breaks," said the Scribe, with relish, "the elves will roll over them like the tide."

"The Hold has twelve years," said the Hand. "A great deal can happen in twelve years."

It was the ninth month of the following year when a stranger in a red robe walked out of the empty desert and into the main street of Ezo Zul.

Chapter Seven: The Stranger in Red

"It did not want anything. That was the worst of it. You can bargain with a thing that wants."

(Testimony of a water-carrier of Ezo Zul, who did not survive the year)

He came in the ninth month of the Year 146, at the hour when the shadows in Ezo Zul were shortest and everyone who could be indoors was indoors.

The children at the well saw him first. A tall figure walking up out of the open sand from the south, where there was nothing, where there had never been anything. He wore a robe of deep red, the red of the Dune Scourge's own banner, but finer than any cloth the orcs could weave, and at his brow, binding the hood, ran a circlet of golden thread that caught the sun. No one could see his face. In his right hand he carried a staff of some grey metal, and the orcs of Ezo Zul, who had never seen metal of any kind, did not know what it was. At its head, held in a cage of the same grey, burned a stone the size of a fist, glowing the deep orange of a coal that has been blown on.

The children called out. An old orc came out of the nearest house to see who the visitor was, and lifted his hand in greeting, and the stranger lifted the staff.

What came out of the stone was not like any fire the orcs knew. It did not crackle or spread. It poured, like water from a jar, and where it poured, things simply became fire. The old orc. His doorway. The roof behind him. The stranger walked on, up the street, and lifted the staff again.

He did not hurry. That was the thing every survivor remembered, in the short time any of them survived. He walked the way a farmer walks a field at harvest, unhurried and thorough, and whatever stood in his way, he burned. Orcs who ran from him he let run. Orcs who barred a door against him, or stood in the street with a spade, or tried to drag a child out of his path, he burned. It was as though he had come to Ezo Zul to burn its houses, and the people were simply things that sometimes got in front of houses.

King Eto had twenty-two subjects and no soldiers. He had a crown he had taken for himself sixteen years before, and he did what a king does. He gathered every orc who could hold a tool and went out to meet the stranger in the street. That was in the Year 147. Eto lived five months under the Harbinger. On the last day of the fifth month he stood in front of his own hall with a wheat-flail in his hands, and the Harbinger came up the street, and Eto did not get out of the way.

They had a name for the stranger by then. The orcs of Ezo Zul called him the Harbinger, because it was all he ever brought. They said it in whispers, as if he might hear his name and come.

By the eighth month of the Year 147 there were four orcs left in Ezo Zul. One of them, a young herder named Ake, put on Eto's crown, because the Royalty had taught the north that a people without a crowned head was not a people, and Ake would not let Zeteurg die headless. He was king for six days. On the seventh, the Harbinger found the cellar where the four of them were hiding, and Ake went up the steps alone to meet him so that the others might get out the back. They did not get far.

By the first month of the Year 148, the city of Ezo Zul and the kingdom of Zeteurg had ceased to exist. Twenty-five orcs had lived there, counting the children born during the siege, and one stranger had killed every one of them. He had done it under the Cosmic Hold, which bound nations and not knives, and which he had walked straight through as if it were not there.

The Scribe searched the ledger for him and found nothing. He had not been placed. He was not one of the three peoples. He was not a people at all.

"Where did he come from?" asked the Scribe.

The Hand, for once, did not answer.

Chapter Eight: The Raiders' Hunt

"We marched out forty-four. We came home twenty-seven, and we did not kill it. Write that down too."

(Spoken by a Raider of Khar Zul)

The smoke reached Khar Zul before any messenger did.

It had been rising out of the south for a year, a thin brown smear above the dunes, and in the capital they had told one another it was Eto burning spinewood for charcoal, or a grass fire, or a grudge. But in the winter of the Year 147 the smoke grew thick and black and steady, and the wind brought the smell of it, and Queen Ates climbed the stone ring of Khar Zul and looked south for a long time. She was sixty-four, the third of Ezo's daughters to wear the crown, a short, square, unhurried orc who had spent her reign mending the damage of her siblings' quarrels. She did not like Eto. She did not like any of the breakaway kings. But the smoke over Ezo Zul was the smoke of orc houses, and she knew it.

"The Raiders," she said.

There were forty-four of them, and they had never fought anything. They had drilled for a war they were forbidden to wage, against enemies they had never seen. Now they marched south under the crimson banner, spears on their shoulders, in boiled-leather harness that creaked in the cold, and found at the end of their march a city of ash and a single figure in red walking among the ruins as if inspecting them.

They spent six months killing seventeen of their own trying to bring it down.

Spears did not reach it. The Raiders learned that in the first hour. They learned that fire poured from the ember stone farther than a spear could be thrown, and that it did not care about boiled leather. They learned to come at the Harbinger from several sides at once, from behind ruined walls, by night. They learned that the stranger did not sleep and did not eat and that, if he was wounded, the wounds did not slow him. They drove him out of Ezo Zul at last by the simple method of being too many and too stubborn, and he walked south into the sand with the Raiders behind him, burning them when they came close and walking on.

South, and south, for weeks. Further south than any orc had ever been. Until, in the late autumn of the Year 148, the dunes beneath their feet turned to hard grey clay with old wheat-stubble in it, and the Raiders understood, slowly and with a cold feeling in their stomachs, that they were in somebody's fields.

Chapter Nine: One Blow

"I saw armour, and I saw a thing in red running away from armour. I did not need to know anything else."

(Von Rrim, as remembered by the Hearthguard of Khazad Krum)

Von Rrim was twenty-four years old that autumn, a soldier of the Hearthguard of Khazad Krum, and he was walking the northern edge of the Cinder Forge because it was his turn to.

It was the dullest duty in the Hearthguard. There was nothing on the northern edge. There was the last line of grain pits, and beyond it the Ochre, and beyond the Ochre, everyone knew, more Ochre all the way to the end of the world. The old dwarves told stories of seeing smoke there sometimes, very far off, but the old dwarves told stories about everything. Von Rrim had volunteered for the northern patrol because he liked to be alone, and because he liked to think, and the northern edge was the one place in the Cinder Forge where nobody would ask him what he was thinking about.

He came over a low ridge with his axe on his shoulder and stopped.

Below him, coming fast across the stubble, was a figure in a long red robe with a burning stone on a staff. Behind it, a long way behind it, came a line of tall, heavy, grey-green people he had never seen before, in harness of hardened leather, carrying spears.

Von Rrim did not know what an orc was. He had never been told there were other peoples in the world. He looked at the line of grey-green strangers, and saw that they wore matching harness and held their spears all the same way, and thought: soldiers. He looked at the figure in red, and saw that it was running from soldiers, toward the grain pits of the Cinder Forge, toward Khazad Krum, toward his home. He thought: then that is the enemy.

That was the whole of his reasoning. The Hearthguard would later tell it as a marvel of judgement. Von Rrim himself never told it at all.

He went down the ridge at a run. The Harbinger turned, and lifted the staff, and the stone flared, and the dwarf did not slow down. Dwarves are quick on their feet, much quicker than orcs, and he was inside the reach of the staff before the fire had finished leaving it. He felt the heat take the hair off his left arm. He swung the axe once, flat and low, with his whole weight behind it.

And the Harbinger came apart.

Not as a body comes apart. There was no blood. The red robe folded in on itself and fell, and inside it there was nothing but a fine grey dust that blew away across the stubble in the wind from the north. The golden threads of the circlet lay in the dust for a moment, glinting, and then they were dust too. Only the staff remained, lying across the furrow, with the ember stone dimmed to a sullen orange and beating, very faintly, like a heart.

Von Rrim stood over it for a long time. Then he bent and picked it up.

The stone was warm. That was all, at first. Just warm, in the cold of the autumn evening, the way a hearthstone is warm in the morning after a long fire. But as he held it, he thought, and ever afterward he could not swear to this, that he felt something on the other end of it, the way you feel a fish take a line. Something very far away, and very patient, that noticed him.

When he looked up, the orcs had come up to the edge of the field. Twenty-seven of them, spears grounded, breathing hard, staring at the young dwarf with the Harbinger's staff in his hand.

Nobody moved. Nobody on either side had a word of the other's language, and if they had, there would have been nothing to say. The Hold lay over all of them, quiet and heavy, and in the stillness it made, they understood one another perfectly well. The orcs had hunted the thing for six months and lost seventeen. The dwarf had killed it in one stroke, on his own land. It was his.

The leader of the Raiders lifted his spear a little, point to the sky, and lowered it again. Von Rrim nodded. Then he turned and walked south toward Khazad Krum with the staff on his shoulder, and did not look back, and the orcs watched him go until he was a speck on the clay, and then they made certain there was nothing left of the Harbinger but the dust, and turned north for home.

That was how the three peoples first met: across a dead field in the Year 148, over a pile of ash that had once been something none of them understood.

The Hearthguard gave Von Rrim a feast. King Thane Varis, a hundred and fifty-nine years old, looked at the staff for a long while and did not touch it. "Keep it close," he said. "Keep it closer than your axe. Something that does not die does not usually leave its tools lying about by accident."

Von Rrim kept it close. He kept it close for a hundred and fifty years.

Chapter Ten: The Queen of the Ruins

"Usae of the Royalty, who built on ash, because ash was what the Harbinger left."

(Inscription at Usar, now lost)

Queen Ates died in her sleep in the Year 151, in Khar Zul, with the crimson banner over her bed. She had sent the Raiders south when the smoke came, and brought most of them home again, and she was remembered for it.

The crown went to Perde, who was sixty-eight, and the last living daughter of Ezo herself. The last link to the first mother of the world. The orcs of Khar Zul gathered to see her crowned the way people gather to see a comet, knowing they will not see another.

That same year another woman of the Royalty, a granddaughter of Daza named Usae, walked south to the ruins of Ezo Zul with twelve orcs behind her.

Nothing stood there. The Harbinger had burned it to the footings. But the seep still ran, and the wheat-hollows were still damp under the ash, and Usae was of the Royalty, and believed, as the Royalty always believed, that a crowned head made a people. She cleared the ash and built in the cleared places. She named the new town Usar. Chapter Eleven: The Gourd Rising

"We split them and scooped them and threw the hollow shells on the midden. For a hundred and fifty years. It never once occurred to us that they were watching."

(Kerrim, Regent of the Cinder Forge)

The dwarves of Khazad Krum remembered it afterward as the most beautiful autumn of their lives.

The Year 153 had been wet. The clay held its water, and the pits brimmed, and the pumpkin vines under the walls of Khazad Krum ran riot. The gourds that year were enormous, great ribbed orange moons as wide as a dwarf was tall. The farmers went out among them with their sickles and their scoops, as dwarves had every autumn since Thane Varis first planted the vine, and cut them from the stem and split them open and scraped them hollow, and threw the hollow shells on the great midden north of the city. They sang while they did it. It was harvest.

It was the end of the year, and the first snow that ever fell in the far south was falling, when the shells on the midden began to move.

No one has ever explained it. The dwarves who lived told it many ways. That the vines had been fed on the ash of a hundred years of hearth-fires, and something in the ash had woken. That the Harbinger's staff had been brought into the city, and the stone in it had called something up out of the ground. That the pumpkins had simply watched, for a hundred and fifty years, how the dwarves treated growing things, and had learned the lesson, and had decided to return it. In Thalassia, much later, when the elves heard the story, they listened very quietly, and then went to sit near the Heart of Azure in the little hall by the water, and did not say what they thought.

Eighty-two of them rose.

They came up out of the midden and out of the fields, great hollowed shells with their split faces gaping and the cut stems sprouting new vine from every wound, and the vine was their arms and legs, thick as a dwarf's wrist and green-black and strong as cable. Where the scoop had carved them, light came out of them, a cold yellow-orange light like a lantern with no flame. And they came into Khazad Krum and did to the dwarves what the dwarves had done to them.

The Hearthguard fought. There were fifty-three of them, and they were the finest soldiers in the south, and they had never fought anything. Axes split the gourds and the gourds kept coming, held together by the vine inside. Arrows went straight through them. The Hearthguard learned, in that first night, that only fire stopped the vine from growing back, and that the dwarves of Khazad Krum, who had built their whole nation around a hearth, could not bear to set their own city alight.

Chapter Twelve: The Death of the Iron Beard

"He was the first of the world, and he fell in a pumpkin patch. The Watchers have a cruel sense of humour."

(Saying of Silta)

King Thane Varis Iron Beard died in the Year 154, in the great hall of Khazad Krum, in front of the first hearth.

He was a hundred and sixty-five years old. He had woken in the sand on the first morning of the world with nothing, and dug a hole and lit a fire in it, and everything the dwarves had or were had grown outward from that fire. When the gourds broke into the hall he took down the old digging-spade that hung above the hearth, the very spade that had dug it, which no one had touched in a century, and he stood in the doorway with it. The chronicles of the Cinder Forge say that he split eleven of them. The chronicles of the Cinder Forge were written by dwarves who loved him. But it is certain that it took more than one of them to bring him down, and that when he fell, he fell across the hearth, and the fire went out.

The fighting was so bad that it was four months before the Cinder Forge could crown his son. Esotar had been governor of Silta for sixty-two years. He came up from the western desert with every dwarf of Silta who could lift an axe, and they put the crown on his head in a guardroom with the door barricaded, and he walked out and went straight back to the fighting.

He held for three years. Three years of war in the streets and the cellars and the grain pits, the gourds rising, dying, rising again from the vine-ends left in the earth. The Hearthguard died one by one, all fifty-three of them, until there was no Hearthguard. The dwarves of Khazad Krum died in their hundreds. In the Year 157, the year the Cosmic Hold had been meant to break, King Esotar was killed in a cellar under the west wall by the last great knot of the gourds, and the throne of the Cinder Forge passed, two months later, to a child.

His name was Bers. He was two years old. He had a twin brother named Kor, also two, and the two of them, sitting in a single blanket in the dark of a storeroom, were the whole remaining royal blood of the dwarves.

It took four more months to burn the last of the pumpkins. They burned them in the end, and the fields, and the midden, and the great hall of Khazad Krum with them, because there was no other way. When the smoke cleared there were twenty-eight dwarves left alive in all the Cinder Forge. The second-greatest nation in the world, a hundred and seventy-seven strong only twelve years before, had become a village. Every one of its five remaining soldiers was from Silta.

And all the while, high above, the Reins had stayed tight in the Hand's grip.

"It is time," the Scribe had said in the Year 157, with the ledger open. "One hundred and fifty years. You promised them."

"I promised to release them," said the Hand. "Not to feed the dwarves to the orcs. Look at them. Look at what's left. If I let go now, the Cinder Forge will not last a season." The Hand was silent for a moment. "Hold. Until two hundred. Give the dwarves a chance."

So the Hold was kept forty-three years beyond its time, and the orcs and the elves never knew how near they had come to war, nor that they owed every year of their peace to eighty-two pumpkins.

Chapter Thirteen: The Regent and the Soldier

"A king must be grown, like anything else. Until then, someone must stand in front of the cradle."

(Kerrim of Silta)

A two-year-old cannot rule a nation, and the dwarves had no Unbroken Chain. What they had was Kerrim.

He was the governor of Silta, Esotar's lieutenant of many years, a lean and sun-dark dwarf with a stoop who had never wanted anything in his life except for Silta's wells to stay sweet and its walls to stay up. When the news of Esotar's death came, he called the twenty-eight survivors together in the ashes of the great hall, and stood in front of the twins in their blanket, and told them how it would be. He would rule. He would rule in the name of King Bers, and only until King Bers was grown enough to rule himself, and on that day he would put the crown into the boy's hands with his own. He made them all swear to hold him to it. They swore.

His first act was to give the five surviving soldiers a name. They were all Silta dwarves, all young, all that remained of a nation's strength, and he called them the Forge, because a forge is where broken things are made whole. His second act was to set them to rebuilding Khazad Krum. Not the hall, which was gone, nor the fields, which were poisoned with vine-root and would be burned and re-burned for years. The walls. The houses. The hearth. He had the first hearth dug out of the ruins and swept clean and lit again, with spinewood carried on dwarves' backs from Silta.

His third act was to send for Von Rrim.

The slayer of the Harbinger was thirty-five that year, and had come through the pumpkin war without a mark on him. That was the strangest thing about it, and it was whispered. He had fought in every street of Khazad Krum. He had been seen in the thick of the gourds, many times, with his axe in one hand and the Harbinger's staff in the other. Some said they had seen fire come from the staff in his hand, and some said they had not. But he had not been hurt, not once, in four years of a war that killed every other soldier in the city. He would not say why. He said very little about anything.

Kerrim made him governor of Krum.

"You do not trust him," Kerrim's wife said to him that night. "You are giving him the capital and you do not trust him."

"I trust him to keep it standing," said Kerrim. "That is not the same thing as trusting him. But it is what we need."

Von Rrim governed Khazad Krum for more than forty years. Under him the ruined city became a working town again: small, scarred, half-empty, rebuilt on the old footings. Its fields were burned clean every autumn and planted with wheat and nothing but wheat. No dwarf in the Cinder Forge ever planted a pumpkin again. And the staff of the Harbinger stood in the corner of the governor's house, with its stone glowing faintly in the dark, like an eye half-closed.

Chapter Fourteen: The Children of the North

"Our crowns pass to children now. A child does not remember why the last war began. That is a mercy, and it is also a danger."

(Saying of Khar Zul)

In the north, while the south burned, the crowns of the Royalty passed down the generations like buckets down a well.

King Zan Tor of Gaz Zul, the first orc in history to rule who was not of the Royalty, died in the Year 164. His son Az Geze was nine. Gaz Zul had not been much of a kingdom under Zan Tor and was even less of one under a boy, and the Royalty in Khar Zul waited, with folded hands, for the upstart city to crawl back. It did not. Az Geze grew up. And Gaz Zul, which had been a granary town, grew up with him. Its people were mostly farmers, as Zan Tor's family had been, and they took a quiet pride in having a king who had come up out of the wheat like themselves. Az Geze was thirty-seven by the Year 200, and he ruled a hundred and forty-three orcs, and, for the first time, he had soldiers: thirty-nine of them, big, slow, heavy-shouldered farmhands with spears, who called themselves the Brutes, and meant it as a joke, and were not joking.

In Usar, on the ashes of Ezo Zul, Queen Usae died in the Year 165. Her son Etan, twenty-two, took her crown and kept it for thirty-five years, ruling a people that never numbered more than a dozen. Raier was the poorest kingdom in the world, built on the graves of the Harbinger's victims, and its people were the proudest. They had little else.

And in Khar Zul, in the Year 169, Queen Perde died at eighty-four, and the last daughter of Ezo went into the earth. Her son Adens was crowned at twenty-one: the eighth ruler of the Dune Scourge, counting from Gorkha the Burned. Adens did not remember a time before the breakaway kingdoms. He had grown up with three crowns in the north and thought it an insult every day of his life.

He spent thirty years building an army. By the Year 200, the Dune Scourge had three hundred and fifty-one orcs, and a hundred and twenty-six of them were Raiders.

Chapter Fifteen: The Releasing of the Reins

Year 200: the Hand opened, and the Reins fell.

The Scribe opened the ledger in the Year 200 and read the world aloud, one last time under the Hold.

The Azure Imperium, ruled by its third sovereign, King Tua, aged one hundred and seventy-seven: three hundred and eighty-six elves. Three hundred and sixty-seven in Thalassia, with two hundred and eighteen Rangers. Nineteen in Thirefin, of whom eight were soldiers of the village's own small guard, the Vanguard. Tua had reigned a hundred and fifty-four years. He had grown thin and dry and very still. He still carried the stone spear.

The Cinder Forge, ruled by its third sovereign, King Bers, aged forty-four: ninety-seven dwarves. Eighty in Silta, with twenty soldiers of the Forge. Seventeen in Khazad Krum under Governor Von Rrim, with no soldiers at all. Kerrim had kept his word. He had put the crown into Bers's hands years before, on the day the young king came of age, and stepped back to Silta, and the dwarves still called him the Regent out of love, though he was regent of nothing.

The Dune Scourge, ruled by its eighth sovereign, King Adens, aged forty-eight: three hundred and fifty-one orcs, with a hundred and twenty-six Raiders in Khar Zul.

The Long Hold was over. It had lasted a hundred and ninety-three years. The Scribe would chronicle only one more century of the world. It would be enough.

Chapter Sixteen: Ga Rek

"She was a nobody. She cut bread for a living. And she killed three Raiders with a bread-knife, and they made her a queen for it, and she deserved it more than any of us."

(Saying of the orcs of Usar)

The first war of the world lasted two years and was fought against five people.

King Etan of Raier died in the Year 200, within months of the Reins falling, an old orc in a poor town. His crown went to a cousin named Seon. And in the Year 201, before Seon had worn it a full season, King Adens of the Dune Scourge declared war on Raier.

It was not a war anybody would have chosen to fight. Raier had five subjects and no soldiers. But Adens had spent his whole reign building an army for the day the Hold broke, and now it had broken, and the nearest banner that was not his own flew over Usar, on land that had been Dune Scourge land before the Harbinger burned it. It was a matter of tidiness, Adens said. Of making the north one thing again. He sent a column of Raiders south down the old road with orders to bring Usar back under the crimson.

They killed King Seon in the first week. A single Raider, at the door of the town, with a single spear-thrust. Then they settled down to starve the rest.

The rest were four. One of them was a breadmaker named Ga Rek.

She was not of the Royalty. She had never held a weapon. She had been born in Usar to parents who had walked south from Khar Zul with Usae, and she had spent her life grinding wheat in the hollows and baking it on the hot rocks, as orcs had done since Gorkha's time. When the Raiders came into the town three months into the siege, to finish it, she was in her bakehouse. Three of them came through the door.

Not one of them came out.

No one in the north ever learned exactly how she did it. The Raiders who found the bodies said there was a bread-knife and a quern-stone and an oven door involved, and that the bakehouse looked as though a storm had been through it. But the three survivors of Raier, hiding in the cellar under the town hall, heard about it the next morning. They came up from the cellar and found Ga Rek sitting on her own doorstep with blood to the elbows, and they knelt in the street and named her queen. Not because she was of any bloodline. Because she had done what no king of Raier had ever done, which was win.

She held Usar for two more years against the whole army of the Dune Scourge with three subjects and a bakehouse. In the Year 203 the town fell at last. The capital of Raier, its only city, was overrun, and the crimson went up over the ashes of Ezo Zul for the second time.

Ga Rek did not die with it. She went out over the south wall in the dark, alone, and ran. She ran south, because north was Adens and east and west were the Brink and the Whitewall, and kept running until the dunes turned to grey clay and burned stubble under her feet, and she understood, as the Raiders had once understood, that she was in somebody's fields.

The Raiders did not follow. Adens had what he wanted: one north, one banner. A single runaway baker was beneath the notice of an army. They took the town and turned for Khar Zul.

But the three who had knelt to her stayed in Usar. They did not run, and they did not kneel to Adens. They were all that was left of Raier, and they meant to make sure it was not forgotten.

Chapter Seventeen: Those Who Would Rather Die

"Write that we chose it. That is all we ask. Write that no one made us."

(Last words of Raier, as remembered by a Raider)

In the Year 208 the north lost two crowns in five months.

In Gaz Zul, King Az Geze died, the farmer-king's son, who had made a kingdom out of a granary. His daughter Ato was twenty-two. She was tall and quick and she had been raised among the Brutes, and the first thing she did as queen was to have the Brutes put into harness of hardened leather like the Raiders', and drill them every morning in the stubble where they could be seen from the road. She had watched what Adens did to Raier. She did not intend it to be done to Nepokuh.

And in Khar Zul, five months later, King Adens died, the eighth of the Royalty, who had made the north one thing again for a little while. His crown went to his daughter Heil. She was seventeen years old.

The last of Raier heard the news in Usar, where they had lived for five years under the crimson, bowed and silent, tilling the hollows for their conquerors. A girl in Khar Zul. A girl who had never seen Raier and would never think of it. That was the moment they chose. As the column of Raiders that had held Usar since the fall formed up to march back north for the new queen's crowning, the last people of Raier came out of their houses with sickles and flails, and attacked them.

They were slaughtered to the last one. It took less than an hour. The Raiders who did it did not speak of it afterwards, except once, to a dwarf, long after, when it no longer mattered. They said that the people of Raier had not fought to win. They had fought so that the last thing in the history of their kingdom would be a thing they had chosen.

Raier was gone. It was the second nation in the world to be destroyed, and the first that destroyed itself.

Chapter Eighteen: The Broken Flank

"Strike first, my father said. Strike first, and strike where they are. He did not say: make sure they are where you think."

(Kor of the Cinder Forge)

Seven years later, in the Year 215, Queen Ato of Nepokuh declared war on the Cinder Forge.

She had reasons. The orcs of Gaz Zul were farmers, and there were more of them every year, and the Ochre between Gaz Zul and the Cinder Forge was bad land, and the clay fields of the dwarves were good. There were older reasons too. The Harbinger had died on dwarven ground, and a dwarf had walked away with its staff, and the orcs of the north had never forgotten that it was their hunt and their seventeen dead. And there were reasons of fear: the dwarves had sheltered the runaway queen of Raier, and Ato did not know why, and did not like it.

But mostly she declared war because Heil was young and the Dune Scourge was busy and the Cinder Forge was small, and Ato had thirty-nine Brutes and a great deal to prove.

King Bers of the Cinder Forge was fifty-nine, and he had been raised by the Regent Kerrim on stories of the gourd war, and on one lesson above all others: that his father and grandfather had both died because the dwarves had waited in Khazad Krum for the enemy to come to them. Bers would not wait. He took the Forge out of Silta, twenty soldiers and every dwarf who could hold a bow, and marched north to meet the Brutes in the open desert before they could reach the dwarven fields. He would strike first, on the left, where his scouts said the Brutes were coming.

The Brutes were not coming on the left. Some of them were. Ato had sent a screen out on the left, a small one, enough to look like an army in the dust at a distance. The rest she had swung wide around to the right, out of sight behind the dunes, and sent straight south at a run toward the one target that mattered. Khazad Krum.

Bers struck the screen on the left in the Year 215 and beat it, and found it was only a screen, and turned his dwarves for home, and found the dust of the Brutes already far ahead of him to the south and east. He spent the next year trying to catch them.

He was killed in action in the Year 216, in the stubble north of Krum, leading a charge against the Brutes' rear guard. The crown went to his twin, Kor.

Chapter Nineteen: The Year of Four Crowns

"Bers, Kor, Ror, Asril. Write them all down. They will not have much else."

(Kerrim, in the Year 218)

It was in that first terrible year that Von Rrim killed Ga Rek.

She had lived in the dwarven lands for thirteen years by then. The dwarves of the Cinder Forge had found her in the fields the night she fled Usar, half dead with thirst, and had not known what to do with her. She was an orc. She was the enemy of their enemy. She did not speak their language and she did not try to. Kerrim, who was old by then and who had a regent's habit of not deciding things until he had to, had let her stay, and she had lived quietly in a hut at the edge of the Silta fields, baking bread on hot stones as she had in Usar, and some of the dwarf children used to go and watch her do it. They said she was kind, in a gruff way. They said she made a very good loaf.

When the Brutes came south she went out into the fields to look, as anyone would.

Von Rrim found her there, among the burning stubble, between the dwarves and the orcs. A tall, grey-green figure with a knife in her hand. He did not know her. Perhaps he had never troubled to know her. He saw an orc, and orcs were the enemy, and he had learned long ago, on the day he killed the Harbinger, not to wait for anything more than that.

So died the queen of Raier, who had killed three Raiders with a bread-knife. The children of Silta went to her hut the next morning and found the oven still warm.

Kor of the Cinder Forge died in the Year 217, killed by the Brutes in the fields south of Krum. The crown went to his daughter Ror. She was killed before anyone could put it on her head, in the burning of the farm where her family had sheltered. It passed to Kor's son Asril, who was barely grown.

The chroniclers of the dwarves called it a duel. It was probably not. It was more likely what happened at the edge of the field with the Harbinger: the dwarf who was very quick, and the thing he had decided was the enemy, and a single blow. But this time there was fire in it. Every dwarf who was there swore that the stone on the Harbinger's staff blazed up like a sunrise when Von Rrim swung, and that Queen Ato burned before the axe ever reached her.

She was the last of Zan Tor's line. The Brutes had been so efficient, so thorough in their killing, that there were no dwarven heirs left either, nearly. But there was no orc heir in Nepokuh at all. Not one. The crown of Gaz Zul had no one left to fall to.

The Brutes did not know their queen was dead. They were far to the south and west, at the walls of Khazad Krum. In the Year 218, King Asril of the Cinder Forge was killed defending those walls. And the Brutes, still believing they fought for a living queen, took the city, the city of the first hearth, and raised the banner of Nepokuh over it. Of the thirty-nine Brutes who had marched south, two were still alive.

Chapter Twenty: The Soldier-King

"He took their crown and ended their war. Some say that was mercy. Others ask: whose war was it, and whose crown?"

(From the Scribe's ledger)

What happened next is the strangest thing in all the chronicles of the Crucible, and the Scribe wrote it down three times to be sure.

Nepokuh had ten orcs left. Two of them were Brutes, sitting in the ruins of Khazad Krum, holding the conquered capital of their enemy. The rest were in Gaz Zul, far to the north, without a ruler, without an heir, without the least idea what to do.

Von Rrim walked into Gaz Zul alone, with the Harbinger's staff on his shoulder, and sat down in Queen Ato's chair. And the orcs of Nepokuh let him.

No one ever learned why. Perhaps it was the staff. Perhaps it was simply that a people without a crown in the north had always, always fallen apart, and here was someone willing to wear one. Perhaps it was something older: the memory of the twenty-seven Raiders, sixty years before, watching a young dwarf walk away across a dead field with the Harbinger's staff, and lifting a spear to him in salute. However it was, the slayer of their queen became their king. He was ninety-four years old.

In the south, in that same year, with every last drop of the royal blood of Thane Varis spilled, the dwarves came to Kerrim in Silta. He was very old now. He had raised two kings and buried both. They asked him to be the third. He did not want to. He accepted, and the Regent became King Kerrim of the Cinder Forge, ruler of a nation that had shrunk once more to a single town in the western sand.

And the first act of Von Rrim, King of Nepokuh, was to declare peace.

By the Year 220, the war was over. The Cinder Forge had thirty-one dwarves left, all in Silta. The Forge, the soldiers Kerrim had named for broken things made whole, had been destroyed to the last dwarf. Nepokuh had twelve: eight orcs, and four dwarves who had come north with Von Rrim and whom nobody quite knew what to call. Two of the orcs were Brutes. Khazad Krum, the oldest city of the dwarves, flew the banner of an orc nation ruled by a dwarf.

The Scribe looked at the ledger for a long time.

"He killed their queen," said the Scribe. "And took her throne. And then he stopped a war his own people were losing. Was that for the dwarves, or for himself, or for something else?"

"Look at the staff," said the Hand.

The Scribe looked. The stone was brighter than it had been.

Chapter Twenty-One: The Conquest of Freedom

"The crimson does not ask whether you are free. It only asks whether you are crimson."

(Saying of Khar Zul under Queen Heil)

Queen Heil of the Dune Scourge was thirty-three years old in the Year 224, and she had spent sixteen years watching.

She had watched Nepokuh fight the dwarves and bleed itself white. She had watched it end with a dwarf on its throne and two Brutes left in its army. She had watched the Cinder Forge shrink to a village. And she had watched her own Raiders grow, year on year, from the hundred and twenty-six her father had left her to a hundred and fifty-seven.

In the Year 224 she called them out, and named the campaign before it had begun. She called it the Conquest of Freedom. Freedom, she said, from the breakaway crowns. Freedom from upstarts and farmers and dwarves on orc thrones. Freedom for the north to be the north again.

The Raiders took Gaz Zul easily. A hundred and fifty-seven spears against a town with two Brutes in it. In the Year 225, Von Rrim the Soldier-King went out over the wall of the city he ruled, alone, with his staff, and left it to fall. He did not fight. He did not burn anyone. He simply walked away, south, toward Silta and the Cinder Forge.

The rest of Nepokuh went south too, to the last place in the world that flew their banner: Khazad Krum. A dwarf among them, one of the four who had followed Von Rrim north, named Queek Ekabel, was crowned queen there in the ashes of the old hall, because there was no one else and a crown in the north had to be worn. She ruled for some weeks. Then the Raiders came south down the old road and over the walls of Khazad Krum, and Queek Ekabel was killed on the steps of the first hearth, and the city fell.

By the end of the Year 226 the Conquest of Freedom was over, and Nepokuh was erased. Every town in the north flew the crimson. Heil had done what no ruler of the Dune Scourge had done since Daza a hundred years before. She had made the orcs one people.

Chapter Twenty-Two: The River Road

"We waited two hundred and twenty years to walk up a river. In the end it took four days."

(Saying of Montes)

The elves had watched it all from their lake.

They had seen the smoke of the Harbinger, and the smoke of the pumpkin war, and then the smoke of the orc wars, one after another, all along the northern and southern horizons. In the hall of the Heart of Azure they had prayed for the dead, though the dead were not elves and not theirs. And King Tua had counted, and counted, and kept the Rangers home, behind the Mirror, safe.

But the elves were growing. The Mirror could only feed so many, and Thalassia had filled to its edges, and the young were restless, and there was nowhere to go but the one place no one had gone.

In the last months of the Year 229, a party of Rangers and settlers took the old transport boats, the broad deep craft of Year 57 that had carried nets for two centuries, and rowed them up the river.

They went in through the gorge where the Vein came out of the Whitewall, between cliffs that went up white and sheer into cloud, and for four days they saw nothing but rock and spray and the narrow band of sky. On the fifth day the cliffs fell away on either side and they came out into the Heart.

The grass was as tall as an elf's shoulder. It was green, and it went on forever, rolling away under the huge ring of white mountains to every side, and the Vein ran bright through the middle of it, and nothing, nothing at all, had ever walked there.

The elves knelt on the riverbank and wept. Then, being elves, and being Tua's people, they began to count.

There was stone. Grey granite, white marble, black basalt, lying loose in the grass in boulders bigger than houses. There was iron, rust-red in the cuts of the riverbank. There was copper and tin in the rocks of the foothills. And there was mithril. A pale, light, silvery metal none of them had ever seen, running in veins through the rock of the Whitewall's lower slopes, so bright that the river seemed to shine with it.

In the Ochre, for two hundred and twenty years, the only thing anyone had ever had to build with was spinewood. Here in the Heart lay everything a people could ever need, sitting in the grass, waiting.

They built a village on the riverbank and named it Montes. And King Tua, when they told him, sat for a long while with his stone spear across his knees and the bark-strip in his hand. Then he gave orders for the forges.

Chapter Twenty-Three: The Turncoat

"Some men change sides. Von Rrim never seemed to have a side. He seemed to have an errand."

(Saying of Silta)

In the Year 232, two things happened that the Scribe wrote side by side.

The first was that Silta expanded west to the sea. Under King Kerrim, the last dwarves of the world dug wells and pits outward through the dunes until they broke through to the coast. The elves of Thirefin, down the shore, saw their fires one evening. And watching the elven boats on the water, the dwarves learned how to make their own. They built a dock. They built fishing boats. And, very quietly, in a cove the elves could not see, they built something else.

They called her the Crimson.

She was small, modelled on the elven Wing, the swift silver-winged ship that the elves used for transport along the coast. But the dwarves, who had learned to work the bitter copper and iron that the Silta pits sometimes turned up, gave her what no elven ship had ever had. Two cannons, short and black, one on either side. And at her bow, a ram of beaten iron shaped like a ram's head, with curling horns. The Crimson was finished in the Year 231, before the dwarves had a dock fit to put her at. She waited in her cove. She was the last thing the Cinder Forge ever built.

The second thing was that Von Rrim joined Queen Heil.

He had left Gaz Zul when the Raiders took it, and walked south toward Silta, and every dwarf in the Cinder Forge had waited for him to arrive. He never did. He turned aside, somewhere in the Ochre. And in the Year 232 he walked into Khar Zul, the capital of the people who had just destroyed the nation he ruled, and offered his service to the queen.

Heil took it. She was not a fool. A dwarf who had killed the Harbinger, and a queen of Nepokuh, and had sat on an orc throne and ended an orc war, was worth having, whatever he wanted. She gave him the fields of Gaz Zul to farm, the city he had once ruled, which under the crimson was growing fast now, spreading out over the old lands of Raier until it rivalled Khar Zul in size. And Von Rrim went to farm them.

What was he doing? The dwarves of Silta asked it bitterly, and the orcs of Khar Zul asked it suspiciously, and the Scribe asked it with real puzzlement. He had taken one throne to end one war. Was this the same game? Was he inside the crimson to bring it down from within? Was he serving something else entirely, something on the far end of the staff? No one knew. Von Rrim did not say. He tilled the fields of Gaz Zul and grew wheat, and kept the Harbinger's staff leaning against the end of every row he hoed.

By the Year 234, in the far west, the elves numbered five hundred. The most there had ever been. Two hundred and sixty of them were soldiers, and for the first time in their history, they wore mithril.

Chapter Twenty-Four: Fire Against the Huddle

"An orc has half the life of a dwarf in him. Nobody told the orcs. They kept coming anyway."

(Saying of the Forge)

In the ninth month of the Year 234, the Dune Scourge declared war on the Cinder Forge. Heil meant to finish what the Harbinger and the pumpkins and the Brutes had begun. She meant there to be no dwarven banner in the world.

It started badly for her. There were only three orcs holding the ruins of Khazad Krum, and the Cinder Forge had a new Forge: nine young dwarves, every one of them born after the pumpkin war, raised on the stories of Thane Varis and the first hearth. They walked north from Silta and took Khazad Krum back in a week. The first city of the dwarves flew dwarven colours again, for the first time in sixteen years.

For a year after that there was only skirmishing. Orc scouts came south and the Forge killed them on the border, one at a time. Nothing that could be called a battle.

Then in the Year 236, Heil sent the Raiders. There were a hundred and sixty of them.

The dwarves had nine soldiers, and every dwarf in Silta who could draw a bow. But they had watched the Raiders for thirty years and had learned some things. An orc in harness, carrying a spear, was slow. Slower still in the clay fields, where his boots sank. An orc, the dwarves had found in the Brute war, had only about half the life in him that a dwarf had, and went down to wounds a dwarf would walk away from. And orcs fought shoulder to shoulder, packed close, spear-points out, the way they had learned on the drill grounds of Khar Zul.

The dwarves were quick. They struck fast and moved faster. And they had bows, and they had axes and warhammers, and they had learned the one lesson the pumpkin war had beaten into them above all others.

Fire.

They wrapped their arrowheads in pitch-rag and lit them from the hearth of Khazad Krum, the first hearth, carried in pots from wall to wall, and they shot them into the packed ranks of the Raiders as they came across the fields. The orcs burned. They burned in their harness and in the dry stubble around them, and when they broke formation to run from the fire, the dwarves came in among them with axe and hammer, and when they closed ranks again against the axes, the fire-arrows came down.

A hundred and sixty Raiders came south. Eighty-seven of them died in the fields of the Cinder Forge.

But seventy-three did not. And there were only so many dwarves.

Khazad Krum fell again in the Year 237, its new walls overrun, the nine of the Forge dead on the steps of the first hearth. Silta fell the year after. King Kerrim died defending it. He was older than anyone could remember and had never wanted a crown in his life, and he died in the gate of the town he had governed, with a bow in his hands.

By the Year 239 the Dwarven Conquest, as the orcs called it, was over. The Cinder Forge was gone. There were thirty-seven dwarves left alive in the whole world, and every one of them now lived under the crimson banner of Queen Heil.

And Von Rrim, all that while, had farmed the fields of Gaz Zul, and the Raiders had marched past his rows every season going south to kill his people, and he had not lifted a hand.

Chapter Twenty-Five: Swan and Dragon

"The elves made ships to look like birds. The orcs made a ship to look like the thing birds are afraid of."

(Saying of the docks of Thalassia)

The elves had built their first warship in the Year 189, and their second the year after, and for half a century nobody in the world but the elves had known they existed.

The first was the Wing. She was small, quick and handsome, with a single sail, and on either side of her bow the shipwrights of Thalassia had carved a great swept-back wing and covered it in beaten silver from the Montes mines, so that she flashed on the water like a gull. She was built to carry Rangers along the coast to Thirefin and back, and to guard the fishing fleet, and she carried an arrow-engine on her foredeck that could loose thirty arrows at a single pull of the lever.

The second was the Crane. She was everything the Wing was not. She had one great mainsail and two smaller ones and she sat deep in the water, and her bow rose in a long, elegant curve into the head and neck of a swan, and the swan's wings, spread out along her sides, were covered in gold. On her deck stood the arrow-barge, the great engine the elves had spent ten years designing, which could hurl fifty iron-tipped arrows at once in a black, whistling cloud. The Wing was made for defence. The Crane was made for something else, and Tua, who had paid for her, had never once in fifty years let her fire in anger.

They had been made with spinewood and lake-pitch and patience. The Heart had given the elves silver and gold and iron since. But no one in Thalassia had ever thought of building a ship to fight other ships. There were no other ships.

Then the Dune Scourge had thirty-seven dwarves.

Queen Heil had kept them alive for a reason. Dwarves worked metal. Dwarves had built the Crimson, the little ram-headed ship from the Silta cove, which the Raiders had found there when Silta fell and towed into Khar Zul's new harbour. Heil looked at the Crimson and then looked west, toward the elves and their silver-winged ships, and she set her dwarves to work.

She died in the Year 246, before the thing was finished. Her daughter Righ, fifty-nine, saw it launched.

They named it the Firestorm. It was bigger than the Crane by a third, and blacker. It had two huge square sails of red sailcloth. Its bow was the head of a dragon, carved and painted, with jaws gaping. Its stern was the coiling tail of a great snake. And along its sides, three on each, were six black cannon.

It was not a ship built to guard a fishing fleet.

Chapter Twenty-Six: The Miser's Arithmetic

"I have spent my life adding. Do not mistake it for a life of peace."

(King Tua, Year 252)

In Thalassia, King Tua watched the Firestorm sail.

In the Year 250 it fired on an elven fishing boat off the northern coast, for no reason anyone could later name except that the cannon were new and the gunners wanted to see what they did. The boat went down with four elves aboard. Tua took out his ledger and subtracted four, and his hand shook. The elves ever after counted the war from that day.

He was two hundred and twenty-nine years old. He had reigned two hundred and six years, longer than all the orc kings of the north put together, and he had watched them rise and fall like grass on the plain. He had kept the elves home through the Harbinger, the gourds, the Raider wars. He had kept them home when the Cinder Forge died. He had never, in all those years, gone to war, or ever wanted to.

But Tua was a miser, and a miser is a person who can count. He took out the bark-strip from his sleeve, which was a great book now, kept by a clerk, and he set down two numbers next to each other.

The Azure Imperium: five hundred and seventy-five elves. Three hundred and fourteen Rangers. Seventeen of the Vanguard.

The Dune Scourge: three hundred and sixty-four orcs and forty-nine dwarves. A hundred and thirty-seven Raiders, and twelve of the dwarves of Silta who had been made soldiers under the crimson and called the New Forge. And the Firestorm.

"They grow," said Tua. "We grow. They build ships. We build ships. In fifty years they will have more ships than we have and they will not stop building them. The count will go down. The only question is when."

He looked at the Heart of Azure, in its hall, warm to the touch.

"Better now," said the king. "While the count is high."

In the Year 252, the Azure Imperium declared war on the Dune Scourge. It was the first war the elves had ever fought.

Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Great March

"We went inland across the Heart and then out of it, across the desert, toward a people none of us had ever seen. We were very brave. We did not know anything."

(A Ranger of Montes)

At the end of the Year 252 the Great March began.

The Rangers went up the Vein to Montes, and from Montes across the Heart, and then out of the Heart where the river gorge let them through and down along the sand toward Silta. It was a long road and the elves had never walked it. The sand was terrible to them. They were creatures of the coast and the green, and the Ochre took them by the throat, and every evening the sergeants counted the line and found it shorter.

Silta was where the war was won and lost for two years.

The New Forge held it. Twelve dwarves of the Cinder Forge, conquered, forced into crimson harness, defending a town they had once called their own against an enemy they had never fought. Whatever they felt about it, they were good. They held the gates of Silta, and killed every elf who tried to come through.

And on the water off the coast, the Firestorm came out.

The elves had never seen a cannon. The first broadside struck the shallows in front of the elven line and threw up great fountains of sand and water, and the second hit the line itself, and Rangers flew through the air like dry leaves. That was what the survivors remembered above all. Not the noise, though the noise was beyond anything. Not the smoke. The way elves flew. The way the Firestorm simply reached out from half a mile away and threw them into the sky, and they came down and did not get up.

The Wing went out to meet it, and the Crane came behind. The arrow-barge loosed its fifty iron arrows at the dragon's head and they hissed and stuck in the black timbers and did nothing. The Firestorm turned its side and fired again, and the Wing staggered and limped home with her silver wing shot away.

By the eighth month of the Year 254 the Raiders had come down to Silta from the north to reinforce the New Forge. At the same time, the last of the Great March came up to the frontlines from the Heart: three hundred and four Rangers, with the seventeen of the Vanguard from Thirefin to support them, the whole fighting strength of the Azure Imperium drawn up under the green banner before a single desert town.

It looked very much as though it would end there.

Chapter Twenty-Eight: Hil

"I asked for lightning. I asked once. I did not ask how it would be paid for."

(King Tua)

King Tua had not gone with the army. He was too old, and his place was in Thalassia, counting. When the news came from Silta of the flying Rangers and the black ship he went to the hall of the Heart of Azure, and he did something he had never done in two hundred and thirty years. He prayed.

He went down on his knees with the stone spear across them. The clerics of the hall knelt with him, and the elves who believed knelt too, crowded into the little hall and out of the door and down the dock, more and more of them all through the evening, until it seemed all of Thalassia knelt in the dusk in front of the Mirror. Tua did not pray long. He was not a man of words. He asked Hil, the God of Nature and Growth, the god that had grown up in the sap of the spinewood and in the warmth of Yage's old staff, and in two hundred years of elves sitting quietly by it, to send lightning upon his enemies.

The Scribe's quill stopped. The Hand leaned forward. Neither of them had made Hil, any more than they had made the Harbinger or the gourds. Neither of them knew what would come.

What came was lightning.

It came out of a clear sky over Silta, in the fourth month of the Year 255. Not one bolt but many, a great white stuttering tree of them, branching down into the orcish lines outside the town and into the crimson harness of the Raiders, and the air smelled of iron and burned grass, and the orcs died where they stood.

The Rangers went over the walls of Silta while the sky was still flickering, and took it. The twelve of the New Forge died at the gate, every one, still in the crimson they had never chosen. And in Silta's harbour, the elves found the Firestorm at anchor, its crew fled or dead, and beside it, the little Crimson with her ram's head.

They turned the ships around. Elven gunners who had never touched a cannon learned that night how to load one, from a captured dwarf who showed them because he was tired of the crimson, too. In the morning the Firestorm and the Crimson came out of Silta harbour under green banners and fired on the orcish lines along the coast, and blew the Raiders to pieces.

The Rangers marched to Khazad Krum, and the green banner went up over the first hearth.

In the Year 257, Queen Righ died in Khar Zul. Her son Oto took her crown. He was sixty-two, and he ruled a hundred and eighty-three orcs, and there were fifty Raiders left.

Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Firebreak

"He said he was burning out vermin. He did not say what kind."

(An elven captain at Gaz Zul)

In the Year 258, King Oto of the Dune Scourge went south to win back Khazad Krum.

He had fifty Raiders and he had courage, and that was all. The battle in the ruins of Khazad Krum was short and dreadful. When it ended the orcs were broken, and the old city, two hundred and fifty-eight years old, built round a hearth dug in the sand on the first night of the world, was rubble. There was nothing left to hold, and no one left who wished to hold it. The dwarves had no people to come home to it. The elves had no use for it. The first hearth was buried under its own fallen walls and the sand began, quietly, to fill it.

The Rangers turned north to Gaz Zul. Only twenty-one orcs stood in the front lines there. In two months the city flew green. There were a hundred and seventy-six Rangers left by then, and they kept going, north, toward Khar Zul.

And it was then, with Gaz Zul in elven hands, that Von Rrim came back into the war.

He walked out of his fields one morning with the staff of the Harbinger in his hand, the stone blazing like a forge, and he set fire to the houses along the border road between Gaz Zul and Khar Zul. Methodically. One after another, a long line of them, from the Whitewall in the west to the deep desert in the east. Orc houses, most of them empty, their people fled north to Khar Zul. He did it without hurrying. He did it the way the Harbinger had done it in Ezo Zul, a hundred and twelve years before.

The elves let him. They watched from the walls of Gaz Zul. To them he was only another fighter, a strange old dwarf with a fire-staff, and as long as he did not burn elves they did not much care what he burned. There was no one left who might have questioned him. The dwarves who had wondered, all those years, why the hero of the Cinder Forge farmed while the Raiders marched past his fields to kill his people, were gone. There was nobody left to ask.

The burned line was a mile wide when he finished. Then he went back to his fields.

Chapter Thirty: The Hellstorm

"We asked our god for fire. We did not think to ask whom it would burn."

(Inscription in the hall of the Heart of Azure)

In the Year 259, King Tua prayed again.

He had seen the lightning come. He had seen what it had done. And Khar Zul still stood, the old crimson city, the capital of Gorkha the Burned, the one city in the north that had never broken away and never fallen. The Rangers were at its walls. There were a hundred and eighty orcs inside, and fifty Raiders, and the war had already lasted nine years by the elves' counting, from the four drowned fishermen, and the count of the elves was falling, falling. Tua could not bear it any longer.

He knelt in front of the Heart of Azure with the stone spear across his knees, and this time he asked for more. He asked for fire. Not lightning. A Hellstorm: clouds that would pour down burning stone upon Khar Zul and end it.

The hall was very quiet. Some of the clerics, later, said that the Heart of Azure had grown so hot that no one could stand near it. Some said it had cracked.

Over Khar Zul the sky went red. Not the red of sunset. A churning, roiling, furious red, low and close, like the inside of a furnace, and out of it came fire. It came down in great molten gobbets the size of a cart, and where they struck, stone ran. All the city burned. The hundred and eighty orcs in it burned. Those who ran from the gates into the desert ran straight into the Rangers' arrows, and the Rangers shot them down.

And then the fire came for the Rangers.

Tua had called it. He could not command it. It spread out from Khar Zul like water from a broken dam, over the walls, out across the desert, and it fell on the elven lines as it had fallen on the orcs. It did not know the difference. It had been asked for fire, and it gave fire.

Of a hundred and seventy-six Rangers before Khar Zul, forty-five lived.

The fire stopped, at last, in the south, at a line where it had nothing left to burn. A mile-wide strip of burned-out houses that ran from the Whitewall to the deep desert, between Khar Zul and Gaz Zul. The Hellstorm reached the line, and found nothing, and died. Gaz Zul, beyond it, with its elven garrison and its wheat-fields, did not lose so much as a roof.

Nobody ever asked Von Rrim if he had known. Nobody would have known how to ask it.

Chapter Thirty-One: The Last Orc

"His name was Hil. Of course it was."

(From the Scribe's ledger)

King Oto burned in the Hellstorm, in his hall, in Khar Zul.

His crown, what there was of it in the smoking ruin, passed to Lohil, a young orc who was the very last of the Royalty, the last drop of the blood of Gorkha the Burned and Ezo, the first mother. He came out of the ashes of Khar Zul to surrender the city, carrying the crimson banner furled. A Ranger on the outer line, who did not see the banner for the smoke, shot him through the head.

There was one orc left in Khar Zul after that. He came out from under a fallen wall when the Rangers searched the ruin, grey with ash, unarmed, with his hands up. The Rangers searched the ruins for days afterward, every cellar, and found no one else. They took him to their captain and asked him his name.

His name was Hil.

The Rangers stared at him. He did not understand why. It was a common orc name, an old one, a word that meant something like wheat-stalk in the old tongue of the hollows. He could not have known that across the Ochre the elves had given that same short name to their god. Nor that his god had just burned his whole people alive.

The Rangers called him the Last Orc. The green banner went up over the ashes of Khar Zul, the city that never broke away, now burned to the ground.

It was the end of the war that the elves named the War of Broken Bones. It had lasted nine years. Eighty-eight elves had died in the fighting. Many, many more had died in the fire their own king had asked their own god to send, and the elves of Thalassia would speak of that for a long time, very quietly, in the hall of the Heart of Azure.

In the Year 262, the Scribe opened the ledger once more and wrote what was left.

The Azure Imperium. King Tua, sole king of the world. Three hundred and eighty elves. Forty-eight Rangers. Twelve of the Vanguard. And within its borders, thirteen dwarves, and one orc.

Chapter Thirty-Two: The Farmer and the King

"One of them killed a hundred and ninety-two. The other killed two. Guess which one still sleeps."

(Saying of the Heart, Year 300)

By the Year 300, the world was green.

Not all of it. The Ochre was still the Ochre, brass and blood and spinewood, running out to the Brink on every side. But the elves had come up the Vein and out across the Heart in their hundreds, and there were villages of white marble along the river now, and mithril mines in the foothills of the Whitewall, and a road with milestones from Montes to the gorge. There were six hundred elves in the world, and more than half of them were soldiers, because King Tua had learned in the War of Broken Bones what a falling count felt like, and would not feel it again.

And over every town, on the coast and on the river and in the long grass, flew the green banner. It was the colour Queen Caelum Strideer had chosen on the first day of the world, standing on the shore of the Mirror with her feet in the water, looking at a place she was not allowed to go. The colour of the thing you could see and could not have. Now the elves had it. All of it.

The Last Orc died that year. He had lived out his life in Thalassia, among people who were kind to him in the careful way that people are kind to something they have broken. He worked in the fish-market. He never learned to like fish. When he died, the elves buried him in the Heart, on a hill above the Vein, facing north toward the place where Khar Zul had been, and on the stone they carved the only word they had for him. Hil.

There were twenty-four dwarves. The elves had let them be. Under the green they were free, in a small and careful way, and they had begun to dig again, and some of them had children who had never seen a crimson banner or a pumpkin. One of them, a girl of nine, had found her way to the ruins of Khazad Krum, and dug, and come back with a blackened stone she said was the first hearth. No one could tell her she was wrong.

And in the fields of Gaz Zul, an old dwarf was farming.

Von Rrim was a hundred and seventy-six years old. He had been a soldier of the Hearthguard and the slayer of the Harbinger, the governor of Khazad Krum, the king of Nepokuh, the servant of Queen Heil. He had ended one war and very possibly another. By the reckoning of the Scribe, who had kept a careful tally, a hundred and ninety-two living souls had died by his hand: orc and dwarf and gourd and one baker queen. Now he tilled the wheat of Gaz Zul the same way he had tilled it in the years when the Raiders marched past him on their way south, slowly, carefully, row by row.

He never cut a plant he did not need. He never threw a hollow husk on a midden. At the end of every row he hoed, propped against the fence, stood a staff of grey metal with a stone at its head that glowed, very faintly, like the last coal of a fire. The elves who passed on the road gave him a wide berth. Nobody knew if he was finished. Nobody had the courage to ask.

In Thalassia, in the counting-house by the water, King Tua sat at his table with the great ledger open in front of him. He was two hundred and seventy-seven years old. He had reigned two hundred and fifty-four years. In all that time he had killed exactly two people with his own hands, two cousins in the dark, and he had never killed another. In his right hand he still held the same spear of black lake-flint that he had picked up on that night two hundred and twenty-three years ago. He had asked his god to burn a city, and his god had burned it, and his own soldiers with it. He had never touched the spear to anything since.

Six hundred, the ledger said. He looked at it for a long time. Then he took up his pen and wrote it again, beneath, carefully, as though to make sure it would stay.

Chapter Thirty-Three: What the Watchers Said

"We made three peoples. Three more things came, which we did not make. In the end, those were the story."

(From the Scribe's ledger, last page)

High above, in the place that is not a place, the Scribe closed the ledger.

"Three peoples," said the Scribe. "One left. Well, one and a half."

"One and a farmer," said the Hand.

The Scribe was quiet for a while. Then: "May I ask you something? The Harbinger. The pumpkins. Hil. None of them were ours. I have looked through every page. You did not place them."

"No."

"Then where did they come from?"

The Hand thought about it.

"I think," it said slowly, "that a world is like a field. You sow three peoples in it, and they grow, and they eat, and they cut and burn and scrape out what they need and throw away the husks. And the field notices. It does not love them or hate them. It just keeps the account. It sends a fire when there has been too much fire. A harvest that bites when there has been too much harvest. A god, when there has been enough asking."

"And the staff?"

"The staff is the field's own tool. Something left it lying in the stubble on the day the Harbinger died. Something that wanted to see who would pick it up. A dwarf did, and he has been carrying it ever since." The Hand looked down at the fields of Gaz Zul, at the old dwarf moving slowly along a row. "Look at how he farms. Do you see? He worked it out. He knows what's on the other end of that stone. He felt it the first day. And he has spent a hundred and fifty years trying to keep it fed and keep it quiet: burning only what had to burn, killing only what had to die, taking a throne to end a war when the war got too hot, burning a firebreak when he knew a fire was coming. And growing wheat. Growing wheat, very carefully, in a field that remembers."

"You do not know that."

"No," said the Hand. "But neither do you."

The Scribe looked down at the green world, and the white mountains, and the brass desert, and the little old king in Thalassia writing a number twice.

"Is that the end, then?"

"It's where the chronicle ends," said the Hand. "It isn't the end."

And with that, the Hand let go of the Reins entirely, and the Scribe put down the quill.

APPENDIXThe Codex of the CrucibleLore, peoples and years

The Land

The Ochre. The desert that covers almost all of the world. Brass-coloured, waterless except at rare seeps, growing nothing but spinewood. All three peoples were born in it.

The Brink. The edge of the world, beyond the Ochre to the north, east and south. The sand slides off into nothing there. No one has ever described what lies beyond it.

The Whitewall. The great ring of snow-capped mountains and frozen foothills in the centre of the Ochre. It cannot be climbed or crossed.

The Heart. The green country inside the Whitewall: long grass, the river Vein, and rich in stone, iron, copper, tin, silver, gold and mithril. Forbidden to all three peoples at their founding. First settled by the elves at Montes in the Year 229.

The Vein. The river that runs through the Heart and leaves the Whitewall by a single gorge, the only way in or out.

The Mirror. The great lake where the Vein spills out into the desert, beside which the elves founded Thalassia.

Spinewood. A grey, thorned, cactus-like tree, the only timber of the Ochre. Every house, boat and spear-shaft built in the first two centuries was spinewood.

The Powers

The Hand. The Watcher who made the map, placed the three peoples and held the Reins.

The Scribe. The Watcher who chose the peoples' names and kept the ledger of their years. Fond of grand words and quick to write a thing down before it was quite true.

The Cosmic Hold. The law the Hand laid over the world: no war and no alliance between peoples. Meant to last one hundred and fifty years. Kept until the Year 200 to give the dwarves a chance after the gourd war.

The Harbinger. A figure in a red robe with a golden circlet, carrying a staff of grey metal tipped with a glowing ember stone. Appeared at Ezo Zul in the Year 146 and destroyed the kingdom of Zeteurg. Slain by Von Rrim in the Year 148, turning to dust. Not placed by either Watcher.

The Staff of the Harbinger. Taken by Von Rrim from the Harbinger's dust. Its ember stone stays warm and glows brighter at certain times. Those who have held it say it feels as though something on the far end is listening. Often simply called the Harbinger.

The Gourds. Eighty-two pumpkins of the fields of Khazad Krum that rose as monsters at the end of the Year 153. They killed King Thane Varis, King Esotar and the entire Hearthguard, and were burned at last in the Year 158.

Hil. The God of Nature and Growth, worshipped by the elves in the hall of the Heart of Azure at Thalassia. Hil answered King Tua twice: with lightning at Silta in the Year 255, and with the Hellstorm at Khar Zul in the Year 259, which also burned the elven army.

The Heart of Azure. The staff King Yage made from spinewood. After his death King Tua placed it in a small hall by the Mirror, where the worship of Hil began.

The Peoples and their Nations

The Azure Imperium. Elves. Founded by Queen Caelum Strideer at Thalassia on the western coast. Cities: Thalassia, Thirefin (Year 62), Montes (Year 229). Soldiers: the Rangers, and the Vanguard of Thirefin. Banner: green. Ruled under the Unbroken Chain, by which the throne passes undivided to the strongest clan when no heir remains. Rulers: Caelum Strideer, Yage, Tua.

The Dune Scourge. Orcs. Founded by Gorkha the Burned at Khar Zul in the far north. Soldiers: the Raiders. Banner: crimson. Ruled by the Royalty, the blood of Gorkha and Ezo. Rulers: Gorkha, Ezo, Daza, Gozan, Gazia, Ates, Perde, Adens, Heil, Righ, Oto, Lohil. Destroyed in the Year 259.

Zeteurg. Orcs. Broke from the Dune Scourge at Ezo Zul in the Year 130. Rulers: Eto, Ake. Destroyed by the Harbinger, Year 148.

Nepokuh. Orcs. Broke from the Dune Scourge at Gaz Zul in the Year 141. Soldiers: the Brutes. Rulers: Ego, Zan Tor (the first orc ruler not of the Royalty), Az Geze, Ato, Von Rrim, Queek Ekabel. Destroyed in the Conquest of Freedom, Year 226.

Raier. Orcs. Founded by Queen Usae at Usar on the ashes of Ezo Zul in the Year 151. Rulers: Usae, Etan, Seon, Ga Rek. Destroyed Year 208, when its last people chose to fight rather than kneel.

The Cinder Forge. Dwarves. Founded by Thane Varis Iron Beard at Khazad Krum in the far south. Cities: Khazad Krum, Silta (Year 92). Soldiers: the Hearthguard, then the Forge. Ship: the Crimson. Rulers: Thane Varis, Esotar, Bers (with Kerrim as regent), Kor, Ror (never crowned), Asril, Kerrim. Destroyed Year 239.

The Ships

The Wing (Year 189). Elven. One sail, silver wings. Thirty arrows a volley. Built for transport and defence.

The Crane (Year 190). Elven. One great sail and two small. Swan's head, golden wings. The arrow-barge: fifty iron-tipped arrows a volley.

The Crimson (Year 231). Built by the dwarves of Silta after the Wing. Ram's head, two cannon. Taken by the orcs, then by the elves.

The Firestorm (Year 246). Built by enslaved dwarves for Queens Heil and Righ. Two huge sails, a dragon's head, a serpent's tail, six cannon. Taken by the elves at Silta in the Year 255.
