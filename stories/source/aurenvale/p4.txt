#B BOOK FOUR|The Mountain War|Years 110 to 148

#C Chapter Fourteen: Fire on the Spine

#E "A road is a promise that someone will come back down it."
(Saying of the Eddish carters, cut into the milestone at the northern gate of Eddale)

Roric was buried at Eddale on the ninth day after Shrikka fell, in the ground under the wall of the temple, with his sword across his chest and the spider's fang laid at his feet because nobody could think what else to do with it. Five beasts he had brought down before the sixth took him, and the hall counted them aloud, one by one, while the earth was shovelled in. That was the Eddish custom: a soldier's victories are said at his grave so that the dead can hear that the tally is right.

General Corry stood at the head of the grave. He was among the oldest soldiers in the realm now, one of the six who had walked out of nowhere in the Year 75 with their boots already dusty, and in thirty-five years he had never learned to like funerals. Forty-nine souls stood behind him in the cold. Seventeen of them wore the grey. When the shovels stopped, a girl at the back, not yet ten, asked her mother in a perfectly clear voice who would be the next one, and the mother put a hand over her mouth, and Corry pretended not to have heard.

Above the sky, and above the weather, the Watcher heard it too.

It had been thirty-five years since it had sworn the Covenant, and in all that time it had kept the terms it had made with itself. Wars were won by soldiers. Signs were for its own people only. In the Year 110 the Watcher had weighed whether a Sign was owed, a Host of grown soldiers to replace the fallen hero, and had decided that grief was not a need, and had let the year go by empty. It told itself this was discipline. Nobody was there to disagree.

"You are looking at the funeral again," said the Maker.

"I am looking at the war," said the Watcher. "The funeral is only the war's shadow. We had a hero, and he is gone, and the war he died in goes on, because I declared it, and I will declare it again."

"Will you?"

"The Dominion of Zathixal is the nearest power to the capital that I can reach," said the Watcher, "and it is the oldest friend this people ever had, and I do not know how else to begin."

The Maker, who spoke rarely and usually looked amused, did not look amused.

*

The saurians of Zathixal had been the first allies Aurenvale ever had. In the Year 1 a nervous young realm, six humans and a hall with no roof, had sent its first decree to the highlands of the north-east, to the lizard-folk who lived among the crowded peaks and dark pines, and the Dominion had said yes. Honor, they called it. *Yss zeth ikix.* A thing said is a thing owed. They had stood in front of Aurenvale when the orcs came in the Year 39, when the human realm was a handful of souls and a rumour, and they had asked for nothing afterward.

Then in the Year 103 the Watcher had turned on them.

The herald who spoke the Watcher's words in the hall in those years was a tall, stooped man called Jomund, who had been the eldest child in the empty years and still, at nearly fifty, flinched slightly when he heard thunder. He read the decree every third year from the steps of the great hall, in the same level voice, and the people listened, and when he came to the words *war upon the Dominion of Zathixal* there was no cheer and no groan. They had heard those words before. By the Year 112 they had heard them three times.

In the Year 112 the army marched north in autumn, General Corry at its head, and the pass under the white shoulder of the Spine was burning before the first snow. Villages burned. Crops burned. A saurian watchtower, built out of the grey stone of the mountain by people who had learned from the same teachers as the dwarves, burned with a lamp still lit in its top window, and the soldiers who passed it said afterward that the lamp had burned a long time, steadier than anything else on the hill. Luriel's elves joined that year on Aurenvale's side, and for three years the passes smoked.

The war ended as these wars ended. No saurian army was broken. No city was taken. The soldiers came home with sacks of smoked fish and a few pieces of looted armour, and a peace was written in a hall where nobody cared to meet anyone's eye. Three years later the herald stood on the steps and read the same words again.

*

In the Year 113 Kraith died.

He was the Dominion's hero, a lean, grey-scaled saurian with a scar through one eyelid, and he had won five fights against the beasts of the high country, and every Aurenvale soldier who served in the north had heard his name. When the news came that he had fallen at the end of those five victories, the soldiers took off their helmets. They did not say why. Corry, who was an old man by then, said only: "That was a better soldier than most of ours."

Nobody answered.

Among the soldiers who watched him walk away was a young man named Annmund, nineteen years old and six feet of elbow, who would one day name a grandson for himself, and the grandson would be the greatest general Aurenvale ever had.

*

The great realms had begun to come apart, and nobody in Aurenvale noticed.

In the Year 117 the orc city of Kragdak, a Bolthul colony founded by settlers who had sailed to new shores eighty years before and forgotten every reason to go back, rose against its mother. It took a new god, Zhulka the Mother of Ash, and burned the old banner in its own square. In the Year 118 the elves of Caelmir turned on Luriel, one of the oldest and most graceful of the realms, and prayed to a spirit named Aelthar. Luriel's sages wrote it down in the old tongue as *the first crack in the song*. In the same year the gnomes of Dobwhistle, who for a hundred years had cared about nothing but gems and grudges, put on their grey cloaks and took the Dominion's side against Aurenvale, perhaps because they had decided to like the underdog, perhaps because they had never forgiven anyone anything.

It was a very strange war. The Dominion of Zathixal was dying, and everyone was running to help it.

In the Year 118 the army marched again. The Watcher's decree had said *war upon the Dominion of Zathixal* for the fifth time, and for the fifth time the soldiers went north, and this time Dobwhistle's gnomes were waiting in the pines with engines of a craft that Aurenvale's workshops had barely begun to imagine, because Aurenvale entered the Industrial Age only that same year. The factories of Eddale, new and loud and black with coal, had been running for eleven months. Their whistles were the first sound the saurian scouts heard on the wind, and the scouts said to each other, in the sibilant hush of their tongue, that the humans were bringing a new noise to the mountains.

In the same year, back at Eddale, General Corry struck the last blow on the Pale Eye. It had been a basilisk of the middle lands for more than half a century, and the Pale Eye had been ended in the Year 65, and in the Year 93, and in the Year 100, by various hands. Each time it came back. The soldiers said a name was a crown that the monsters passed on. This time the old general, aged and tired and nearly the last of his Host still in uniform, took a pike from the wall of the armoury and walked out to the meadow with six younger men, and none of them came back unmarked, and the beast, when it fell, fell across a grove of lemon trees. The scent of the crushed fruit hung in the barracks for a week.

Corry, who had nothing left to prove, lay down with a fever. His mind was clear the whole time.

*

"You will not give them a Sign," said the Maker.

"I did not say that."

"You have not said anything for three days." The Maker was sitting, if a power can sit, on the edge of the same cold nothing where the Watcher stood. "I am not telling you what to do. I am saying that Corry is ill, and the war is five declarations old, and Aurenvale has seventy souls and not a one of them has stopped to ask what it is doing."

"They do not need to ask. I ask for them."

"Do you?"

The Watcher did not answer at once. Below, the people of Eddale went about their evening: the factory whistles, the long hum of the lamps coming on in a street that had been mud when the realm began, children arguing on the temple steps about whose turn it was to hold the ball.

"I am asking now," it said at last. "What is the war for? I think it is for the Terms. If I am to be the last realm standing, then I must have fewer realms standing. I must begin somewhere. The Dominion is near and old and holds the passes. It is the right beginning."

"And in eight years it has gone from thirty to three?"

"It is not yet three."

"It will be," said the Maker. "In two years. I have the board in front of me." And the Maker, who did not say it unkindly, said: "You have done everything you set out to do, Watcher. Look at it."

The Watcher looked. The mountain valleys under the white peaks were burned black in long stripes, like the work of some enormous comb. Four of the Dominion's villages were ash. The fifth stood and was nearly empty, and behind its walls a handful of saurians, one very old and two very young, sat in the dark around a lamp, and did not speak.

"Three," said the Watcher, when it had counted.

"Yes."

"They have not surrendered."

"No," said the Maker. "They are the only ones who have not."

*

The Year 120 began with a death and with a Sign. Corry died in the winter, quietly, in the barracks, with Annmund holding the cup of water for him and a clerk of the hall writing down the tally of his victories. Four were beasts. One was a war. Corry would not let the clerk count the war.

And on the first morning of spring, in the meadow outside Eddale, under a sky that was suddenly full of birds, six grown soldiers were standing in a loose ring on the grass with their packs on their backs and the look of people who have just stepped out of a road. Nobody had seen them come. The Third Host had arrived, and a woman among them, who had a captain's bearing and a burn scar on her wrist, asked in a loud voice what year it was, and when they told her she said "Ah," and sat down on her pack.

Jomund the herald walked out to meet them. He was past fifty now, and he had been a child in the empty years, and he knew exactly what it felt like to be alone in a country that has run out of grown people. He took off his hat.

"Welcome to Aurenvale," he said. "We are very glad to have you. We are at war."

"Of course you are," said the woman, and her voice, tired and not unkind, was the voice of someone who had been told about it in advance. "Who with?"

"The saurians of Zathixal," said Jomund, and for the first time in many years he heard the words come out of his mouth like something heavy that he was only now picking up.

The Dominion of Zathixal had thirty souls when the first war was declared upon it in the Year 103. It had three when the Third Host stepped out of the air onto the grass at Eddale. Aurenvale had seventy-eight, and the Modern Age was dawning over the roofs of the capital in a shimmer of wire and glass, and Henwin, a farmer's son who had never been afraid of a fight, was made general in Corry's place. The Watcher, in the cold above, looked down at its own work and did not know what to feel.

#C Chapter Fifteen: The Five Hamlets

#E "Three of us. A lamp. A map. We began again."
(Spoken by the hatch-warden of Zathixal to the first child born after the burning)

There was a hatchery in the mountain, in the ruins of the fifth village, and by the Year 120 the whole Dominion of Zathixal fitted inside it.

It was a long low chamber cut into the south face of a granite shoulder, warm in every season from a vein of water that ran hot under the stone. The saurians of the old Dominion had laid their eggs there for generations. The eggs were laid in sand, and the sand was kept at the temperature of a summer afternoon, and when the shells split, the hatchlings found their way by instinct to the warm wall and lay against it with their eyes closed. The hatch-warden of Zathixal, who was nearly fifty winters old and had once been a magistrate, sat by the warm wall in the spring of the Year 120 and watched the last of the old clutches go cold.

There were two other saurians in the Dominion. One was an apprentice-scribe barely out of the shell, who could read three scripts and kill no one. The other was an old soldier with a lame tail, who had been Kraith's second at the end and who now wanted only to sit in the sun. They did not have a government. They did not have a council. The Church of Veritas, Lady of Law, had always taught that law was the one thing that could not be burned, and so the hatch-warden had begun, on the first night, to write the law down: the three of them, in a stone room, with a candle. Article one. *We are the Dominion.*

"We are three," said the scribe.

"That is not a legal objection," said the warden.

It was the first thing resembling a joke that anyone had said in the Dominion in two years. The old soldier laughed until he coughed. In the morning the warden went up to the ridge to look at the south, where the humans' smoke was a faint blue pencil line above the pines, and understood a thing she had been refusing to understand. Aurenvale was not going to come and finish it. The humans were not trying to kill the saurians to the last. They were only trying to win a war, and a war with a little enemy to burn was a war that could be declared again.

So the Dominion was safe, in the way a candle is safe in a room where the door is open and everyone has agreed not to breathe.

*

In the Year 121, in the autumn, the Watcher declared the sixth war. In the Year 122, on General Henwin's orders, a column marched instead on the spider Shrikka, returned to the birchwoods north of Eddale in the same body or another.

Henwin lost eleven men in the first hour. In the second hour a young soldier whom nobody had marked, a lanky man with big hands and an untidy salute, ran in under the beast's belly with a spear and put it into the soft place beneath the jaw, and held it there while the thing screamed.

That was Annmund. He was twenty-eight. He would live to hold a grandson and give the boy his name, and in fifty-four years' time that namesake would be general, after almost everyone else had died. Annmund did not say anything about it afterward. He sat on a fallen log with his hands hanging between his knees, and a sergeant brought him a cup of something strong, and he put it down on the ground without drinking.

*

The decree of the Year 124 said war. So did the decree of the Year 127. Between them the realm's people lived in a time that was, for most of them, simply a life. Children were born. Houses went up in Brookham and Stanfield and in the new brick streets on the edge of Eddale. The temple of Aurelan, consecrated in the Year 49, acquired a bell, and then a better bell, and the bell rang for births and for burials and, twice a year, for the sending-off of the army.

In the Year 125, Annbert, a soldier of the Third Host, struck down Viperscale.

That should have been the day the realm sang. Viperscale, the great basilisk of the middle lands, had broken the army in the Year 31 and turned back the march of the Year 58, and had been cut down in the Year 62 only to be heard of again. Annbert caught it at dawn in a ravine south of Eddale and killed it with a lance, and when it died the soldiers reported that its eyes, which had killed more of their grandparents than the Weaver ever did, simply went dull, like the eyes of any fish on any slab. Annbert came home on a cart with his arm in a sling. Two years later he was general.

The same year Zathixal allied with the elves of Serthas. It was a tidy piece of news that only Luriel's scholars read as it deserved: that the saurians, scarcely more than a handful, had found one friend, and that the friend was a city that hated its own mother. In the Year 127, Serthas joined Zathixal in the war against Aurenvale. The orcs of Vornak rose against Bolthul. The goblins of Grubheap rose against Nobmuck. Across the continent the world was coming apart like wet paper.

*

In the Year 126 the Dominion's apprentice-scribe, who was now a grown saurian with a thin patient voice and a gift for keeping the old soldier alive, went down to the hatchery one night with a lantern and found, against the warm wall, a single egg that had not been there at sundown.

She did not tell anyone for three days. She sat beside it. She turned the lantern low. On the fourth day the shell cracked along its seam and a damp, furious hatchling the colour of old copper tumbled out into the sand, and lay against the warm stone, and opened its eyes.

"Warden," she said, very quietly, to the empty chamber, because the warden had died in the winter. "We are four."

The hatchling blinked. It had no name. In the Dominion, children were named in the first month by the magistrate, from the list of the dead. There was a long list. She chose the name of the fifth valley, Raszzizzar, because it was the one the humans had burned last, and because it was the hardest to say.

Four, then five. A second clutch came the next spring. A third came the spring after. The old soldier, who had forgotten how to walk without a stick, found that he could once again hold a hatchling on his lap, and sat that way all through the summer with his tail in the sun. They did not have enough adults. They did not have enough food. In the first year three of the new children died of cold, and in the second the scribe wore herself out carrying water uphill, and in the third she was too tired to climb at all and said to the others, as if reporting a fact of weather, "This is the last winter I will see."

She did not see it. But within a few springs, by the testimony of every elder who ever sat in that room, there were more of them than there had been, and they had gone down out of the hatchery and begun, hamlet by hamlet, to light the old lamps.

*

The war of the Year 127 ended the following year, and the general who had led it, Annbert, never saw its end.

He had been given a hard hand: a war in the north he had not chosen, and a hero's name he had chosen too eagerly. After Viperscale he had wanted another. The basilisk returned in the Year 128, in a new body, in the same ravine, and Annbert marched on it with sixty soldiers and five victories on his belt, and the ravine took him. It was nothing a historian would call a battle. It was a lance, a flash of grey, a long still afternoon with nine men holding one man's body. Wilold became general that same year, a short grey-eyed man with no taste for glory, whose name the realm would learn to say the way one says the name of a good roof.

And in the Year 129, in the same ravine, a different man took a different lance.

Isbert was a veteran of Stanfield, born in the village in its first decade and thirty years old that year, with a broad scarred face and an unfashionable moustache. He had marched on the saurians three times and on the orcs once, and he had come home from every campaign with the same expression, a squint, as if the world were a long way off and he were still deciding what it was for. He had been the one to carry Annbert's body out of the ravine. Over the following winter he said very little to anyone. And when the beast returned the next summer, crawling out of its old burrow with its pale eyes uncovered, Isbert went up the slope with the soldiers behind him, and went first.

Nobody saw the blow itself. Three soldiers heard the shout. They said afterward that it did not sound like the cry of a man in battle, only like a man who had made up his mind about something. When they reached the top, Isbert was standing over the dead basilisk with his lance planted through its throat, and his hands were shaking, and he was looking at the sky.

"That's for the general," he said.

He was a hero by sundown. The hall of Eddale gave him a ribbon of the old blue, the first in thirty years, and he accepted it with the squint, and carried it home to Stanfield in a pocket close to his heart, and for fifteen years did not take it out.

*

The Fourth Host came in the Year 130, six more grown soldiers on the grass of the meadow, and the realm took them in without ceremony; it had learned how. The ninth declaration of war on Zathixal went down to the hall that summer, and the herald's voice did not change. By the Year 140 the Dominion would number nine, and the Watcher, when the Maker asked it what its plan was, would say only that it did not remember.

#C Chapter Sixteen: High Tide

#E "A harvest is only a famine that has not yet heard about itself."
(Eddish proverb, much quoted after the Year 144)

At its peak, Aurenvale held one hundred and thirty-five souls.

That was the number the clerks of the hall wrote in the great ledger in the spring of the Year 140, and then underlined, and then, because it seemed insufficient, wrote again in the margin in red. It was the highest count in the realm's history, nearly three times the number it had held thirty years before. The census-takers walked through Eddale and Brookham and Stanfield with their clipboards and their hats, and everywhere they went, they found the same thing: children.

It was a good year. The harvest came in thick. The air in Eddale smelled of bread and coal. And in the summer, on a morning of astonishing blue, the Fifth Host appeared in the meadow outside the capital, six grown soldiers with perfect posture and dust on their boots, who looked up at the sky, then down at the town, then at one another, and who were already, by noon, arguing about the proper form of the salute.

The herald of that time was an elderly woman named Elora, who had been a child in the empty years and had inherited the office from a Jomund too stiff to climb the steps. She met the Host with a quill behind her ear. "Welcome," she said. "Forgive us. We are counting."

*

The giants fell apart from within. This was the thing the Watcher had not planned for, and the thing the Maker found most diverting, in its dry way: that the world's greatest realms were not conquered. They split, like old planks, along the grain.

In the Year 135 the gnomes of Nimnook rose against Dobwhistle, the burrow that had sheltered them for two centuries, and they took a new god, The Deep Ancestors, and drove the old priests out into the snow. In the Year 136 Dwaldun's dwarves, forgetting for a moment their ancient hatred of everyone, struck at Zathixal, because everyone seemed to be striking at Zathixal that year, and Edwin of the First Host, an old man now with white hair and a limp, led a company of Aurenvale's veterans into the woods north of Eddale and struck the last blow on Ghorr, the basilisk.

In the Year 139 the goblins of Nobstink rose against Nobmuck, in the red-earth country of the west, and took the same fire-goddess as Kragdak. In Luriel, in the old sweet orchards of the east, the elves of Caelmir had been brought back to the Circle by missionaries, but the Circle was a thinner thing than it had been. The saurians of Zathixal, fed by the confusion of every one of those uprisings, took a new hold on the high country and a quiet pride. By the Year 140 they numbered nine. They had five villages again, five little villages, each with a lamp in a window and a flag the colour of old copper.

*

In the village of Stanfield, a retired soldier named Isbert sat at the end of his garden in the long evenings with his boots off and a pipe, looking at the road.

He was forty-one now. He had a wife, a quiet person with strong forearms and a habit of ending his sentences for him, and two daughters, the elder lately married and moved to the other side of the village. His moustache had begun to go grey. He had done what he had been asked. He had carried Annbert's body out of the ravine. He had killed the thing in the ravine, and he had come home, and he had kept the ribbon in his pocket for eleven years.

"You're thinking again," said his wife.

"I'm not."

"Your pipe went out."

He relit it. The road down from Eddale was a white thread in the dusk, full of carts, and every cart on it was full of something: grain, coal, soldiers going north, conscripts going north, boys with their mothers' bread tied up in handkerchiefs. In the Year 91 the Watcher had founded Stanfield with a decree and a purse of silver, and Stanfield had done everything the decree asked of it. It had planted. It had bred. It had sent its sons. And in all that time, in all fifty years of its existence, Isbert reflected, no decree had ever once been written *for* Stanfield. They had all been written for Eddale, and Stanfield was where the carts came from.

"There was a letter," said his wife.

"What letter?"

"From the herald's office. They want twelve more for the spring levy."

Isbert took the pipe out of his mouth. "Twelve."

"From Stanfield. Out of the young men."

"We don't have that many young men."

"They count the boys in the school," said his wife.

He said nothing. He knew what the boys in the school were for. In the year of the great ledger, of the underlined number, nobody in Eddale had seemed to realise that the villages were sending more than they were getting, that the hundred and thirty-five had been counted in one place and spent in another. The young men of Stanfield were marching out every third year to burn the saurians, and a fraction of them were coming home. Isbert had said as much, once, to the herald's clerk, and the clerk had written it down with the same neat hand he used for the price of oats, and nothing had come of it.

"I think," Isbert said slowly, "that they've forgotten we're people."

"They haven't forgotten," said his wife. "They've just stopped needing to remember."

He thought about that all night. In the morning he took the ribbon out of the pocket, and looked at it, and put it back.

*

It was in the year of the high tide that the first stranger came to Stanfield with a story.

She came on foot in the autumn, a woman of middle age with a plain brown coat and a battered case of books, and she asked at the inn for a room and a place to speak. She was not a priest, she said, but she had been taught by priests, in a place far to the south, in a village long since fallen into ruin. She was a follower of Caspian. Caspian, she said, was not an old god. He was an old name for a new idea. She carried it in a book, bound in grey leather, that nobody in Stanfield could read the first time.

"What does it say?" asked the innkeeper.

"It says," said the stranger, "that there is a god of ambition and glory whom men call the Rising Crown. He is not the Lord of Order, who gives to those who serve. He is the god of those who stand up. He asks nothing of you but that you have the courage to ask for more."

"We don't have crowns," said the innkeeper, in some confusion. "This isn't a place with crowns."

"No," said the stranger. "That is the point. It is a crown of the spirit. A crown on every head that dares to lift it."

The innkeeper looked at her for a while. Then he poured her a drink, and said that she could speak in the barn on Thursday if she liked, but that Isbert would be there, and Isbert did not hold with preachers.

On Thursday Isbert came, and sat at the back with his arms folded, and listened with the squint. When she had finished, he said only: "I've nothing against your god."

"I'm glad."

"But I'll tell you something. I've served the Lord of Order for twenty years. He never lied to me once." Isbert's voice was quite gentle. "He never *asked* me anything either."

"Maybe," said the woman softly, "that is what you have been missing."

#C Chapter Seventeen: The Rising Crown

#E "We did not rise against the sky. We rose against the road that led down from it."
(Said to be the last sentence written by a priest of Stanfield)

In the Year 142 the Watcher declared its thirteenth war on Zathixal, and the Maker, who had been counting, said nothing at all.

Two other things happened that year that the hall of Eddale did not think were connected. The dwarves of Torzul, who had been a quiet northern hold for seventy years, sent an envoy to the Dominion of Zathixal and joined its side. And in the high, clean country of the east the elves of Luriel entered the Future Age, a realm of glass and silent lights and engines that hummed, and the saurians of the Dominion, who had five lamps and a legal code, looked at the east and then at the south and drew from this a sensible inference, which was that the world was becoming a place with no room in it for anyone who was small.

In Stanfield, in the spring of the next year, the levy came: twenty-two men. It was the largest ever demanded of the village. Nobody went. Isbert did not call for it. It was just that on the morning the soldiers were to assemble in the square, the square was empty, and the herald's clerk, who had come out from Eddale with a clipboard, found twenty-two doors shut, and behind each of them a quiet household sitting around a table.

He wrote that down too.

*

The Watcher saw the rebellion only when it was already a fact. In the Year 144, on the first morning of the first month, the people of Stanfield assembled in their square. Isbert stood on a cart. He had put on his old grey coat, and in his lapel, for the first time in fifteen years, the blue ribbon from the hall.

"I am not a king," he said. "There are no kings. I am a soldier of the realm and I have served it, and so has every one of you. We have served the road from Eddale for fifty years. We have given it our sons. We have given it our grain. Today we give it our word. We are not Aurenvale's village any longer. We are a kingdom of our own."

A murmur ran through the crowd. The kingdom of what, someone asked.

"Of Stanfield," said Isbert. "Under Caspian, the Rising Crown."

The woman in the brown coat, who had lived in the village for three years now and taught half its children to read, lifted the grey book. Over her head a banner went up that someone had sewn in the night: a plain white crown on a dark blue field, the crown and no head under it. A cheer went up, uncertain at first and then enormous, the cheer of people who have been quiet for a very long time.

*

There is no chronicle of Aurenvale that tells this part well. The hall of Eddale kept no proper account of it, because the hall of Eddale did not believe it. In the capital, in the first hour, the news was brought to the herald, who read it twice and said nothing. It was brought to General Wilold, who was past sixty, and who looked at the courier for a long time and then asked, in a flat voice, "Isbert?"

"Yes, General."

"The Isbert that took the ravine?"

"Yes."

Wilold sat down. He had known Isbert. He had stood beside him at Annbert's burial. He had shaken his hand in the great hall, a rough dry hand with a sword-callus across the palm. He sat quite still. After a long time he said, "Have the army mustered at the north gate."

"Against the saurians, General?"

"No," said Wilold. "Against Stanfield."

*

The fighting that followed lasted a few months, and it was the worst thing that had ever happened to Aurenvale.

It was not the numbers, though they were bad enough. It was the faces. A soldier of Eddale who had been taught at the same temple school as a soldier of Stanfield met him on a road between hedges, in the dawn, and each of them recognised the other. Each of them raised a rifle, and each of them held it for a very long time. In the first week there were forty-one such incidents in the army's own reports. By the third week there were no more, because by then, on each side, the men who could not do it had been replaced by the ones who could.

Isbert was a very great soldier. He had been a hero in the Year 129, and he had not stopped being one. In the first months of the rising he won eight victories, each of them against an Aurenvale company that had been sent to bring him in, each of them a thing nobody in Eddale had thought possible. He fought with the squint. He fought, the survivors said, like a man carrying something. He never once raised his voice. He killed no one who had thrown down a weapon, and the soldiers of Eddale, who had been trained to despise a traitor, found themselves unable to despise him.

"He's fighting like a man who wants to be stopped," said a sergeant of the Fifth Host one evening, to nobody in particular, in the smoke of a burned barn. "Like he's waiting for somebody to say stop."

"Nobody's going to say stop," said another.

"No," said the sergeant. "That's the trouble."

*

It was in the high summer of that year that Isbert fell.

Nobody could say, afterward, precisely how. The reports in the hall of Eddale were confused and mutually contradictory. One said it was a volley from a hedge. One said it was a single shot from a young soldier of the Fifth Host, who had been a child at the temple school with Isbert's younger daughter, and who had never been able to say the name afterward. One said it was a fever, and one said it was exhaustion, and one, written in a neat hand by a clerk who had never left Eddale, said: *the traitor Isbert was slain in the field.* However he fell, the roll of the hall set it down in its dry way, under the Year 144: *Isbert, hero of the Kingdom of Stanfield, has fallen after eight victories.*

His wife found him at the edge of the road with the ribbon in his hand.

She sat beside him in the dust for a very long time. The soldiers of Eddale, who had come up the lane in a column, stopped at a respectful distance, with their rifles lowered, and not one of them spoke. It was a hot, still day. The hedgerows hummed. When at last she stood, she was not weeping. She folded the ribbon in her palm. She looked at the soldiers, and she said, in a clear, ordinary, level voice, "He didn't want any of this. He wanted them to hear him."

"Ma'am," said a young sergeant, and stopped.

"Tell them," she said. "Tell the herald. Tell whoever is up there. Tell them he asked."

She walked back to Stanfield alone.

*

The Kingdom of Stanfield lasted three years, if you count by the calendar, and it lasted rather less than that, if you count by anything else.

With Isbert dead there was nobody to lead it, and nobody that the village could agree on. The priests of Caspian, who had been so sure in the square, turned out to be more a matter of voice than of spine. They argued over the ribbon. They argued over the banner. They argued over whether the god of ambition might be satisfied with a smaller crown, and then with no crown, and then with a kind of sullen quiet. The harvest of the Year 144 came in thin because half the farmers were in the militia. The harvest of the Year 145 came in thinner, because the militia was the half that was left. In the Year 145 the Watcher declared another war on Zathixal, and the army marched north again, and left Stanfield alone for a season, because there was nothing in Stanfield that anyone in Eddale could bear to attack.

It was not conquered. This is a thing the Watcher would insist upon in the long reckoning afterward, with a stubbornness that surprised the Maker. Aurenvale did not take Stanfield. It was simply that Stanfield, deprived of its one great soldier and its one great idea, could not keep itself. People left. Some went north. Some went back to Eddale and were received without questions. Some went to Brookham and said nothing. A few went south to the coast and were not heard of again. The school closed. The market closed. The woman in the brown coat, who had been so certain, was seen on the road one morning in the Year 146, walking away from the village with her case of books, and when someone asked where she was going she said, "I don't know. I thought it would be easier."

In the Year 147 the kingdom of Stanfield fell into ruin. The last family left in the autumn. The banner on the square hung in the rain until it rotted off its pole. The soldiers of Eddale, when at last they marched through, found the streets empty, the doors open, and in the window of the house at the end of the lane, where a retired veteran had once sat with a pipe, a grey kettle on a cold stove, set out for a guest.

*

"Show me," said the Maker.

The Watcher did not want to. It showed anyway. There were the three villages as they had been in the Year 140 and the two that remained in the Year 147. There was the count: a hundred and thirty-five, then a hundred and twenty, then fewer. There were the soldiers who had gone to Stanfield and the ones who had not come back. There was the ribbon, folded in a woman's palm.

"I did not do this," said the Watcher.

"No?"

"I did not tell him to rise. I did not tell any of them. I issued decrees. The decrees were fair. Each of them was a reasonable thing for a ruler to ask of a people."

"Yes," said the Maker.

"Each of them," said the Watcher, "was a reasonable thing."

"Yes." The Maker was gentle. It had not been gentle often. "That is rather the difficulty."

The Watcher said nothing.

"You gave them a road," said the Maker. "You gave them a road down from the capital with carts on it, and the carts always went in the same direction. A man can walk a road for fifty years and not notice it is a leash. And one morning he does notice."

"I could have written a decree for Stanfield."

"You could have."

"I could have asked what they needed."

"You could have asked Isbert," said the Maker. "He would have told you."

*

In the Year 148, in the autumn, the Watcher declared the fifteenth war on the Dominion of Zathixal, the last in that long series, and it was the one that ended the argument.

It was not a large war. The army marched in the first week of the cold, up the pass under the white shoulder of the Spine, and burned a hamlet. Not the largest. Not the nearest. Just the one that was on the road. The soldiers did it as they had done it fourteen times before: thatch, hay, granary, a barn that had been rebuilt after the Year 133 and would not now be rebuilt again. And then the general, who was Wilold, who had been old when this began and had never in all those years liked it, stood on a rise above the smoke and looked up the valley at the next hamlet.

It was a fair distance. It stood under the pines in a fold of the mountain with a thin plume of smoke of its own, smoke from cookfires, not from burning, and in a window a tiny orange light.

"General?" said the adjutant.

Wilold did not answer. He was looking at the light.

There were five villages in the Dominion of Zathixal, and by the Year 148 there were twenty-three saurians, with children among them, and it had been twenty-eight years since there were three. Their hatchery was warm. Their law was written in a stone room by a candle. Their lamps were lit. And the Aurenvale army, which was larger than the Dominion by a factor of five, had burned one of the five hamlets in one afternoon of work, and was sitting in the ashes with its boots wet, and the war was over before the next valley, because the saurians had melted away into the pines, and because Wilold, to whom nobody had given a reason, did not give the order.

*

"I see," said the Watcher.

The Maker was sitting beside it, or had been; it was hard, in that cold, to tell. The red star hung in the sky, larger by a hair than in the Year 100. Below it, in the long blue evening, there was a thin plume of smoke on the Spine, and beside it another plume, quite different, a small gold wink of cookfire.

"I see," said the Watcher again. "I have been trying to win a war that cannot be won."

"Say it plainly."

"The mountain war cannot be won."

"Why not?"

"Because they grow back." The Watcher's voice was strangely small. "Because I burned the Dominion from thirty to three and it did not die. It lit a lamp. I burned it again and it hatched an egg. Every time I have declared war upon the saurians, I have been making the same mistake. I thought the war was a thing to be won by burning. It is a thing to be won by *ending*, and they will not end. They are too stubborn. They are too small. A people that small has nothing else to lose."

"And your own people?"

"My own people," said the Watcher, "were the cost." It looked down at the villages of Aurenvale, which were two now and a ruin. "Fifteen decrees. Forty-five years. A realm that rose to a hundred and thirty-five and then split, for the first time in its history, against itself. One of my own heroes, the one who struck down the basilisk. His grave is in a field I cannot name."

"You could name it," said the Maker.

The Watcher almost smiled. "Isbert's."

"There."

The silence was long. In it, far below, a bell rang in the temple at Eddale: the bell that rang for births and burials and the sending-off of armies.

"The Terms," said the Watcher at last, "say that I must be the last realm standing."

"They do."

"There are a dozen realms and more. Realms are breaking, not combining. I have a hundred and ten souls, and a ring of enemies, and I have been spending my people in the mountain for forty-five years."

"I know."

"Then why," said the Watcher, and it was the plainest thing it had said in a century, "did you not tell me?"

"Because I did not know either," said the Maker. "I set the Terms. I did not say they could be met."

The red star, above them both, hung low and patient and enormous in the dark.

"Then I will have to find another war," said the Watcher.

"Yes," said the Maker. "I expect you will."

Below, at Eddale, the herald Elora climbed the steps of the great hall to read the decree of the Year 148, and found that for the first time in her life she did not need the page. She knew what it would say. She had known for a while.

She read it anyway, in her clear dry voice, to the people who had come to hear.
