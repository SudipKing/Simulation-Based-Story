#B BOOK TWO|The Age of Hoods|Years 80 to 99

#C Chapter Five: What Came Up from the Quarry

#E "We dug that hill for eighty years and thought we knew what was in it."
Maren the Younger, Captain of the Guard

The quarry of Aegis-Sol had been cut deeper every year since the first.

It had begun as a scrape on the hillside where Valerius had pointed, and by the eightieth year it was a shaft and a web of galleries running far down into the pale rock, propped with acacia trunks and lit by tallow. The stone of the whole Dominion came out of it. So did a little copper, some tin, and the gold that gave the realm its name, though gold was never plentiful. The miners were proud people. They said the hill was the mother of the kingdom, and they were not far wrong.

In the spring of the eightieth year, the men of the lowest gallery broke through into a space that was not on any plan.

They said afterwards that the air which came out of it was cold and had a smell of old cloth. The two who were nearest put their lamps through the gap and saw a chamber with smooth walls, far too smooth for a natural cave, and something standing upright in the middle of it. They took it for a carved post. Then it turned round.

Only one of those two men came back up the shaft, and for a day he could not speak. When he could, he said: "It has a gold hem. Why does it have a gold hem?"

By then the thing was in the streets.

*

The chronicles name him Vexius, though nobody ever learned how the name was known; it was simply in people's mouths on the second day, as if it had been whispered into the whole city at once. He was shaped like a tall man. He wore a robe of deep purple, stitched and bordered with gold thread, and in his hand was a staff made of long bones bound end to end, with a skull fixed at the top that seemed always to be looking at whoever looked at it. Behind him out of the shaft came the dead. There were not many: seven or eight skeletons, brown with age, wearing scraps of a harness that no smith of the Dominion had made. They walked jerkily and they did not stop.

They killed two miners at the pit-head and a woman drawing water at the well, and then the bell on the wall began to ring and the guard came down from the gate.

The Dominion had never fought anything. It had hunted lions and chased off jackals, and its soldiers were for the most part farmers who kept a spear behind the door. But Theamin had built walls for fifty years against the day his father's country turned on him again, and he had made every grown person drill on them each month until they hated him for it. On the day the quarry opened, they stopped hating him. They formed up in the lane below the granary with shields locked, exactly as they had been made to a thousand times, and they held.

It took a month. The skeletons could be broken, but Vexius raised them again with a motion of the staff, and twice he raised the newly dead to walk with them, which was worse than anything else that happened. He was driven back into the galleries and came out by another adit. He was cornered in the tannery and went through the wall. All that month the city slept in shifts with the lamps lit, and the children were kept in the granary loft, and Thesia, eighty years old and the king's first counsellor, walked the lines each night with a slate, marking who had eaten.

In the end it was Maren's granddaughter, who bore the same name and the same office, who saw what had to be done. The dead got up whenever the staff was raised, and so the staff must not be raised. She put her eight best archers on the roofs around the pit-head and gave them one instruction, which was to shoot at nothing but the hand. On the thirtieth day an arrow went through his wrist, the staff fell, and the guard closed in over the top of the bones and did not leave off until there was nothing left that could be called a shape.

Five people of Aegis-Sol had died. It was the first time the Dominion had buried anyone killed by an enemy, and five seemed to them a terrible number. Before long they looked back on it differently.

Theamin went down to the pit-head when it was over. He looked at the purple cloth, which was already going to dust, and at the gold thread, which was not. He looked for a long while down the shaft.

"Close it," he said.

"The lower galleries, my king?"

"All of it. Bring the props down. Fill the shaft. Put stone over the fill and earth over the stone, and let nobody dig in this hill again while my house rules."

"The stone for the walls..."

"We will find other stone. I would sooner have a low wall than a door in the floor."

So the mother of the kingdom was sealed. The work took the rest of that year. They brought the galleries down gallery by gallery, tamped the shaft with rubble, capped it with the largest blocks they had, and held a rite over the cap, the first rite of that kind the Dominion had ever needed, to ask whatever lay beneath to stay there.

It stayed. Nothing ever came out of that hill again. The next one came by the coast road.

#C Chapter Six: The Red Desolation

#E "Five, and then three. Remember those numbers. You will meet one of them again."
*The First Book of Crowns*

He was seen first on the last day but six of the eightieth year, walking up the beach below Sol-Vael at evening with the tide behind him.

The watchman in the tower took him for a shipwrecked sailor, which shows how far the world had come: by then the fishermen of Sol-Vael had coracles, and the idea that a man might come out of the sea was no longer absurd. This one was dressed in red, a bright, clean red that did not belong on a beach, and he had a hood drawn up that caught the low sun and threw it back. The hood was gold. He carried a staff with a live coal burning at its head.

The watchman called down to ask his name.

The man in red lifted the staff, and the watchtower of Sol-Vael, which had been the first thing the *Swan* ever looked at, went up like a rush-light.

*

His name was Burcien, and that too was simply known, as the other's had been. It was not lost on Thesia, when the first riders reached the capital, that this was the second of them in a single year, and that both had worn gold. She set it down in her book that evening: *The one from below wore a gold hem. The one from the sea wears a gold hood. I do not think they are the same, but I think they were dressed by the same hand.* She kept a list of gold thereafter, and it had a third entry before she died.

Vexius had been a horror of the old dark, slow and close and full of bones. Burcien was a different thing. He burned. He walked into Sol-Vael on that first night and burned the tower, the boat-sheds and the long hall, and when the people ran out onto the plateau he burned the standing grain around them. Then he sat down in the ashes of the market cross and waited for morning, as if he had all the time there was.

He had three years, as it turned out.

No part of the Dominion's history is harder to tell than this, because for three years nothing happened but the same thing. The people of Sol-Vael did not flee. Some did, at first, and the fire found them on the road, and after that the survivors understood that the town was where their walls and wells were and the open plateau was only a wider place to die in. They dug in. They lived in cellars and in the sea-caves under the cliff. They came out at night to draw water and to shoot.

It was the bow that kept them in the fight. A spearman had to come within reach of him and a bowman did not. Theamin sent every archer Aegis-Sol could spare, and then some it could not, and they went into the ruins by the cliff path in the dark and took their turn. They learned that the fire went where the staff pointed and that he could not point it two ways at once. They learned to shoot from three sides. They hit him often. He bled like a man. He simply did not seem to mind it as a man would, and in the morning he would be sitting at the market cross again, with his hood up, and another street would be gone.

At the end of three years, one hundred and nineteen people of Sol-Vael were dead. Dace the watchman, who had seen the white ship come out of the mist as a boy and was twenty-nine when the tower burned, pulled the last of the living out of the sea-caves on the day it ended, and counted them. There were five.

It ended in the autumn of the eighty-third year. He had grown careless, or tired, if such a thing could tire. A company of forty archers out of Aegis-Sol under the younger Maren caught him at dawn in the open ground where the granary had stood, with the low sun in his eyes, and loosed until their quivers were empty. He went down on one knee. For the first time, the coal at the head of the staff guttered. Then a bank of his own smoke rolled between him and the archers, and when it thinned he was gone from the market, and there was a trail of blood on the south road.

They followed it for two days and lost it in the scrub country north of Ostrich-Bane.

*

For two years, nothing.

The Dominion held its breath. Sol-Vael lay black and empty on its cliff, with five people living in a lean-to against the one wall still standing, because they would not leave. The south was searched and searched again. The settlers of Ostrich-Bane, hard people who had cleared the plain of the great birds with their own hands, slept with pails of water by the door and scouts in the thornbush. Nobody found him. Some said he had died of his wounds in a gully and the jackals had had him. Thesia wrote that she would believe it when she saw the hood.

He had gone to ground like a wounded animal, in a dry watercourse a day's walk from Ostrich-Bane, and lain there mending. In the spring of the eighty-sixth year he got up.

He burned what was left of Sol-Vael first, in a single night. He seemed set on finishing what he had begun. Then he came south, and Ostrich-Bane, which was built of timber and thatch and the dry grass of the plain, burned faster than the white stone town on the cliff ever had. The settlers fought him in the streets with bows and boar-spears and buckets, and they wounded him again, and it was not enough. When the smoke came off the plain, three people walked north out of the south country. They were all that was left of two cities.

After that there was only Aegis-Sol.

One hundred and sixty-four people stood on the walls of the capital in the summer of that year, and that was every Human left alive except the three on the road. Theamin was seventy-three. He had his father's bronze brought out of the chest where it had lain for sixty years, and he put it on, and he found that it fitted him.

"I have counted," he told them from the gate. "I have counted us, and I have counted the arrows, and I have counted the paces between the wall and the tree line. There are enough arrows. Nobody is to throw a spear and nobody is to go out. He walks into our range or he does not come in at all."

Burcien walked into their range.

He came up the valley road at noon with the grass burning behind him in a line a mile wide, and the whole wall loosed at once. It is said that the first flight darkened the gate. He kept coming. He set the palisade alight in three places and they put it out with river water, and he set it alight again. A gate-tower fell. But they had three years of Sol-Vael in them now, and they knew that he bled, and all that afternoon one hundred and sixty-four people fed arrows into a single figure in red until he was stuck with shafts like a thornbush, and at last, twenty paces from the gate, he went down on both knees.

The younger Maren went over the wall with an axe.

She was ten paces from him when he raised his head, and she saw his face under the hood. She would never afterwards say what it looked like. He was looking past her, upward, the way a man looks at someone standing behind you, and he spoke one word that nobody caught. The air behind him split open. It was a black seam, narrow, upright, with nothing in it, not even darkness. He fell backwards into it. It closed.

The staff was gone with him. There was nothing left on the road except some blood and a great many arrows.

*

They never knew where he went.

Everything the Dominion did afterwards was shaped by that. They had not killed him, and they knew it. He had been dying and he had left, by a door that could presumably open anywhere, and for all they knew he was lying in another gully mending again. From that day there was a watch kept on the walls of Aegis-Sol against a single man in red, and it was kept without a break for over two hundred years, until there was nobody left to keep it. Children were taught his colours before their letters. A rumour of a red cloak on the south road could empty a market in a minute, a century after everyone who had seen him was dead.

Three had come north from the ruins, and with the hundred and sixty-four on the wall that made a hundred and sixty-seven. In a single decade the Sun-Gilded Dominion had been cut down from the height of its first flowering to one frightened city.

And at the end of the eighty-sixth year, Theamin stood on the western cliff among the ashes of his father's town and looked south. The violet light was still there on the horizon. It had been there all through the burning. Twice in those six years, the survivors told him, the white ship had come and stood off the cliffs, and the tall horned creatures at her rail had looked at the smoke for a while and gone away again without a word.

"They watched us burn," said the younger Maren.

"Yes," said the king. He was quiet for a time. "And they did not land. Remember that too. They could have had this whole coast for the price of walking up the beach."

He did not know that the Player had stayed their hooves. He thought it was mercy, or prudence, and he built two hundred years of policy on that mistake.

#C Chapter Seven: The Luck of the Clover

#E "Luck grows there. Nobody has yet learned who gets to keep it."
A saying of Roux

Quilla had counted the smoke.

She was no longer the young mare who had stood at the *Swan's* rail on the first voyage. She was the queen's Eye now, the head of what the Unister Mysin did not yet call a navy, and she had taken the ship north herself in each year of the burning. She brought back a careful account: one town gone, then two, the fields black, the people fewer every time, and all of it done, so far as she could tell, by a single creature in a red cloak.

Angelica heard her out beneath the great tree.

"One," she said. "One of them did this?"

"It is not one of them, my queen. It is something else. They shoot at it."

"And they still stand on their wall?"

"What is left of them."

The court expected an order then. There were a hundred and twenty unicorns in the Grove by that time, and among them thirteen who did nothing but train for war. These were the first of the Unicorn Defense, and every one of them could have gone through the gate of Aegis-Sol like a bull through a reed fence. The humans were on their knees. The court was full of the love of blood that the Player had put there, and it leaned toward the north like grass in a wind.

Angelica felt it too. She said so afterwards, to Ysolt. She felt it very strongly, and then she felt something else, a reluctance that was not hers, a sense of being held gently by the mane. She put it down to the watcher in the trees. She was not altogether wrong.

"No," she said. "Not yet. A thing that has been burned for six years and is still standing on its wall is not finished, and I will not have it said that the Unister Mysin picked over a carcass. Let them grow back. I want them at their full height when I look at them." She turned her head toward the west. "Quilla. What is beyond the sunset?"

*

What lay beyond the sunset was the third land, the green one that nobody had yet walked on.

The *Swan* found it in the eighty-seventh year, eleven days' sail from Utica: a low coast without cliffs, and behind it clover. There was nothing else. It grew knee-high to a unicorn from the tide-line to the horizon, broken only by scattered copses of ordinary oak and ash. Coming from the lit boughs of the Grove, the crew found those trees strangely small and dull.

There was something odd about the place, and they felt it before the anchor was down. A rope that should have fouled ran clean. A mare who slipped on the gangway landed on her feet. Then a perfectly sound water-cask split its staves for no reason, and the whole crew's supper went into the bilge. It went on like that. Things fell out a little better than they ought, or a little worse, and nobody could tell which way it would go next.

"It is luck," said Corvane the shipwright, turning a four-leafed sprig over with his horn-light. "The ground is full of it. Like ore."

"Can it be mined?" asked Quilla, who was practical.

"I don't know that it can be kept at all," he said.

In the eighty-eighth year they came back with settlers and raised a town on the south-eastern coast, where a headland of good red-brown stone ran out into the sea. They called the town Roux, for the colour of that stone. It was the first city on the Clover Fields. It was also the first step either people had taken off its own continent, and the unicorns had taken it while the humans were still raking through ashes.

*

Four years later the garrison of Roux saw the end of Burcien and had no idea what it was they saw.

It was a still evening in the ninety-second year. A sentry on the landward wall noticed smoke to the north-west, a single dark column going straight up, and under the smell of burning clover there was another smell, of roasting meat. There should have been nobody out there. Three of the garrison went to look: a scarred mare named Hesper who led the watch, and two younger soldiers.

They went quietly, which unicorns can do when they choose, and at the edge of an oak copse Hesper did a thing her people rarely had cause to do. She reared, set her forehooves in a low fork, and with the Reach hauled herself up among the branches like a foal at play, until she could see over the clover.

In a burnt circle a hundred paces across stood a figure on two legs. It wore red. It had a hood that glinted. In its hand was a staff with a coal at the tip, and it was sweeping fire around itself in great arcs, and the fire was going out almost as fast as it was made.

There were things in the clover around it. Hesper thought at first they were boulders. Then one moved, and she saw that they were rabbits.

They were the size of a yearling calf. Their fur was grey and lay flat and had a hard shine on it like hammered metal, and the fire slid off it. Their eyes were red, shot through with blood. There were perhaps thirty of them, and they were not behaving as rabbits behave. They were closing a ring. Two lay dead and smoking in the burnt ground, and that was where the smell of meat had come from. The rest came on in short rushes, one from each side, never where the staff was pointing.

"What is it?" whispered the soldier below her.

"I don't know," said Hesper. "Hold still."

It was over quickly. The figure in red turned too slowly, once. Something hit it behind the knees, and then they were all on it. The staff went up in the air, end over end, and its coal went out before it reached the ground.

The thing that happened next was the part Hesper always doubted when she told it later. The body did not stay. It thinned, like breath on a cold day, and was gone, cloak and hood and staff together. The great rabbits nosed at the place, lost interest and went away into the clover with the dusk shining on their backs.

When it was full dark and nothing had moved for an hour, the three unicorns went down to the burnt ground. Where the figure had fallen there lay a scatter of lumps, about a dozen, each the size of a hoof. They were a kind of metal. They glowed a dull steady red, like embers that had no intention of going out, and they were warm.

"Luck," said the younger soldier, without much confidence. "Good, or bad?"

"His was bad," said Hesper. She looked at the glowing lumps for a time. Then she gathered them in her horn-light, carefully, the way one carries eggs, and took them back to Roux, and in the spring they went to Utica in a lead-lined box, with her report.

*

Above the world, the Player marked a name off a list.

"Burcien," it said. "Dead in a field of clover, of rabbits."

"Of luck," said the Watcher. "He opened a door to anywhere, and of every place there is, he came out there."

"Shall they be told? Yours, I mean. They are still watching the south road for him."

The Watcher looked down at Aegis-Sol, where an old woman with a slate was walking the wall at dusk, counting the sentries.

"No," it said. "It was never theirs to know. Let them keep watching. A people that watches is a people that lasts."

So the Humans never learned it. The man who had killed two-thirds of their nation was dead in another country at the teeth of animals, his ashes feeding the clover, and for two centuries more they taught their children to fear the colour red.

The lumps of ember-metal went into the queen's keeping, and she turned them over for many nights in the light of her horn. She did not know what they were either. She did know what had been said about the creature that left them: it had destroyed two cities alone, and fire had been its nature. A thing like that might one day be useful against something that fire could hurt. She had the lumps sealed in the root-vault under the great tree, and there they stayed for a long time. In the end the smiths of Trecua drew that metal out into thin bands and bound it into the keels and the cannon-mouths of the warships of the Unister Mysin.

Nobody has ever proved it made a difference. But a day came when every tree on a continent got up and walked, and tore down everything unicorns had built there, and reached the shore, and stopped. This book will have more to say about that in its place.

#C Chapter Eight: Ghost Sails

#E "They tie up at our quay like neighbours, and stand on their deck like statues, and leave before the kettle boils."
A harbour-master of Aegis-Sol

Theamin rebuilt, slowly, as he did everything.

He did not try to raise three cities at once. For five years after the fire the whole Dominion lived inside the walls of Aegis-Sol, a hundred and sixty-seven people in a town built for more, and he let them be crowded, and let them have children, and counted. When the count allowed it he sent families back to the south, to the black ground where Ostrich-Bane had stood, because the southern plain was the best grazing in the realm and the Dominion needed meat. He sent others to Sol-Vael, to join the five who had never left. He gave each party archers before he gave them masons.

In the ninety-third year, for the first time, the Humans went down to the sea on purpose.

The quarry was sealed, and the plains had burned twice in a decade, and a king who could count knew that grain alone would not feed his people through another such season. So docks went out into the river-mouth below Aegis-Sol and into the bay at Ostrich-Bane, of driven acacia piles and limestone rubble, and from them went fishing boats: small, tarred, single-masted things, built by people who had studied the *Swan* from a cliff top for twenty-seven years and drawn their own conclusions. They were not handsome. They floated, and they came home full.

Angelica's captains noticed within the season, and they did something that the harbour-folk of the Dominion never managed to explain and never forgot.

The *Swan* came in.

She came up the river-mouth at Aegis-Sol on a grey morning in the autumn of that year, glowing faintly, with her bone-coloured sails half furled. The fishing boats scattered ahead of her like ducklings. The bell rang on the wall. The guard ran down to the quay with their bows strung, and the people who had been taught from the cradle to fear anything they did not understand stood packed along the waterfront, and watched a ship the size of their granary lay herself gently alongside their new dock.

A line snaked out from her deck with nobody holding it and took a turn around a bollard by itself. The crowd drew back a step from that.

Then nothing happened.

A dozen unicorns stood at her rail, twice the height of the tallest guard, with their horns unlit and their eyes on the town. They did not speak. They did not come down. They looked at the wall, and the gate, and the bronze on the old king, who had come down to the quay himself and stood there with his arms folded, looking back. For most of an hour two peoples regarded each other across four yards of harbour water, and the only sound was the hull creaking on the piles.

Then the line unknotted itself and slid back aboard, and the *Swan* went out with the tide.

She did it again the next month at Ostrich-Bane. She did it eleven times in that year alone. The harbour-folk called them ghost dockings, and argued endlessly about what they meant. Some thought it a threat. Others thought the creatures were working up the courage to say something. One old net-mender held to her dying day that they were simply shy.

Theamin understood it better than any of them, because in his own way he was the same kind of creature as the queen he had never met.

"She is counting how many of us come down to the quay," he told Thesia. "And how fast. And what we are carrying when we get there."

"And what should she find?"

"That each time there are more of us, and that we come faster, and that nobody looses an arrow." He watched the pale ship dwindle. "I want her to learn that we do not start things. I think it is the only message we are strong enough to send."

Thesia was ninety-three that year. She had buried a hundred and nineteen people out of one town. "And if she starts something?"

"Then I hope it is after my time," said the king. It was, by a hundred and twenty-seven years.

*

So the century ran out.

In the hundredth year of the world there were two hundred and seventeen Humans in the Sun-Gilded Dominion, and so thoroughly had the fire taught them to trust walls and bows in the hands of every citizen that they kept only two soldiers by trade. In the Unister Mysin there were a hundred and fifty Unicorns, of whom twenty-one belonged to the Unicorn Defense. Both peoples had ships. Their routes crossed every month.

Above the mist, the Player turned to the Watcher.

"It is your year," it said. "One hundred. I have held them as I promised. Do we make them fight?"
