#C Chapter Twenty-Five: The War of Fire and Fur

#E "We built our ships to keep the world apart. We should have built them to keep it together."
(Said to have been written on the wall of Cat Town in the last days)

Queen Gris did not hate the cats. She was careful to say so, in the long, cold council in the Rock where she announced the war. It was not hatred. It was geometry. The cats had joined the Azer Legion. The Legion held the swamp, and the swamp held the south, and the south held the only coast from which a fleet could ever threaten Metallica. A map is a very plain thing. It does not care what any of its people wish.

So war was declared, and the web of the alliance, which had been woven in a time of peace, snapped taut.

Bound by the oath of the Azer Legion, the three peoples came to the cats' defense, and thus Gris found herself at war, not only with a nation of eighty cats, but with Ember of Aethel, and Cater of the Kitten Empire, and Raar of Ali Land. It was the most dangerous coalition the dwarves had faced since the days of Sylvas.

It began badly for the Legion.

In the first month, Queen Raar, the sister of Dhil, who had taken the throne of the crocodiles seven years before and who was the only one of the three leaders who rode at the head of her own army, was struck down in the first skirmish. She died with a jaw full of dwarven armor, and her people, leaderless, reeled.

The elves, though, were not idle. At the shore of Nile, in the same hour, something astonishing happened.

A dwarven warship, Yeth 1, the pride of Yethigrih, came sailing up the coast with banners flying and her decks crowded with soldiers. On her prow stood a young dwarven woman, a soldier named Helen, and in her hands she held a fire-staff. It was not Aron's. It was another, a lesser relic of the old mages. She had been told it would be a wonderful thing to burn the elven docks from afar.

She raised the staff. She spoke the word.

The staff roared. But the wind was wrong, or the staff was worn, or the powers that watched from above simply had a sense of humor. The flame turned. It leapt back across the deck. It caught in the sails and ran along the rigging and in an instant Yeth 1 was a blazing torch, the pitch bubbling in her seams, her soldiers leaping into the water with their beards on fire. The elves on the shore watched, appalled, as the dwarven fleet's greatest ship went down in a pillar of flame.

Helen was not among the dead. She was found at dawn, half-drowned and burned, clinging to a spar in the shallows. An elf of Nile named Carthen waded out and pulled her ashore, and, being an elf, who did not know how to refuse a creature in need, nursed her back to health in a fisherman's hut.

It was the first act of what would become an extraordinary love. But no one knew that then.

*

Gris did not flinch. She mustered every soldier in the empire. From the Rock, four hundred and six. From the Stone, a hundred and seventy-four. From O'Raka, two hundred and sixty-nine. The whole of the dwarven army, nearly nine hundred strong, marched down to the southern coast under banners of black and silver, and they marched against eighty cats, of whom only twenty-one could fight.

"Will the elves help?" Cater asked her council. "Will the crocodiles come?"

No one answered. Nobody knew. Ember, the cold, careful queen, had sent word that Aethel's fleet would not sail until the dwarves had crossed the swamp, and the crocodiles, grieving Raar, were burying their dead.

Cat Town fell with the last days of the year.

It was a slaughter. The cats fought as cats do, in the trees, from the shadows, with claws and silence. They killed three times their number. But there were only twenty-one of them, and the dwarves came on like a tide. The city of Cat Town, the proud new city the cats had built to rival Meow, was taken by the dwarves.

And then Gris did something that all the chroniclers of that age would cite as the proof of her cold heart, and the beginning of her undoing.

She did not march in to take Meow. She did not wait for the cats to surrender. She raised her hand, and the dwarves, who had come prepared, flung burning pitch into the trees of the jungle.

The jungle burned.

It burned from the coast to the mountains, a sea of orange flame that turned the night into an afternoon. The cats, in their tree-built city, had nowhere to run. Cater, queen of the Kitten Empire, was caught in the fire as it swept through the royal grove, and she died with her fur burning and her eyes on the stars. The Kitten Empire, born in the Year 618, died in the Year 666, forty-eight years old.

In Nile, a single elf named Tohil, the oldest and the most hated, laid down his head in his own small house and died peacefully, as a man dies who has finally outrun his own conscience. The elves did not mourn him. The chroniclers recorded his death in a single line, and the line was not kind.

*

But the fire was not done with the dwarves.

The burning jungle, which they had lit as a weapon, was a creature with its own will. The wind turned, as it had turned for Helen, and the flames swept back through the dwarven camp. In a single night, the greatest army that Metallica had raised in a hundred years was caught between the burning trees and the sea. Four hundred dwarves died in the flames of their own making. They burned where they stood, in their armor, with their hammers in their hands, and the ash of them mingled with the ash of the cats.

Gris stood on the shore of the burning jungle and watched four hundred of her soldiers die, and she did not weep.

She turned to her generals and said, "We will not stop."

The remaining dwarves of Anaconda, thirty soldiers who had been left behind to guard the marsh, marched on Ali City. They found it undefended. The crocodiles, mourning Raar, had put every warrior on the field. Only the old, the young, and the sick remained in the mud-brick halls. After killing a few crocodiles, the dwarves took the city. The ruler of Ali Land, a crocodile named Afar, who had taken the throne after Raar's death, saw the dwarven soldiers at the gate, and made the calculation of a survivor.

Afar fled the city. Not to fight, not to rally the people, but to run. The crocodile king abandoned Ali Land and left the city to surrender, and the dwarves walked into it without a blow. In the chronicles of the crocodiles, Afar would be remembered, not kindly, as the Coward King.

Aron, the old fire-staff hero, the sleeper in the courtyard, who had not stirred in sixty years, woke at last. He was very old. He staggered to his feet, took up his staff, and stumbled out of Ali City toward the elven shore, whether in a last fit of heroism or in a mad delusion no one ever knew. He tried to attack the elves with his staff, and the elves killed him. His body was left on the beach. His staff was found in the sand by a soldier named Onata.

The war was far from over. With Ali City in their hands, the dwarves of Anaconda marched on Nile.

Twenty-four elves stood on the walls. Four cats, three of them mere kittens, huddled in the elven halls, the last of their people. Twenty-two crocodiles waited in the marsh. And on the shore of the river, with the banners of Metallica flapping in the wet wind, the dwarves took their first measure of the elven defenses and found them strong.

#C Chapter Twenty-Six: The Siege of Nile

#E In the Year 669, the dwarves did not come. It was the most frightening thing they could have done.

The dwarven general, studying the walls of Nile and the wet ground and the sour smell of the elven magic, made a decision that was the better part of valor. He did not attack. The elves held a strong defense, and the dwarves, who had come across a swamp and a burning jungle and left four hundred of their own in the ash, decided to wait.

It was in that waiting that a soldier named Onata, who had taken Aron's staff from the beach, grew impatient. He crept to the shore at night and tried to burn the elven dock. He was spotted by the sentries. He was seized. And he was brought before the queen of Aethel in her wooden hall.

Ember stood up, tall and pale, and took the staff from his hands. It was heavy, and warm, and in its heart a flicker of orange light stirred as though it recognized her. She held it a moment, and then she raised it, and Onata, who had a heartbeat to understand what was happening, died.

She looked at the staff in her hands. There was a name in the wood, an old inscription in letters older than any language of the swamp. It said: *Aron's Fire.*

"It is aptly named," said Ember.

The battle made no progress. For five years the dwarves and the elves faced each other across a strip of mud, and neither could move. It was a dreary, sleepless time, the swamp heavy with fog, the water lapping at the walls of Nile.

In the Year 674, Ember struck.

She used the only weapons she had, the oldest in her people's inheritance: the voice of the green. The elves of Nile, standing at the edge of the swamp, sang a song that no elf had sung since the days of Sylvas. It rang through the roots. It woke the seeds. And in Anaconda, three hundred miles of marsh away, where the dwarven garrison was growing its crops of barley and bog-wheat, the plants listened.

They woke. They twisted. They put out thorns, and wrapped themselves around the throats of the farmers who tended them. Anaconda, which had had almost ninety dwarves, fell to thirty-five in a single night. It was the revenge of the Enchanted Wood on the people who had burned it.

And then Ember took Aron's Fire in her hands, climbed to the highest tower in Nile, and aimed it at the sea.

The flame that left the staff was not a bolt. It was an arc, a long curving meteor of fire that crossed the strait in a single breath, a sky-borne comet, and fell on the docks of Yethigrih. The shipwrights of the old dwarven city, the proud builders of the fleet, saw the fire in the sky and had time to look up before it fell. The docks burned. The fleet burned. Ember, tireless, turned and fired again, and the docks of O'Raka burned too. The navy of Metallica was dust.

The elves cheered on the walls of Nile. For the first time in a hundred years, it seemed that the dwarves might be beaten.

But Ember did not know what was happening in the south.

#C Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Fall of Aethel

#E "The elves had been right about everything except the one thing that mattered."

In Ali City, which the dwarves had taken so easily, a very different kind of preparation was under way. In the royal dockyard, built of mud-brick and stolen timber, two ships were rising from the swamp. The dwarves of Anaconda, with the help of captive crocodile carpenters, had built them in secret, over the course of a year, and the elves, whose eyes were fixed on the north, had never noticed.

The ships were named Biten and Bita.

They were not like Fry or Yeth. They were enormous, flat-bottomed, armored in black iron, and each held seventy-five dwarves in the hold. A combined force of a hundred and fifty. They were the first dwarven warships ever to be built on the swamp, and the first dwarven soldiers from the mainland ever to set foot in the marsh. For the first time in two hundred years, soldiers of the Rock and the Stone and O'Raka sailed across the water, and the elves did not know.

In the Year 675, the murky land meant nothing.

The dwarves marched along the coast, ignoring the swamp, ignoring the leeches, ignoring the mud, and fell on the elven fleet at the docks. Fry 3, the last ship of Aethel, burned in the shallows. And the army of the elves, such as it was, fifteen soldiers behind the walls of Nile, stared out at the hundred and fifty approaching dwarves and understood what Ember had understood a thousand times in her long and careful life: that this was the end.

They gave up. Fifteen elves laid down their bows at the gates of their own city, and the gates were opened. Nile fell to Gris.

Ember did not give up.

She stood on the steps of her hall with Aron's Fire in her hands for a long, still moment, as the dwarves came up the stairs, and she looked at them. Perhaps she thought of Embera, her mother, who had founded the nation on the bank of the river. Perhaps she thought of Sylvas, the long-dead king, who had stood in an empty hall tasting ash. Perhaps she thought of nothing at all.

Then she turned and ran.

She fled across the marsh, through the reeds, along the sodden tracks of crocodile hunters, away from the dying city, with a hundred soldiers on her heels. For three days she ran. On the fourth day, in a stand of drowned trees, she was run to ground by a single soldier, a young woman with a burned face and a fire-staff of her own.

It was Helen.

The two women looked at each other, the elven queen and the dwarven soldier, in the grey light of the swamp morning. The elf held the most powerful weapon in the world. The dwarf held a lesser one. And yet, in the moment before the end, Ember did not lift her staff. She seemed, for a moment, almost relieved.

"Do it," she said.

Helen did.

So Queen Ember of Aethel, daughter of Embera, last ruler of the elven nation that began with Sylvas and ended in the swamp, fell in the Year 675. Aethel was no more. The war was over.

#C Chapter Twenty-Eight: The Pax Metallica

#E In the Year 676, the world was at peace, and every living thing in it was waiting.

There were fireworks over the Rock that night. They were red and gold and blue, and they burst above the black granite towers like the flowers of some celestial garden. In the streets, the dwarves of the mainland danced. They drank. They sang the old marching songs, and the sound went up like thunder into the night sky.

Gris had done what Grih had done. She had united the world.

Sixteen hundred dwarves reigned from the Rock to the Stone to O'Raka to Yethigrih, from the northern ice to the southern swamp. Beneath them, in the marsh and the shadows, thirty-eight crocodiles, fifteen elves, and three cats clung to the edges of the world. They were allowed to live. They were not allowed to rule.

In the half-ruined hall of Nile, a woman named Helen was celebrated as a hero. She had killed the queen of the elves. She had burned a ship with her own hands. She wore a medal of black iron on her chest and was given a house with a view of the river. She would not use it. She went back, instead, to the fisherman's hut where an elf named Carthen had once pulled her from the sea.

The Watchers, seeing the dwarves' empire at its zenith, grew thoughtful.

"Do we let the world end?" asked the first Watcher at last. "Or shall we spice it up again?"

"It is your call," said the second.

"Then pick one. This is the last of the spice. After this, the plot plays out, and the world is done. So choose wisely."

They looked down upon the sixteen hundred dwarves, and the cities of the Rock and the Stone, and the forgotten jungle with its scars. They thought of the cats in the burning trees. They thought of the four hundred soldiers who had died in the fire of their own making, and of what happens to the dead who are not buried.

And then, from the long habit of the story, they knew what to do.

#B BOOK SEVEN|The Age of Ash|Years 683 to 725

#C Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Walkers in the Ash

#E "Fire does not forget. It only waits for something to burn."

In the Year 683, in the half-drowned halls of Ali City, the Coward King Afar died of a fever, and no one wept. The crocodiles of the marsh buried their ruler in the mud and went on with their small, stunted lives.

In the Year 686, a ghost died.

It was Uncater, the ghost of Queen Cater, who had been the last great shadow of the Kitten Empire. She had haunted the burned jungle for twenty years, a pale, glimmering shape in the blackened stumps, a cat's thin cry on the wind. She had watched the world go on without her. She had waited. And in the Year 686, with a long and weary sigh, she unmade herself.

Her death was not an ending. It was a key.

The ground of the burned jungle trembled. The ash, which had lain undisturbed for twenty years, rose in long, smoking threads. And out of the black earth of Cat Town came four hundred shapes, gaunt, armored, and silent, with the hollow eyes of the dead and the blackened axes of the war that killed them.

They were the dwarves who had burned.

The four hundred soldiers who had died in the fire of Gris's own kindling, in the Year 666, in the jungle. They had lain in the ash of their own pyre for twenty years. Now they were up again, not as ghosts, nor as zombies, but as something stranger and worse. They had been killed by fire, and fire had marked them. They were fireproof. They walked in the flames and were not burned. They could not be struck by the fire-staves of the old mages. They could not be driven out by the burning of the grass.

And wherever they walked, the land died.

Every step they took left a footprint of scorched, glistening, acid-green earth. The jungle, which had grown back in the years since the fire, withered at their approach. The soil turned sour. The streams ran yellow. In their wake there was nothing but a wasteland, and the wasteland was the same land that had given birth to the orcs, so many centuries before.

They were called the Ash-Walkers, and there were four hundred of them, and they were marching on the Rock.

Gris, queen of Metallica, who had won the world in the Year 676 and had held it for ten years, heard the news in her hall and went white. She had two thousand and two hundred dwarves. She had armies, and weapons, and the dim memory of a hundred wars. But she had no way to burn an enemy that could not be burned.

"Can the army stop them in time?" her generals asked.

"No," said Gris. "But we will have to try."

#C Chapter Thirty: Nedhel at the Pass

#E In the Year 687, the queen died, and a soldier took her crown.

After forty-five years on the throne of the dwarves, Gris died in her bed in the Rock, as her father had. She was a long-lived queen, with the cold eyes of a general and the unbending will of a ruler who knew what the world cost. She had burned a jungle. She had broken the elven queen. She had given orders that sent four hundred of her own to their deaths. In her last hour, it was said, she asked only that the window be opened, so that she might hear the wind.

The wind, that night, smelled of ash.

Her successor was a warrior named Nedhel. He was not of the royal blood. He was a general, a soldier, a hard, square, silent dwarf with a scar through one eyebrow and a habit of standing very still when others panicked. He had been a part of the fleet that sailed to Nile in the Year 675, one of the hundred and fifty on Biten and Bita, and he had seen, with his own eyes, what a clever enemy and a poor position could do to an army. He was perhaps the one person in all of Metallica who knew that an army is only as good as the ground it stands on.

He took the throne in the first week of the Year 687. He studied the maps for three nights. And on the fourth morning he gave an order that surprised the entire court.

"We will not meet them in the open," said King Nedhel.

The jungle was gone. Meow, the old cat capital, and Cat Town, rebuilt in the years since, were destroyed, eaten by the acid. The trees of the southern jungle were dead. The toxic wasteland spread north each day, a creeping wave of poisoned earth, and if the Ash-Walkers reached the farms of the Stone and the Rock, Metallica would starve whatever happened in the battle.

But there was a place, in the mountains, where the world narrowed. A single pass, between two walls of black granite, so tight that a man with outstretched arms could touch both sides. A bottleneck. The ancient passage that the dwarves of the old days had sealed, and that had cracked open in the Year 253 when the stars fell.

Nedhel moved the whole of Metallica's archers into the cliffs.

*

In the Year 690, the Ash-Walkers came to the pass.

They came as they always did, in silence, trailing ruin. Their blackened axes were notched, their armor pitted and rusted. They did not have to fear fire. They did not have to fear death. They could not be burned out of the pass, nor could they be starved. They marched straight into the gap between the cliffs, forced by the walls into single file, and the dwarves above, hidden among the rocks, drew their bows.

The first volley fell like a black rain. The arrows were heavy, tipped with the hammered iron of the Stone. They did not pierce the Ash-Walkers' armor, but they shattered their bones. A dwarven arrow does not kill a skeleton. It breaks it. The marching line staggered, tangled, fell apart. And the next volley fell, and the next.

It was not a battle. It was a harvest.

Row after row the skeletons advanced into the pass, in the narrow darkness, forced into rows by the rock, and the dwarven archers picked them off one by one. The Ash-Walkers lost their numbers in the dark. The dwarves lost fifty, in all, to the acid and the occasional axe that came too close. Four hundred became two hundred. Two hundred became a hundred and fifty.

"Doesn't feel like much of a threat," said a young archer, to no one in particular.

"No," said Nedhel, from the cliff-top. "It never does."

#C Chapter Thirty-One: The Madness and the Tremor

#E The world had one more twist in it. The world always did.

In the Year 696, the dwarves of The Rock went mad.

No one has ever explained it. Perhaps it was the strain of the war. Perhaps it was the poison of the ash on the wind, or the memory of the Year 611, when dwarf had slaughtered dwarf for no reason anyone could name. Perhaps it was merely the weight of seven hundred years of history, settling on a people who were not made to carry it. In the middle of a cold spring night, in the streets of the capital, a quarrel broke out. A blade was drawn. And then another.

By morning the streets of the Rock were slick with blood. Brothers had killed brothers. Husbands had killed wives. Fathers had struck at sons with the same hammers that had broken the skeletons in the pass. It lasted three days. When it was over, the Rock was quiet, and the dwarves who survived could not meet each other's eyes.

In the Year 697, the earth shook.

It was a dreadful quake, centered under the Stone, and it ran through the whole mountain like a shudder through a great animal. Houses split. Walls fell. The granite roads that the dwarves had laid for centuries cracked like dry mud. The empire's granaries, its smithies, its storehouses, were flattened. The logistics of a nation went to pieces in an hour.

The empire reeled. Between the madness and the earthquake, the dwarves of Metallica fell, in the Year 699, from two thousand and two hundred to eighteen hundred. Four hundred souls had died by their own hands, or by the hand of the ground itself, without the Ash-Walkers firing a single shot.

And yet the pass held.

That was the great and terrible irony of the war. The Ash-Walkers had not broken Metallica. Metallica had broken itself. But the narrow road between the cliffs had not been touched. Behind it, the archers still stood on their cliffs, and every day, they loosed their arrows into the dark.

By the Year 699, only forty Ash-Walkers remained. Three hundred and sixty of them had been pulverized into dust, a pathetic remnant shuffling blind and slow into a wall of arrows that would not stop.

"It is almost done," said King Nedhel. "We only need to be patient."

And it was a long patience. It lasted fourteen years.

#C Chapter Thirty-Two: The Last Skeleton

#E "When you have been dead for so long, you can't be killed twice. But you can be finished."

The years after the great madness were quiet. Quieter than anyone had dared hope.

In the Year 700, the warship Fry 2, the old elven vessel that had survived two centuries of storms and sieges, was retired. Her timbers were salvaged, her figurehead carved into a small statue that stood, for many years, on the docks of Nile. In the Year 710, a surge of madness, like a ghost of the old plague, swept through the streets of Anaconda. It was brief, a single night of cold sweats and shaking hands. And this time, the dwarves were ready. They had learned. The madness was suppressed before a single life was lost.

In the Year 713, the last of the Ash-Walkers was killed.

It was the final skeleton of the original four hundred, a thing of bone and rust, still dragging its notched axe along the stones of the narrow pass. It had not known fear. It had not known anger. It had not even known why it was walking. It had only known that there was a place it should go, and a thing it should do when it got there.

An archer on the cliff, a young woman who had not been born when the war began, drew her bow and loosed a single arrow. It struck the skeleton in the center of the chest. For a moment the creature stood, swaying, and then it came apart, bone by bone, falling into a small pile of white dust on the black stone.

The pass was silent.

No one cheered. They only stood there, on the cliffs, in the grey morning, and looked at the little pile of dust. After twenty-seven years, the Ash-Walkers were gone. The dwarves, who had suffered every kind of horror that the world had to offer, were finally free.

They had recovered. Metallica stood at two thousand again. The farms of the Rock and the Stone were green. The scars of the acid wasteland were slowly healing, the jungle creeping back over the dead earth as it had after the Age of Despair, as it always would.

In the Year 714, the great ship Biten, the proud iron flagship that had carried the dwarves to the swamp and the fall of Aethel, sprang a leak in a calm sea and sank. It went down in a great slow gyre of foam and rust. No one was killed. It was the last ship of the great war.

#C Chapter Thirty-Three: Gri Ali

#E "It is a very small price to pay, for being allowed to live."
(Attributed to the crocodile-queen of Ali Land, on the renaming of her city)

The Year 725 dawned over a different world.

It was a world with no cats. They had gone in the fire of Cat Town, in the long reign of the Ash-Walkers, in the quiet, decisive hand of Gris and the poison of the ash. The last of them had died, one by one, in the years after the great war, their whispering voices fading from the jungle. The Kitten Empire, born in the Year 618, was a story that dwarven grandmothers told their grandchildren on winter nights.

It was a world with no humans. They had gone long before, in the Year 472, with Aria and her one-woman empire of Ense, and in all the long years since nobody had seen so much as a human footprint.

It was a world with no orcs, and no dragons, and no necromancers. They, too, were gone.

But it was a world with crocodiles. In the Year 725, ninety-three of them lived in the marshes of the south. They had boomed, in the years since the great war ended, bred like rabbits in the warm mud. They had built a new city on the ruins of the old one. They had learned to farm, a little, and to fish, a great deal. And they had learned to be pragmatic.

When the dwarves of Metallica, in the Year 725, proposed to rename their capital as a gesture of goodwill, the crocodiles accepted. Ali City, the city of King Ali, who had been killed by the pumpkins, was renamed Gri Ali. It was a name that blended the two peoples, the name of the first dwarf king of the old line, Gri, who would later be called Grih, and the name of the first crocodile king.

It was a small thing. A name. But the crocodiles understood it perfectly. It was a very easy price to pay for the right to live, to build, and to breed in the shadow of the greatest empire in the world.

And it was a world with elves.

There were thirty-three of them, in the Year 725. Thirty-three, up from the fifteen who had stood in the ruins of Nile in the days of Gris, and the thirty-three were more than any elven people had been in two hundred and fifty years. They lived in Nile, on the river, in houses of wood and clay. They fished. They sang. They remembered, quietly and without bitterness, the old green songs.

And the ruler of Nile, in that year, was not a dwarf.

His name was Carthen. He was an elf, tall and grey-eyed, and thoughtful, and a good fisherman. He was the husband of a woman named Helen. Helen, the dwarven soldier who had burned the great warship Yeth 1, who had killed the queen Ember with her own hand and been given a medal and a house for it, had never used the house. She had gone back to a fisherman's hut. She had stayed. She had borne her husband a daughter, small and dark-haired, with a dwarf's thick hands and an elf's long gaze.

The dwarves of the Rock thought it was strange. The elves of Nile thought it was stranger. But the new King of the Dwarves, Nedhel, who had faced the Ash-Walkers in the pass and learned that no victory is ever final, shrugged and let it be.

It was a fragile thing, that peace. It was a pragmatic peace, an arrangement of survivors, as unlikely and as stubborn as a weed in a crack in the stone. The dwarves held the mainland. The crocodiles held the marsh. The elves held the river. Nobody loved anybody. Everybody lived.

#C Epilogue: The Quill Set Down

#E And above the world the two Watchers looked at what they had made, and, for the first time, neither of them spoke.

It had been seven hundred and twenty-five years.

They had begun with a mountain, and a wood, and a jungle. They had let the sun rise on a king named Sylvas and a king named Baron. They had broken the wall, and watched the elves win, and watched the elves lose. They had watched dwarves climb out of the stone, and orcs rise from the poisoned earth. They had watched pumpkins rise from soil that remembered, and snow fall for forty years, and a dragon wake in the north. They had watched a man named Tohil kill the only king he ever swore to protect, and a queen named Ember run through a swamp. They had watched a farmer-king rule for three hundred and forty-eight years. They had watched a cat become a ghost, and a ghost become an army, and the army fall, one skeleton at a time, in a narrow pass.

They had watched the humans go, and the cats go, and the orcs go. They had watched the world shrink to a river, a marsh, and a mountain.

And they had watched, in the end, not the death of a people but the quiet, difficult, improbable birth of a bargain.

"Is it over?" asked the first Watcher.

"For now," said the second. "This chapter is closed."

"And the next?"

The second Watcher looked down at the small world, glowing in the dark like a coin dropped in a deep well. A dwarven child, in the Rock, was asking her mother why the sky was red. A crocodile, in Gri Ali, was teaching its hatchlings to swim. An elven woman, in Nile, was singing to a baby who had a dwarf's thick hands and an elf's long gaze.

"It will write itself," said the second Watcher. "It always does."

They put down the quill.

The year was seven hundred and twenty-five, and the sun was rising over the world.

It was the same sun that had risen on the first day. It lit the green of the Enchanted Wood that was no longer there, and the blue of the river that had split the world, and the black granite of the Rock, and the grey mud of the swamp where the crocodiles slept. It lit the bones of kings and the ashes of cities, and the little hut by the river where an elf and a dwarf woke up together and listened to the wind.

It lit everything.

It had always lit everything.

And the story, which had begun with a mountain, and a wood, and a jungle, ended, as all true stories end, not with a victory, but with morning.
